### 3.1 EV Charging and Flexibility  (1 paragraphs)

[P03] section 3.1, pos 1/1
In this chapter of the literature review, the focus is on the consequences of EV integration on the grid, charging strategies, and V2G technologies. This is done to find out how the literature extracts flexibility out of EVs, and how flexible charging and inflexible charging can be a problem for the grid.

### 3.1.1 Results of increasing EV diffusion  (3 paragraphs)

[P04] section 3.1.1, pos 1/3
With a goal of all new consumer cars sold in Norway being zero emission from 2025 [46], either hydrogen or electric, the diffusion of EVs and the impact on the energy system has to be investigated. Henriksen et al. [2] studied the impact of increasing numbers of EVs in Norway. They find that the diffusion of EVs in Norway has significantly strained local electricity grids, and the increase in total electricity use because of EVs is calculated to be 0.42% of the total electricity use in Norway as of 2020. With electric energy use for transportation in Norway forecasted to be at 20 TWh by 2030 [3], transportation is going to make a substantial impact on the grid.

[P05] section 3.1.1, pos 2/3
A study done by P. Wangsness and A. Halse [47] investigated the impact of EV density on local grid costs in Norway based on a historical dataset spanning from 2008 to 2017. It is stated that the diffusion of EVs within a DSOs operational area is often associated with higher local grid costs. It is also found that an increase in EV diffusion in areas where the DSO has a higher density of customers results in a lower effect on the local grid cost.

[P06] section 3.1.1, pos 3/3
In an article written by Affonso and Kezunovic [48] it was found that the installation of EV chargers in a commercial building increased the daily energy consumption by 23%. This increase in electricity use creates issues in neighborhoods, overwhelming the old grids. Stringent regulations and safety concerns have led to a shift from simply plugging EVs into a regular socket, to installing dedicated charging stations, contributing to a substantial market for various EV chargers in Norway [2]. This shift aggravates the strain on local power capacity, leading to congestion being more of a concern. This necessitates costly upgrades to low-voltage networks as DSOs struggle with increasing demand for EV charging infrastructure. However, newer and faster charging stations provide not only a significant increase in safety but also enable EV charging which considers peak loads which in turn can help with grid congestion.

### 3.1.2 Grid problems due to EV integration  (2 paragraphs)

[P07] section 3.1.2, pos 1/2
The intense integration of EVs has a huge energy requirement and has serious effects on the energy sector [49], such as overloading, voltage instability, voltage drops, voltage unbalance, energy losses, harmonics, frequency variation, and consequently reductions in supply quality [50].

[P08] section 3.1.2, pos 2/2
A study from Canada considered the integration of EVs into the distribution grid, where the impacts of uncoordinated charging were evaluated on a real test feeder [51]. It was found that for normal and fast charging scenarios, the uncoordinated charging led to unacceptable voltage drops in remote buses, and the feeder line loading constraint was violated at 15% and 25% EV penetration for fast charging and normal charging respectively. Another case study was done on the power system on the Danish island of Bornholm [52], which analyzed the impacts of EV penetration on the grid. For the uncontrolled study, the feasible integration of EVs was 10%, while the controlled charging could handle the integration more effectively. Voltage drops are found to be the limiting factor, and the peak demand in the network is increased by 31% for a 50% EV integration controlled charging scenario. From the literature, it is evident that increased EV penetration with uncontrolled charging significantly increases peak demand and strains the grid. Having more flexible EV charging is one way to combat this.

### 3.1.3 Flexibility from EV charging  (4 paragraphs)

[P09] section 3.1.3, pos 1/4
Knezovic et al. [53] state in their research that DSOs have historically dealt with grid-security challenges by making investments in grid reinforcement. However, an alternative solution, enabled by the rollout of smart meters and high penetration of flexible loads, would be the increased use of flexibility services. Flexible loads, with EVs at their forefront, can modulate their consumption or even inject power back into the grid, depending on current grid conditions.

[P10] section 3.1.3, pos 2/4
As the battery system in EVs can be modeled as a flexible load, looking into BESS literature can also be relevant. In an article written by Y. Hu et al in 2022, they investigate the potential utilization of BESS in some of the major European electricity markets [54]. It was found that BESS can be a feasible solution in some parts of Europe, but has more limited potential in Norway. They state that this is due to the Norwegian high penetration level of hydropower. However, the study shows that the potentially profitable utilization increases every year in Norway. The need for power increases and hydropower in Norway is already well developed, making BESS a potential part of the solution for the future grid in Norway.

[P11] section 3.1.3, pos 3/4
Winschermann et al. studied the significance of historical data in enhancing EV charging strategies at office buildings [38]. Given the challenges posed by the high energy demands of EVs on the electrical grid, smart charging was proposed as a potential solution. In the study, historical data from an office parking lot, specifically focusing on arrival times, energy requirements, and dwell times, was used to determine how past data mirrors real-time operations. An algorithm was used to construct a power charging profile for EVs based on user-specific parameters. Using a greedy charging approach in office settings can result in high power peaks due to synchronized arrivals. Here, greedy charging entails charging without an energy management system, which charges at the maximum available power from the plug-in. Using historical data for efficient planning helped reduce the peaks by 50%. Complete historical data which concerned arrival and departure times and energy requirements, was found to be essential for efficient planning. In an article written by Affonso and Kezunovic [48], smart EV charging in an EVPL for a commercial building is used to reduce the cost of operation for the building owner while also reducing the strain on the local grid transformer. Without the smart EV charger, the transformer was overloaded in the summer season, which led to accelerated aging of the transformer.

[P12] section 3.1.3, pos 4/4
Understanding the motivation for the end-users to participate in smart charging and the flexible use of their EVs is important. The study by I. Henriksen et al. [2] presents the key motivations for the adoption of smart chargers for EVs. Participants emphasized the safety and faster charging speed of smart chargers compared to regular sockets. Many of the participants were interested in smart home technology, which in turn made smart charging very interesting. Additionally, the recognition of practical and economic benefits, such as improved load management and potential incentives from the pilot project, played a significant role in the decision-making process. The flexibility of smart charging, allowing users to delay charging during periods of lower electricity demand, was also seen as advantageous. In conclusion, the end-users adoption of smart chargers was influenced by a combination of safety considerations, technological interest, practical and economic advantages, and the flexibility offered in the charging process [2].

### 3.1.4 Vehicle to grid  (3 paragraphs)

[P13] section 3.1.4, pos 1/3
When optimizing the flexible use of an EVPL, V2G is a technology that could help provide the wanted flexibility. There are three main domains for V2G studies, namely the transmission grid, distribution network, and consumer [41]. This literature study focuses on the consumer domain.

[P14] section 3.1.4, pos 2/3
A study conducted by Yee Ting Chai et al. [41] introduces a two-stage optimization method for EVs in a V2G program at an office building. For the V2G program to work, the scheme must address both the needs of EVs and building owners. For building owners, EVs need to be reliably present to supply power. EV owners, on the other hand, should benefit financially without V2G affecting their travel plans. To achieve this, the EV owners are allowed to reach their desired State of Charge (SOC) at any time by raising the energy anxiety factor. The proposed method uses day-ahead optimization based on expected loads and EV patterns, aiming to cut electricity costs. Then, in the second stage, a real-time optimization adjusts to actual EV behaviors. The simulations show promising results, although it is shown to be difficult to consider the monthly peak demand when optimizing for shorter intervals.

[P15] section 3.1.4, pos 3/3
Jingli Guo et al. [55] explores the impact of V2G services on EV battery degradation. The study introduces EVs as a solution to environmental issues and highlights the potential of V2G to support electricity grids. They also express concerns about the possible acceleration of battery degradation due to frequent charging and discharging cycles associated with V2G. Findings from different studies vary on whether V2G has acceptable or detrimental impacts on battery degradation. Factors affecting battery degradation include battery chemistry, charging/discharging regimes, types of V2G services, and service frequency [55]. Appropriate V2G management, such as optimizing cycling during idle states, could minimize battery degradation. It is concluded by emphasizing the need for accurate battery degradation models and improvements in battery management for V2G deployment. Zhoa [56] states that aggregators should compensate the EV owners for battery degradation when providing V2G services to the power grid.

### 3.2 Demand Response  (1 paragraphs)

[P16] section 3.2, pos 1/1
As mentioned in Section 2.3.1, DR is a method where the objective is to manage and modify electricity consumption in response to external signals, such as changes in electricity prices or grid reliability concerns [23]. The goal is to research how the two of the most common classifications of DRPs, PBDRP and IBDRP, are implemented and used.

### 3.2.1 Demand charge  (3 paragraphs)

[P17] section 3.2.1, pos 1/3
The demand charge is categorized as PBDR and is a part of the electricity bill for end-users. It is based on the highest level or levels of electricity demand during a specific period, typically measured in kilowatts (kW) [29]. In this section, the implementation of the DCC is investigated together with the impact of considering the long-term impact of short-term decisions in optimization models.

[P18] section 3.2.1, pos 2/3
In the study done by Y.He et al. [57], the DCC in a charging station for an electric bus system was analyzed. DCC is defined as a part of the total system cost, and the text illustrates how both the total system cost and demand charge increase with an increase in the demand charge rate. When including an energy storage system, the total cost for the system was reduced by 9.2%. In the study by K. Thorvalsen et al. [58], the implications of the new capacity-based grid tariffs in Norway, which include a demand charge, are investigated. Stochastic Dynamic Programming (SDP) is implemented to represent the long-term impacts of the demand charge. The method was applied to a real Norwegian household, to strategically determine the cost-optimal peak power and the expected total electricity cost. Two scenarios were investigated: one where the future cost from the grid tariff, i.e. the demand charge, is unknown and not considered, and another with perfect information about stochastic inputs. The results revealed a 36.1% increase in cost when not considering the demand charge.

[P19] section 3.2.1, pos 3/3
Operating an energy system with a monthly demand charge can pose challenges when trying to identify the optimal peak-import level to aim for. A metaheuristic approach was conducted in [59] to balance the short-term cost of operation for a household with the long-term impacts of the demand charge, using a user-defined weighting constant. A high value for the weighting constant indicated that the energy management system weighted the real-time price less. A low weighting constant would indicate that the energy management system pays more attention to the real-time price, resulting in a high DCC. When the the weighting constant was optimal, the total cost was reduced by 64,76 % compared to when the system did not have a management system.

### 3.2.2 Use of flexibility in IBDRPs  (4 paragraphs)

[P20] section 3.2.2, pos 1/4
In this section the goal is to showcase how earlier studies used IBDRPs to provide the DSOs with the needed flexibility. The studies have also looked into how and what the incentives should be for participation. Latly, this section presents a study that investigates the consequences of different notice times prior to an IBDR-signal.

[P21] section 3.2.2, pos 2/4
Rana et al. studied how a residential community could provide ancillary services like voltage support and loss minimization to a DSO [60], assuming that the ancillary service provider has information on the status of the grid. It was found that when the objective was to minimize the losses, the active losses were reduced by 4.2%. In the article written by Berg et al. [61], the primary objective was to study how an energy community and a DSO can coordinate to improve the voltage profile in the grid. A minimum compensation of 15 e per year to the energy community for the voltage services was found, based on a yearly cost increase of 0.12% because of increased electricity use and battery degradation. It is suggested that the energy community and DSO should collaborate to make sure that the battery characteristics are sufficient to improve the voltage profile in the grid.

[P22] section 3.2.2, pos 3/4
Zhang et al. [62] studied smart EV charging at non-residential sites to reduce peak consumption using a proposed water-filling algorithm with an IBDRP. The work found that an 80% DCCR of the theoretical maximum could be achieved with the IBDRP, leading to significant savings to the retail venue. In the study conducted by Yee Ting Chai et al. [41], where a V2G program at an office building was implemented, a detailed analysis of the costs and benefits associated with V2G to reduce peak electricity import is included [41]. To encourage EV participation in V2G, an IBDRP is implemented, which rewards the discharging of EVs. This research indicates the potential of V2G in commercial buildings, benefiting both building and EV owners. The studies presented show that the integration of IBDRPs with other flexible resources offer a promising opportunity for enhancing grid stability, reducing peak demand, and optimizing resource utilization when various stakeholders in the energy system collaborate.

[P23] section 3.2.2, pos 4/4
Study [63] investigated how different notice times in a DRP, with continuous updates of wind forecasts, affected flexible consumers. The DRP used was demand bidding, which is defined as a market based program. The study found that a long notice time is associated with higher forecast deviations due to the uncertainty in long-term wind power predictions. A upside however, is that the longer time period means the energy demand is more flexible, allowing consumers to adjust their consumption schedules. Shorter notice time resulted in smaller forecast deviations as the certainty of predictions in wind power increased. However, the flexibility of energy demand decreased, which made it harder for consumers to reschedule their consumption at short notice.
