Abstract
Balancing technical details and computational complexity is an important trade-
off in long-term hydropower scheduling (LTHS) models. Rapidly increasing shares
of variable renewable energy sources (VRES) challenges historical assumptions
regarding the appropriate level of technical details and the representation of uncer-
tainties in LTHS models. This work expands on previous research and formulates
an efficient solution strategy to accommodate short-term variability and uncertainty
into LTHS models, through gradually approximating the short-term dispatch prob-
lem per stage and under VRES uncertainty in the context of multi-stage Benders
decomposition. The proposed solution strategy is embedded in an LTHS model
based on stochastic dual dynamic programming and applied to a 2050 scenario of
the Central and Northern European power system. We extract water values from the
LTHS strategies and analyze their dependency on short-term variability.

1Introduction

Climate and energy policy targets of the European Commission aim to make Europe
the first climate-neutral continent by 2050. To achieve this target, emissions from the
power sector must fall essentially to zero or even turn negative by 2050. This will
require large-scale deployment of variable renewable energy sources (VRES), such
as wind and solar power, and a gradual phase-out of dispatchable fossil-fueled gen-
eration technologies. This pushes forward a need for entrance of new dispatchable
resources to ensure a reliable and affordable power system. In the Nordic part of the
system, the existing hydropower facilities will play a crucial role as a dispatchable
renewable generation technology, balancing the variability in VRES.

   Operational decisions in hydropower systems need to account for both the dynam-
ics of long-term storages and the short-term variability. Toolchains of decision sup-
port models have matured over time in countries such as Brazil, Norway and the US
[1] to account for uncertainties and technical details across time scales. These tool-
chains involve long-, medium-, and short-term scheduling models suited for different
planning horizons, ranging from multiple years to weeks, and with varying levels
of technical detail and uncertainties. The long-term hydropower scheduling (LTHS)
models typically take an aggregated view on system details and use coarse time dis-
cretization to accommodate the computation of expected marginal values of water
(water values) for a multi-annual scheduling horizon within acceptable computation
times. Water values are treated as the marginal cost of hydropower in both liberal-
ized [2] and centrally planned [3] systems. While these assumptions were reasonable
for the historical system, there seems to be increasing awareness in the research com-
munity that short-term variability and uncertainty will play a central role in future
energy systems in general [4], and for the particular case of LTHS models [1, 5].

1.1 Literature review

A variety of methodologies have been proposed for solving the LTHS problem, and
the use of methods based on optimization has matured over the last decades [5]. In
particular the stochastic dual dynamic programming (SDDP) algorithm introduced
in [6] has been widely applied in operative scheduling models [7, 8], and is subject
to improvements and extensions by the research community, e.g., for shorter-term
dispatch [9] and risk management [10].

   Dynamic programming algorithms applied to the LTHS problem typically assume
weekly or monthly decision stages, where uncertainties are known within the stage.
While this assumption is reasonable for inflows, VRES variations are less predictable.
The concept of multi-horizon stochastic programming, which was first introduced in
the context of expansion planning [11], provide an efficient and integrated view on
uncertainties at different time-scales. This concept was later applied for scheduling
hydropower storages in [12] and fitted in an efficient decomposition scheme in [13].

   From a system perspective, the short-term variability and uncertainty in VRES
within LTHS models were recently addressed in [14­18]. In [14] costs associated
with short-term variability were controlled through a risk-measure, formulating the
short-term power balance as a probabilistic constraint. In [15], a medium-term sched-
uling model was calibrated through iterations with a short-term unit commitment
model to capture short-term variability. The work in [16] elegantly addressed the
combination of scheduling flexible hydropower with long-duration storage capability
and dispatch decisions made at fine time resolution, by analytically expressing the
short-term dispatch as a function of the total hydropower production prior to running
SDDP. The authors in [17] advanced the formulations of the analytical representa-
tion in [16] and included VRES uncertainty. A further improvement was presented in
[18] where the short-term dispatch function was constructed iteratively by accommo-
dating a two-stage Benders decomposition approach within the multi-stage Benders
decomposition scheme.

1.2 Proposed method and contributions

In this paper we present a solution strategy for LTHS, combining the long-term
operational planning of hydro storages under inflow uncertainty and the short-term
dispatch under VRES variability and uncertainty. The solution strategy is based on
multi-stage Benders decomposition (MSBD), and further exploits the decompos-
able structure of the short-term dispatch problem per decision stage. Inspired by the
notion of multi-horizon stochastic programming [11] and the decomposition of intra-
stage cost functions [18], we demonstrate how the short-term dispatch problem can
be efficiently approximated within the forward iteration of MSBD.

   We identify two main contributions with respect to the existing literature:

 The development of an efficient solution strategy to include treatment of short-
    term variability and uncertainty in VRES in the LTHS problem. This strategy
    consists in gradually approximating the dispatch problem per decision stage in
    the forward iterations of the MSBD algorithm. The efficiency of the solution
    strategy is demonstrated both in a didactic numerical example, and within an
    SDDP model in a large-scale case study. We add to the research in [18] by: (i)
    gradually approximating the decomposed dispatch problem per forward iteration
    rather than solving it to convergence; and (ii) demonstrating the solution strategy
    in the context of stochastic programming.

 Demonstration of the proposed solution strategy within an open-source SDDP-
    based LTHS model for a large-scale case study. The results convey new knowl-
    edge on how the variance of water values depends on short-term variability.

2 Problem description

In this section we explain the basic scheduling problem formulation, including the
representation of uncertainties and the underlying assumptions. The fictitious power
system illustrated in Fig. 1 will be used to support the explanations below, and to
illustrate the example provided in Sect. 3.3.

   Consider a system where demand is supplied by production from a hydropower
station, a wind power plant, and a set of thermal generators, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
The system is to be scheduled over a period of t  T decision stages, for which each
can be divided into k  K time steps. In the further explanations we assume weekly
decision stages and hourly time steps. Inflow to the hydro reservoir (per stage) and
maximum wind power capacities (per hour) are considered uncertain variables and
are both revealed on a weekly basis.

   The uncertainty in inflow to the reservoir is represented as a scenario tree, com-
prising 4 decision stages t1-t4 and 15 nodes n  N , as illustrated Fig. 2. Each node
represents a system state. Except for the root node in t1, each node n has one unique
ancestor node a(n). For each node n, except for the leaf nodes, two possible realiza-
tions (branches) of inflow leads to the corresponding immediate descendant nodes
d  Dn.

   We assume that hourly wind power capacities are revealed as scenarios w  W
per decision stage, and that the stage decisions related to hydropower utilization are
taken before knowing the realization of a specific wind scenario. Possible correla-
tions between inflow and wind power are assumed negligible, justifying the use of the
same set of wind scenarios for all scenario tree nodes within a stage, as indicated for
stage t2 in Fig. 2. This assumption is supported by the modest correlations between
weekly average wind and inflow observed in our data material for the case study in
Sect. 5.

3.3Example

The proposed MSBD solution algorithm is demonstrated on the power system shown
in Fig. 1 in the following example. Assume that the hydropower station has produc-
tion capacity of 15 GWh/week, and that the upstream reservoir has storage capacity
30 GWh and is initially full. Inflow to the reservoir has two equiprobable outcomes: 5
or 15 GWh/week, according to the illustration in Fig. 2, with the extension to 6 deci-
sion stages and 63 nodes. The wind power plant has a maximum output of 20 GWh/

were created per stage to represent wind power uncertainty. Thermal generation is
represented with 100 small generators, each with capacity 0.2 GWh/week and mar-

constant at 30 GWh/week. The resulting stochastic optimization problem was solved
using four different formulations:

    grams as in (2).

    and 3 until convergence at each node in the forward iteration.

4 Solution approach

This section elaborates on how the solution approach presented in the previous sec-
tion can be used in the context of LTHS for large-scale systems. We first define how
the multi-reservoir system is modeled in Sect. 4.1, before describing how the decom-
position algorithm shown in Fig. 3 naturally fits into the SDDP algorithm in Sect. 4.2.

   The SDDP algorithm can be classified as a sampling-based variant of MSBD [20],
where one simulates along sampled scenarios in the forward iteration rather than vis-
iting all nodes in a pre-defined scenario tree. Thus, the notion of a node n is replaced
by stage t in the following.

4.1 Adaption for multi-reservoir systems

We extend our problem formulation in Sect. 3 by allowing multiple hydropower sub-
systems s  S to be represented by an equivalent reservoir and power station, and
with a unique inflow.

   In the stage problem in (3), hydropower is modeled as a single reservoir with a
downstream power station, with storage and production measured in terms of energy.
If the hydropower system is a cascade, one can adopt an equivalent representation
(ER) of the reservoirs and plants in the cascaded system. This is a commonly used
technique to reduce dimensionality and computation time in LTHS models [8, 21].
One approach is to fit ER parameters so that the simulated results of the equivalent
system are similar to those obtained from the detailed system [22]. A different tech-
nique was presented in [23], constraining decisions related to the aggregated hydro-
power system by feasibility cuts. These linear inequalities can be directly derived
from optimization of the detailed hydropower system, and takes the following form:

The feasibility cuts in (5) constrain both the volume (vst) and production (est) deci-

derivation relies on convexity, a piecewise-linear and convex relationship between
production and discharge for the individual hydropower stations is assumed when
constructing feasibility cuts.

4.2SDDP

Uncertainty is represented by sampling from a statistical model rather than a pri-
ori establishing a scenario tree. Inflow to the subsystems is represented by a vector
autoregressive model of first order (VAR-1). Since inflow correlations are important
to capture in a stochastic hydropower scheduling model, and the SDDP algorithm
requires a linear (or at least convex) inflow model, the VAR model has been widely
used in practical models, see e.g. [7, 8]. Thus, inflow correlations in time and space
are considered in the stochastic model, and inflow becomes a state variable due to its
correlation in time.

4.2.3 The extended SDDP algorithm

   Another technique for analyzing water values is described below. By fixing reser-
voir storages to discrete values and assuming average inflow, the FCF cuts in (6f) can
be transformed into t+1  c, where c is a constant. Consequently, the cut with
highest c will be binding, and cut coefficients for this cut are interpreted as the water

we let the relative reservoir volumes increase proportionally to find the water values
for increasing storage volumes. An example for the Hallingdal subsystem in South-

the range of water values is wider for the cases with finer time resolution.

6Conclusions

This work presents an efficient solution strategy for embedding short-term variabil-
ity and uncertainty in an LTHS model. The short-term dispatch problem is approxi-
mated by use of Benders decomposition within each decision stage in a multi-stage
decomposition approach. Long-term and short-term uncertainties are treated sepa-
rately, assuming negligible correlation between the two. While long-term uncertainty

                                                           13
                                                                                                           A. Helseth, B. Mo

(inflow) is seen as a lumped value per stage with inter-stage correlations, short-term
uncertainties (VRES) are provided as finely resolved scenarios per stage without
inter-stage correlations.

   It was demonstrated how the proposed strategy naturally fits into the forward itera-
tion of the MSBD and SDDP schemes, gradually approximating the short-term dis-
patch problem under VRES uncertainty by cost cuts. Since the computation of cost
cuts is time consuming, further research could focus on efficient parallelization of
such computations or even performing them off-line.

   The proposed solution strategy was implemented in an open-source LTHS model
and applied to a 2050 scenario of the Central and Northern European power system.
The system was scheduled under inflow and wind power uncertainty. Results show
that the variance in water values depends on the time resolution, demonstrating the
need for fine time resolution in LTHS models applied to this type of system.

   This work leaves several pathways for further research, such as further exploring
the computational aspects related to the multi-layer decomposition scheme, improv-
ing the joint modeling of long- and short-term uncertainties, and further investigating
the sensitivity of the results to different representations of uncertainty.
