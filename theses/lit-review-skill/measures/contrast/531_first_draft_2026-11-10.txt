







 

Representing uncertainty explicitly pays off in hydropower scheduling, although
the gain depends on the plant. Belsnes et al. (2016) compare stochastic
and deterministic strategies for Norwegian cascades. The stochastic strategy
earns more in every case, mainly because it sees a risk of spill that the
deterministic plan does not. Löhndorf et al. (2013) make the same comparison for
a pumped-storage plant against a rolling deterministic plan. The gain is small
when the power capacity is small relative to the reservoir, but several times
larger when the plant can move its reservoir content quickly. A plant with
large pumping and generating power therefore has more to gain from a stochastic
policy than a conventional plant with the same reservoir.

Inflow is the uncertainty that reservoir plants cannot leave out, since it
determines how much water is available over the season. The studies that
schedule reservoir hydropower over a year or longer all treat it as stochastic
(Abgottspon and Andersson, 2013; Helseth et al., 2016; Helseth et al., 2017; Bore and Brensholm, 2026).
Inflow follows from the weather, which is why system models typically draw
inflow, temperature and wind together from historical weather years
(Helseth et al., 2018). The closed-loop plant studied by Chazarra et al. (2017) is
the exception, as its reservoir is refilled only by pumping. Findings from
such plants may transfer poorly to Norwegian pumped storage, where the pump
adds to an inflow that is itself uncertain.

Prices are the second uncertainty, and most owner-oriented studies treat prices
and inflow jointly as stochastic
(Löhndorf et al., 2013; Helseth et al., 2016; Kleiven et al., 2023; Bore and Brensholm, 2026).
Helseth et al. (2016) and Helseth et al. (2017) add uncertain reserve-capacity
prices, whereas short-term bidding models often keep only the energy price
uncertain (Faria and Fleten, 2009; Aasgård, 2020). Other studies take prices as
known and solve each price year separately (Ak et al., 2019; Chazarra et al., 2017),
which shows sensitivity to price conditions but not how decisions adapt. Each
added uncertain variable multiplies the number of states: Helseth et al. (2016)
need nine joint energy- and reserve-price states, but have only 80 simulated
scenarios from which to estimate the transitions between them.

When several inputs are uncertain, their dependence becomes a modelling choice.
The studies that test it find that it matters more for operation than for
revenue. Kleiven et al. (2023) show that ignoring the negative correlation between
Norwegian prices and inflow lowers water values and keeps the reservoir higher
in autumn. Expected revenue, however, falls by a fraction of a per cent. In
contrast, Pérez-Díaz et al. (2020) find a cross-correlation of $-0.43$
between energy and reserve prices for a Spanish plant, yet obtain almost the
same water values with an independent and a joint price chain.
Helseth et al. (2016) assume prices independent of inflow because their method
requires it, and justify the assumption from experience only. All three
concern conventional plants, so whether dependence stays unimportant for
revenue when prices also drive pumping is an open question. The present study
keeps price and inflow in joint states for that reason.

Overall, the literature settles on prices and inflow as the uncertain inputs
and keeps their number small for tractability. Their dependence appears to
matter for how the reservoir is run rather than for what it earns. For pumped
storage, however, the price enters twice: as the revenue from generating and
as the cost of pumping. How prices are described over time may therefore
carry more weight than the number of uncertain inputs. The next subsection
turns to when price information becomes known and at what resolution.
