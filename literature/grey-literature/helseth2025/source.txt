Chapter 1

Introduction

This report documents the principles behind the open source long-term hydropower scheduling model ReS-
DDP. It was prepared as a part of the research project "Modeling a 100% Renewable Electricity System" (RES100)
1. The computer code is maintained at the GitLab repository https://gitlab.sintef.no/energy/res100/
resddp. The model reads data prepared for the EMPS model (version 10).

    The main goal of the ReSDDP model is to find the expected marginal value of water (water value) in large-
scale hydropower systems as part of renewable electricity systems. Supplementary functionality for simulating
the dispatch by using the obtained water values is available, but will not be discussed in detail here. The
model is designed to explicitly model costs and technical constraints related to system dispatch. Traditionally,
this type of model is classified as long-term hydrothermal scheduling models [1], emphasizing the balance
between operation of long-term reservoirs and use of thermal generation in lieu of inflow uncertainty. In future
scenarios with large-scale deployment of variable renewable energy sources (VRES), such as wind and solar
power, and a gradual phase-out of dispatchable fossil-fueled generation technologies, the key contributors to
this balance change. In addition to VRES, new technologies are expected to add flexibility on both the supply
and demand side. These changes will stress the need to update long-term scheduling models with proper
representations of the short-term variability and uncertainty related to VRES as well as technical descriptions
of the new technologies.

    The ReSDDP model is based on stochastic optimization, where both long- and short-term uncertainties
can be represented. It was designed to balance detailed system description and computational effort by use
of decomposition techniques, as described below and illustrated in Fig. 1.1. Decomposition techniques en-
able us to formulate a lightweight and computationally efficient function to estimate coupled water values for
hydropower subsystems. The decomposition allows the separation of:

    · A Core Function: The core function consists of a stochastic dual dynamic programming (SDDP) algorithm
       applied to hydropower subsystems. The SDDP model expresses hydropower strategies by means of a fu-
       ture expected cost function (FCF), from which water values can be derived. Both the detailed hydropower
       system and the residual demand for hydropower are represented by use of decomposition techniques.

    · Approximate Hydropower: A detailed formulation of the hydropower system is formulated so that the
       feasibility of aggregated decisions per subsystem can be controlled. The feasible operation region of each
       hydropower subsystem is constrained by feasibility spaces, as elaborated in Chapter 3 and indicated in
       Fig. 1.1. The feasibility space is expressed as a function of a subsystem's decision variables, involving
       capabilities to store and produce energy, ramp production and provide reserve capacity.

    · Residual Demand for Hydropower: A detailed description of the least-cost dispatch problem is used to
       derive the residual demand cost function seen by the hydropower system, as elaborated in Chapter 4

Problem Formulation

As a starting point we describe the scheduling problem and how the SDDP algorithm can be applied to solve
it. All details regarding the hydropower system, other technologies on the supply and demand side, and the
transmission grid are explicitly formulated in a least-cost dispatch problem. Applying the complete formulation
to a large-scale system will quickly lead to a computationally intractable problem. This motivates the need for
decomposition techniques that are further described in this report.

    The SDDP algorithm was introduced in [2] and has later been widely applied in operative scheduling models
and has been subject to improvements and extensions by the research community. An introduction to some
of the extensions and application of the algorithm are provided in Section 2.6.

    The SDDP algorithm is iterative and typically relies on statistical convergence criteria. Each main SDDP
iteration consists of a forward simulation and a backward recursion, repeatedly solving stage-wise decision

2.3 Stochastic Model

The stochastic model for energy inflow is briefly described in the following. As a starting point, inflow data are
read for the detailed hydropower system. Inflow to the reservoir (regulated) and the power station (unreg-
ulated) are specified for each hydropower module. Unique historical inflow records may be associated with
both the regulated and unregulated inflow. These records are scaled according to an expected annual inflow
to form time series of regulated and unregulated inflow for each hydropower module.

    A simple aggregation, based on the best efficiency to sea, is used to aggregate detailed inflow per module,
measured in water, to time series of energy inflow per subsystem. In this process, regulated and unregulated
inflows are accumulated to energy inflow that enters the subsystem energy reservoir. The unregulated part
defines a minimum generation requirement for the subsystems equivalent power station. Next, energy inflow
time series are normalized 1, and a VAR1 model is fitted to the normalized data. The VAR1 model takes the form

2.5 Need for Approximation

The problem described in (2.7) provides a basic mathematical formulation of the stage problem which is solved
repeatedly in the SDDP forward simulation and backward recursion. More functionality is typically included,
such as environmental constraints related to hydropower operation, power flow constraints, constraints lim-
iting the ramping capability of various production technologies, other types flexibility such as batteries and
demand response, etc. Including all details soon lead to stage problems that can be classified as medium-
to large-scale LP problems, comprising hundreds of thousands of variables and constraints. Relevant experi-
ences with detailed formulation of the unit commitment and dispatch problem for the Nordic power system
are presented in [4­6], emphasizing the complexity in this type of problem.

    In a typical SDDP run, the stage problem detailed in (2.7) is solved millions of times in total. One can view
the stage problem as a function that is visited frequently to provide: (i) reservoir trajectories during the for-
ward simulation; and (ii) estimate the marginal value of the state variables in the backward recursion. In the
subsequent chapters we will describe how one can define a lightweight function by making use of decompos-
ition principles. Approximations leading to this lightweight function are derived from the underlying detailed
optimization problem.

    If the main purpose of using SDDP is to estimate coupled water values for the long-term hydropower stor-
ages that are later to be used in more detailed simulations of the system, it is reasonable to assume that the
detailed formulation in (2.7) can be relaxed or approximated to reduce computational effort. However, such
approximations should be carefully assessed in the context of the rapidly evolving power system landscape,
e.g, involving more variability and short-term uncertainty. Although the trade-off between modeling accuracy
and computational performance is not new to long-term scheduling models, the system changes ahead will
challenge us to revisit previous findings in this matter. In this context, there is a need for transparent approx-
imations for which the accuracy can be controlled.

2.6 Further Reading on SDDP

Since the seminal works in [2, 7], the SDDP algorithm has been improved in various directions and applied in
numerous studies of hydro-dominated power systems around the world. We provide some pointers to the
literature below, while emphasizing that this list by no means is complete.

    · Stochastic programming and decomposition techniques are elaborated in [3], e.g., explaining the multi-
       stage Benders decomposition algorithm in detail. An early reference on multi-stage Benders decompos-
       ition including a numerical example on hydrothermal scheduling is provided in [8], while a higher-level
       description of SDDP is provided in [9].

    · Convergence properties are studied by many authors, such as in [10] and [11]. The latter references also
       discusses limitations regarding the statistical convergence criterion.

    · Representation of uncertainty in inflow within SDDP is discussed in [12], emphasizing techniques for
       avoiding generation of negative inflows within the AR-1 model. Joint treatment of uncertainty in inflow
       and wind is discussed in [13] and [14].

    · The hydropower scheduling model ProdRisk, which is maintained by SINTEF Energy, is widely used for re-
       gional (or medium-term) hydropower scheduling in the Nordic market (price-taker assumption). ProdRisk
       is based on a combination of stochastic dynamic programming and SDDP, treating the power price a
       stochastic exogenous variable [15, 16].

Approximating Detailed Hydropower

For a typical description of the Nordic power system, a significant share of variables and constraints in stage
problem instances from (2.7) are due to the hydropower system.

    We describe a detailed hydropower system as a collection of hydropower modules which may be linked to
form one or more cascades. A module then forms a basic building block for the hydropower system, including a
reservoir and a power station. As an example, consider a model representing a system with 1000 hydropower
modules, with hourly time resolution. This would lead to 168.000 reservoir constraints of type (2.7f) and
168.000 hydropower production variables (). Moreover, the dimensionality of the FCF represented by cuts
of type (2.7j) will be 1000 plus the number of individual inflow time series being represented in the AR1 model.
The high dimensionality may hamper both the SDDP convergence properties and the accuracy of the stochastic
inflow model, as demonstrated and discussed in [17].

    In the following we discuss how the operation of complex hydropower systems can be approximated by use
of feasibility spaces, represented by linear inequalities which we refer to as feasibility cuts. The details related
to the construction and use of feasibility cuts were first described in [18] and later explored in [19]. We use
the term hydropower subsystem to describe a collection of hydropower modules for which aggregate decision
are made. For each price area with hydropower we require at least one hydropower subsystem. The use
of feasibility spaces is based on the assumption that operation-dependent costs associated with hydropower
operation can be neglected.

3.6 Assumptions and Further Work

The use of feasibility spaces to approximate the detailed hydropower system relies on a series of assumptions:

    · Hydropower operation has no explicit cost components. As discussed in the previous sections, the al-
       ternative cost of stored energy is accounted for in the SDDP algorithm, and the cost of operating pumps
       can be accounted for in (3.17).

    · Subsystem capabilities are expressed per stage. As shown in [18] capabilities can be expressed per time
       step within the stage, but this modeling comes at the expense of significantly increased computational
       effort. Making subsystem decision per stage challenges the capability of representing intra-stage vari-
       ations such as ramping. An approximate solution was presented in Section 3.3.1.

    · Disaggregation is approximated. The aggregated system state, including the initial storage and inflow for
       the subsystem, needs to be disaggregated to the detailed problem in (3.17). Moreover, the aggregated
       energy storage at the end of the stage needs to be computed based on the detailed storage volumes.
       This topic was discussed in Section 3.4.1.

The work on feasibility spaces has been documented in [18] and [19], including mathematical descriptions and
numerical analyses. Some possibilities for further work are listed below:

    · Further testing and validation is needed. For example, one could test to what extent feasibility cuts may
       capture the impact of new constraints, planned revisions, pumped storage plants, etc.

    · Improve the distribution of the subsystem's energy storage to the detailed hydro storages in the feas-
       ibility check. The process of balancing stoarge levels across complex hydropower storages is, to a large
       extent, controlled by the operator in real life. This process can borrow from concepts developed for the
       detailed drawdown part of the EMPS model [20].

    · Improve the distribution of the subsystem's energy inflow to detailed hydro storages in the feasibility
       check. This process is uncertain and cannot be planned. One could explore feasibility spaces that are
       robust towards different and unfortunate distributions of inflow.

    · Further study and possibly improve on the aggregate capabilities of hydropower representation on sub-
       system level.

Residual Demand Cost Function

With the high variability in the power system caused by increasing amounts of wind and solar power, there
is a growing need for fine time resolution in scheduling models. As this variability will impact the economic
dispatch, and consequently the marginal cost of supplying demand, it should also be considered when valuating
water. With modeling at a finer time resolution comes higher computation times, and thus a need for careful
modeling.

    So far we have treated the stage decision problem as an LP according to (3.11) where the hydropower system
is approximated by feasibility cuts as described in Chapter 3. Hydropower subsystems will mainly allow flexible
operation within a decision stage (week). Recall that their storages are state variables in the stochastic dynamic
programming scheme, and we can therefore assume that they most of the time can accommodate variations
within the week without reaching their storage boundaries. This leads to the idea of optimizing the use of
hydropower for the whole week against a residual demand cost function (RDCF) which represents 'everything
else' being part of the economic dispatch problem. The separation between hydropower system itself and the
RDCF for hydro is done by use of two-stage Benders decomposition, serving to valuate hydropower capabilities.

    In this work we follow the decomposition principle first discussed in [21] and later explored in [22] and
[23]. The concept is illustrated in Fig. 4.1, indicating abstraction of the residual demand for hydro by means of
decomposition. The decomposition allows us to represent the RDCF as a function of the hydropower decision
variables, expressed as linear inequalities, or cost cuts, as explained in the following.

4.5 Short-Term Uncertainties

The presentation so far has focused on representing uncertainty in inflow on a weekly level, according to
an VAR1 model. Inflow is often considered the major source of uncertainty in hydro-dominated systems. It
typically show a significant autocorrelation between weeks, necessitating inflows as state variables in SDDP.
Moreover, inflow predictions within a decision stage (week) are considered to be appropriately accurate for
our purpose.

    Several other sources of uncertainty may be relevant to include in the scheduling. In this work we are
particularly interested in representing the uncertainty in VRES (wind and solar power). Unlike for inflow, the
variability in VRES is typically more difficult to predict for the near future, while longer term auto-correlations
are muted. Thus, we argue that VRES uncertainty can be characterized as short-term, and wind and solar
power can be neglected as state variables in the SDDP algorithm. Further statistical analyses should be done
to support such statements for the system at hand, see e.g. [13] and [14] for related discussions.

    Creation of cost cuts naturally fits in to the forward simulation phase, where the state trajectories are
simulated according to sampled inflow scenarios. The process of creating cost cuts is illustrated in Fig. 4.10.
First, StageProb, formulated as (5.1), is solved. Subsequently, the hydropower production is passed to the
residual market problem CostProb, formulated as (4.8). Along the lines of [23], short-term uncertainties can
be revealed in the second stage, solving CostProb for  different realizations of VRES. A total of  iterations
between LP-FWD and LP-COST are performed.

                                  Figure 4.10: LP problems solved in forward iterations.

    Cost cuts can either be generated for each of the  scenarios (multi-cut) or as an aggregate cut where
cut coefficients are probability-weighted. In the following we use the aggrgated cuts.
The outlined approach for representing short-term uncertainties are based on two assumptions:

    · The hydropower productions are part of the first-stage decision. Thus, these decisions are made with
       information about VRES uncertainty, but are not tailored to a specific VRES scenario.

    · VRES are not SDDP state variables. Thus, VRES realizations in a given decision stage do not depend on
       realizations in the previous stage. Moreover, it is assumed that wind and inflow are uncorrelated.

Under the assumptions above, cost cuts can be shared among different states in a given stage without further
adjustments. Note that the two-stage stochastic approach allows for other types uncertainties to be repres-
ented, as long as the two assumptions above can be made. Uncertain parameters entering the objective (such
as prices) and the constraint matrix (such as topological changes) may therefore be considered.

    The treatment of VRES uncertainty within the ReSDDP model is also described in [25], supported by nu-
merical examples.

4.6 Assumptions and Further Work

The approximation of the residual demand for hydropower within long-term hydropower scheduling models
by means of cost cuts is based on a set of assumptions:

    · Hydropower operation can be optimized at coarse time resolution. Through this assumption we argue
       that hydropower operation is flexible and sees no (or few) operational constraints within the week. While
       this may be true for large and aggregated systems, many intra-week constraints exists and may be im-
       portant for the formation of water values.

    · Hydropower decisions are made before knowing short-term uncertainties. Following the representation
       of short-term uncertainties described in Section 4.5, weekly decisions for the hydropower system are
       made before knowing weekly VRES outcomes. This allows the cost cuts to embed short-term uncertainty.
       The VRES realizations are known for the whole week at the time.

    · The residual demand optimization problem is convex. The least-cost dispatch problem for the week
       should be formulated as a convex optimization problem to allow for computation of cost cuts.

    The work on approximations of the residual demand for hydro has been documented in [21­23,25], includ-
ing mathematical descriptions and numerical analyses. Some possibilities for further work are listed below:

    · Further investigating the representation of hydropower system. While the modeling of hydropower de-
       cisions at a coarse time resolution is important for the computational performance, the need for ag-
       gregation and the possibility of further constraining of its flexibility in the market problem should be
       investigated.

    · Further investigating the representation of the residual market problem. The majority of further ex-
       perimentation with the ReSDDP model within the RES100 project will directly concern the addition of
       new functionality in the market problem. New features such as demand response, batteries, and hydro-
       gen will be implemented and studied. Other aspects, such as detailed grid constraints, (linearised) unit
       commitment, and ramping constraints may also be included.

    · Offline approximation of the residual demand function. The proposed scheme evaluates the market
       problem in the forward SDDP iterations. This allows the residual demand function to be evaluated
       around obtained operating points. Optionally, one could experiment with a priori estimation of this
       function.

    · Representation of short-term uncertainties. Studies to further investigate the impact of short-term un-
       certainty should be carried out. Different uncertainty structures and types could be investigated.

ReSDDP

So far we have defined the long-term scheduling problem in Chapter 2, and described how decomposition by
feasibility and cost cuts can be applied to isolate the computation of future cost functions for the hydropower
subsystems, in Chapters 3 and 4, respectively. The future cost functions are computed in the SDDP algorithm.
Both the feasibility and the cost cuts can be seen as spatial decomposition techniques, serving to reduce the
dimensionality of the stage problem solved in the SDDP algorithm.

    The resulting scheduling model will provide strategies, in the form of future expected cost functions per
decision stage. These strategies are valid for the long-term storage capability provided by hydropower subsys-
tems and can be used in more detailed simulation models. A desirable property of the outlined decomposition
scheme is that it allows explicit representation of all detailed technologies and power system features into the
computation of the FCFs:

    · Hydropower details are embedded in the fasibility cuts

    · Other technologies on supply and demand side as well as grid bottlenecks are reflected in the cost cuts

This allows quantifying the impact of various features on the water values in a transparent manner, where the
accuracy of the approximations can be controlled to a fine level.

    The ReSDDP is modeled in Julia using the optimization package JuMP [26]. The computer code is main-
tained at the Gitlab repository at https://gitlab.sintef.no/energy/res100/.

5.2 Cut Management

FCF cuts of type (5.1i) are computed in the SDDP backward recursion. These cuts are not necessarily tight since
we have decomposed the overall problem into multiple stages. Efficient treatment of Benders cuts within the
SDDP algorithm is important to achieve reasonable computation times for large-scale problems. Several tech-
niques are suggested in the literature, see e.g. [27]. A well-known technique is often referred to as relaxation
(or dynamic cut selection in [27]). It involves: (i) solving the decision problem without any cuts; (ii) checking
if cuts are violated by inserting trial decisions; and (iii) adding the most violated cut(s). Such relaxation tech-
niques can be very efficiently implemented in compiled programming languages, but at the time of writing
we did not see a significant speed-up when implementing relaxation in our Julia/JuMP code. Thus, we do not
consider cut relaxation for neither of the cut types in this work. Another technique is to remove redundant cut
according to the elimination procedure outlined in [28] (Section 4.3).

    The feasibility cuts in (5.1g) and cost cuts in (5.1h) are derived from two-stage Benders decomposition ap-
proaches and are therefore always tight in the states for which they were created. In other words, and unlike
the case for FCF cuts in a multi-stage decomposition procedure, creating the cut once for a given state is suffi-
cient. This opens for the possibility of creating the feasibility and cost cuts before running the SDDP algorithm.

PROJECT NUMBER              REPORT NUMBER      VERSION              35 of 48

502003757                   2025:00598         1
As an example, the creation of feasibility cuts was made both prior to running SDDP (static) or as part of the
forward simulation (dynamic) in [18]. The static approach requires either an enumeration of the state space or
a qualified best guess of which states that are likely to be visited in the SDDP run. The ReSDDP implementation
currently allows feasibility cuts to be computed on beforehand by use of enumeration. Since feasibility cuts are
computed per subsystem in isolation, the state space to enumerate is relatively modest. Conversely, cost cuts
involves decision variables for all hydropower subsystems and are therefore considerably more challenging to
create based on enumeration.

    In summary, the creation and management of cuts in ReSDDP is implemented as follows:

    · FCF cuts are computed in the backward recursion. These cuts are accumulated, i.e., the number of cuts
       gradually increases per backward recursion.

    · Feasibility cuts are computed before running the SDDP algorithm. The number of feasibility cuts is con-
       stant throughout the SDDP run.

    · Cost cuts are computed as part of the forward iteration. A user-defined number  of feasibility cuts
       are computed per forward scenario and per decision stage.

    To allow experimentation with cut management, the ReSDDP model can be run in continuation mode.
Then, a previously established set of FCF and cost cuts are read as a starting point, and further iterations are
run to improve the established strategy. In continuation mode one can choose to turn off further computation
of cost cuts.

6.6 Feasibility Cuts

We repeated cases T1 but this time with the use of feasibility cuts. Feasibility cuts were computed prior to
the SDDP run and used throughout the iterations. A 3-hour time resolution was used when computing feas-
ibility cuts, and the subsystem decisions related to energy, volume, and ramping capability were discretized
in 5 segments. The need for reserve capacity was not considered, and all time-dependent and environmental
constraints were deactivated. A total 42, 40, and 35 feasibility cuts were created for each of the three systems.
As a consequence, the total computation time increased with approximately 30 % compared to the values
reported in Table 6.2.

    The strategies were simulated using the same 50 scenarios as for the cases without feasibility cuts. We
compare simulation results for case T1 with and without feasibility cuts in Fig. 6.9 and 6.10.

    The duration curves for production for the three hydropower subsystems are shown in Fig. 6.9. This figure
shows that, although the total energy production per subsystem seems similar, the use of feasibility cuts (black
curves) leads to a more constrained operation for subsystems 2 and 3.

    Duration curves for simulated power prices for areas 3 and 4 are shown in Fig. 6.10. The use of feasibility
cuts further constrains the operation of hydropower, which in turn contributes to a higher price level.

Chapter 7

Summary

This report documents the methodological basis for the ReSDDP model. It first introduces the operational
planning problem and describes the SDDP algorithm. Subsequently, further decomposition through feasibility
cuts and cost cuts are presented, leading to the ReSDDP model algorithm outlined in Chapter5. Finally, the
model was applied to a simple test system to demonstrate its computational capabilities and show some of
the result types that one can extract from it.
