# Fatigue damage in hydro turbines (Liu, Luo & Wang, 2016)

## Research question / purpose
Review of loading features and fatigue damage in hydraulic turbines (mainly Francis runners), the effect of operating regimes (part load, start-up, emergency shutdown, load rejection, runaway), materials and welds, and methods for predicting fatigue damage and remaining life; brief coverage of cavitation, erosion and ingested bodies (abstract, p. 1).

## Setting
- **System:** hydraulic turbines in general, mainly Francis runners; some remarks on reversible pump-turbines (p. 6).
- **Markets:** none. Motivation includes the shift in Europe toward more flexible operation driven by wind and solar (p. 5).
- **Horizon and resolution:** not applicable (engineering review).

## Method
- **Model type:** literature review with one illustrative case (crack-growth analysis of two start-up schemes using Hydro-Québec in-situ measurements at Beauharnois, Sec. 4.4, pp. 10–11).
- **Uncertainty / solution method:** not applicable.

## Data / case
Table 1 (p. 2) lists fatigue-related failures with outage durations and estimated lost revenue. Case in Sec. 4.4: strain measurements from Beauharnois (2002), crack growth per BS 7910:2005 (p. 10).

## Key findings
- (pp. 1–2) Fatigue is the most fundamental failure type; some plants were shut down for up to a month or longer for repairs, with large economic losses (Table 1). Many turbines now run under conditions very different from their design conditions.
- (p. 5) Very low part-load operation used to be exceptional, but in Europe the growth of wind and solar has created a trend to run hydro turbines more often at low part load and for longer; prolonged low-load operation can consume runner life quickly.
- (p. 6) Transient events "often account for most of the damages" in operation. Start-up is one of the most damaging conditions: the damage from one start-up can equal many hours or even days of low-load or speed-no-load operation, and one start-up can reduce runner life as much as many years of normal operation (citing [67], [69]). Normal shutdown is mild; emergency shutdown and load rejection are severe.
- (p. 7) Load variation, start–stop and load rejection cannot be avoided, but improved start–stop schemes can significantly extend runner life; utilities "have to understand the full impact of start/stop cycles" to optimise maintenance and operation (p. 8).
- (pp. 10–11) The two-start-up-scheme case shows that the start-up sequence can strongly affect life expectancy; results are plant-specific.

## Strengths
- Compact, citable source for the physical reason behind start-up costs and wear from cycling.

## Weaknesses / limitations
- No cost numbers per start or per cycle that a scheduling model could use directly; focused on Francis turbines, little on pump-turbines in pumping mode.
- Quantitative statements on start-up damage are relayed from other studies ([67], [69]), not shown here.
