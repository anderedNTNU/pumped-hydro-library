# Investment and operational planning of a storage plant (Sauhats et al., 2016)

> **Page convention.** Journal pages 12443–12453 = PDF pages 1–11 (journal p. = PDF p. + 12442).

## Research question / purpose
Develop a combined short-term scheduling and long-term investment-planning method for **stand-alone storage power plants** that earn money by **day-ahead price arbitrage**, accounting for price uncertainty, over a **30-year** planning horizon; apply it as a pre-feasibility / cost–benefit analysis of a **pumped-storage hydropower (PSH)** plant and a **hydrogen storage** plant (abstract, p. 12443; p. 12444). Contributions (p. 12445): a problem statement that accounts for uncertainty, ANN-based forecasting of volatile parameters, and an algorithm combining long-, medium- and short-term planning for operational and investment planning.

## Setting
- **System:** generic closed-loop, stand-alone storage plant characterised only by charge efficiency, discharge efficiency, installed power, discharge time and capital cost (p. 12446). Case: **pure (closed-loop) PSH**, 25 MW generation, **150 MWh storage, 6 h discharge**, turbine efficiency 0.9, pump efficiency 0.8 (round trip 0.72), vs. a hydrogen plant (electrolysis 0.7, fuel cells 0.7) with the same 25 MW / 150 MWh (Table 1, p. 12450; test case p. 12448). No natural inflow, no reservoir/head data.
- **Markets:** **day-ahead only** — Nord Pool (Elspot) **Latvian bidding area**, hourly prices 2013–2015 (p. 12443, p. 12445). Plants are **price-takers**, prices exogenous (p. 12444). No reserves, intraday or forwards.
- **Horizon and resolution:** 30-year investment horizon (2 years construction) (p. 12450); medium-term 360 h (15 days) LP around each sampled day; short-term 24 h NLP; **hourly** resolution (pp. 12446–12449).

## Method
- **Model type / algorithm (Fig. 4, pp. 12446–12447):**
  1. Randomly select **N days** per year of the horizon (Monte Carlo).
  2. For each day, **deterministic medium-term LP** over 1–2 weeks before and after the day (hourly), using the point forecast; it only provides the storage level at the start and end of the selected day.
  3. **Short-term 24-h optimisation** per selected day, nonlinear programming, over M price realisations → expected daily income and expense.
  4. **Annualise** by time-averaging (expected annual net cash flow ≈ time average, p. 12447) → NPV (eq. 6), IRR (eq. 10), payback.
- **Short-term formulation (eqs. 1–5, p. 12447):** decision ΔL_{i,t} = change of stored energy per hour; maximise (1/M) Σ_m Σ_t P_{i,t} c_{i,m,t}, where P = −ΔL·k/η_rt when charging (ΔL > 0) and P = −ΔL·k when discharging (eq. 2): **round-trip efficiency applied entirely on the charging side**; Σ_t ΔL = L_T − L_0 (eq. 3); cumulative storage bounds (eqs. 4–5). The piecewise, non-smooth objective is solved with MATLAB **pattern search**, warm-started by an interior-point solution (p. 12448).
- **How uncertainty is represented (pp. 12445–12446):** **electricity price only**. Three-layer feed-forward **ANN** point forecast (Levenberg–Marquardt; inputs: Latvian DA price, day of week and hour, ambient temperature), with training-window length (336–672 h) and hidden neurons (10–15) re-selected by partial enumeration before each forecast, by minimum MSE on the previous day. **Price realisations** are generated by adding logged historical forecast errors to the point forecast (a "naïve model", Fig. 1, p. 12445), assuming error statistics constant in time. Long-term: uniform **3% annual price increase** (base case) or scenarios with 0–8% annual increase in the peak/off-peak spread (p. 12451). Days are sampled randomly (Monte Carlo).
- **Economic model (pp. 12448, 12450):** NPV with discount rate 7%; revenue (eq. 7) and charging cost (eq. 8) annualised as 365/(M·N) × sampled sums; fixed O&M; investment = (1 + contingency) × TCC (eq. 9), contingency 10%; TCC = PCS + BOP cost per kW + storage section cost per kWh (Zakeri & Syri 2015).
- **Key modelling choices and assumptions:**
  - Constant efficiencies; no head effects; energy-based storage (no water volumes).
  - Each 15-day medium-term window starts and ends at **50% filling** (p. 12449) → no inter-week or seasonal coupling, no water values.
  - Variable O&M ignored (p. 12447). Price-taker.
  - Note (our reading): in eq. (1) one schedule ΔL_{i,t} applies to all M realisations, so the objective is linear in prices and equals optimising against the **average** of the M realisations; it is not a recourse model. The paper does not discuss this.

## Data / case
Nord Pool Latvian area hourly DA prices and ambient temperature 2013–2015 (p. 12445). Table 1 (p. 12450): PSH — PCS+BOP **528 €/kW**, storage section **68 €/kWh**, TCC **23.4 M€**, fixed O&M **4.6 €/kW** (per year presumably, unverified); hydrogen — 1383 €/kW, 3.7 €/kWh, TCC 35.13 M€, fixed O&M 16 €/kW. Cost data from Zakeri & Syri [3]. PC: 2× Xeon E5-2630 v3, 16 GB RAM, 12 parallel MATLAB workers (p. 12449).

## Key findings
- (p. 12449) **Medium-term horizon:** 29 days vs 15 days around 20 Jan 2015 gives near-identical start/end levels (40.05%/26.91% vs 40.06%/27.29%) and daily profit (4059.58 € vs 4025.56 €, −0.84%) → 15 days used.
- (p. 12449) **Boundary storage level:** start/end 50/50% vs 100→0% vs 0→100% changes daily profit by only 0.16% and 0.91% → 50% at start and end of every 15-day window.
- (pp. 12449–12450, Fig. 7) **Monte Carlo days:** vs 365-day reference, **80 sampled days** per year gives 4.1% estimation error at 19 min 58 s computation.
- (p. 12450) **Stochastic vs point forecast:** with 11 price realisations for 21 Jan 2015, expected daily profit rose from 1727.2 € to 1905.8 € (+10.34%); the authors conclude a stochastic approach is beneficial (see our critique above).
- (p. 12451, Fig. 9) **Base case:** PSH payback **24 years, IRR 8.3%, NPV 4,204,645 €** by year 30; hydrogen NPV **−32.7 M€**, no break-even. Hydrogen would need PCS+BOP of 207.84 €/kW (TCC 5,750,915 €) to break even.
- (p. 12451, Fig. 10, Table 2) Hydrogen needs **≥ 8% annual growth in the price spread** to pay back (NPV 11.99 M€, IRR 8.5%, payback 27 y). Support schemes: doubled selling price → payback 6 y, NPV 20.99 M€; zero-interest loans (r = 0) → break-even in 15 y, NPV 10.76 M€.
- (p. 12452) Conclusions: hourly operating conditions can be accounted for over decades-long horizons; problem split into medium-term (stored energy), short-term (daily profit) and long-term (NPV/IRR by Monte Carlo with few trials).

## Strengths
- Links **hourly arbitrage operation to investment metrics** (NPV, IRR, payback) — exactly the operation-to-value chain needed to compare configurations.
- Transparent, reusable **cost parameters** split into power-related (€/kW) and energy-related (€/kWh) parts, enabling cost comparisons of power vs storage duration.
- Practical tuning studies (horizon length, boundary levels, number of sampled days) with quantified errors.

## Weaknesses / limitations
- Only **one configuration** (25 MW / 150 MWh) per technology; **no sizing optimisation**, no variation of power or storage duration — sensitivity only on capital costs and price spread.
- **Short-duration** (6 h) storage: daily decoupling with a fixed 50% boundary level eliminates any value of inter-week or seasonal storage; no water values.
- Price uncertainty enters only through the average of forecast-error realisations (single schedule for all realisations); no recourse, no risk measure.
- Uniform 3% price growth for 30 years; prices exogenous to storage expansion.
- Day-ahead arbitrage only (no reserves); no inflow; constant efficiencies; venue is a hydrogen journal, PSH modelled generically.
- Some text extraction ambiguity in Table 1 units (fixed O&M per kW, per year presumably).
