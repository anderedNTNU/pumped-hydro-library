Hydropower scheduling is studied from a system perspective and from the perspective of a single producer. Which one a study takes depends on whose decision it supports, the operator of a whole system or the owner of a plant. The two perspectives answer different questions, and results do not transfer directly between them.

System studies model several areas with generation and demand, connected by transmission, and optimise all plants under one objective (Helseth et al., 2013; Henden et al., 2016; Helseth et al., 2018). The prices are a result. This gives a consistent picture of prices, exchange and reservoir use across the system. Such models are therefore used to plan the operation of a whole system, as Helseth et al. (2013) do for Iceland, or to value an expansion by the change in socio-economic surplus, as Henden et al. (2016) do for Norwegian pumped storage. Their objective is the system's cost or surplus, not the profit of any one producer (Helseth et al., 2023). What a producer earns can still be read from the resulting prices and dispatch. However, that dispatch is the system's optimum rather than the owner's.

The owner perspective instead takes a producer that operates its own plants. It maximises their expected operating profit over a planning horizon, given forecasts of future prices, inflow and other inputs (Helseth et al., 2023). Helseth et al. (2016) schedule a Norwegian watercourse in this way against energy and reserve prices. Ak et al. (2019) and Alic et al. (2023) value the addition of pumping to an existing cascade by the change in its revenue. What this perspective loses is the feedback from the plant's own decisions to prices, which matters when the plant is large relative to its market (Henden et al., 2016). For the question of what a plant's operation is worth to its owner, it is nevertheless the relevant perspective.

Within the owner perspective, studies differ in whether the producer influences prices. Most treat it as a price-taker, for whom prices are uncertain inputs unaffected by its own decisions (Helseth et al., 2016; Ak et al., 2019; Bore and Brensholm, 2026). This is justified by the small size of the plant relative to its price zone and by the efficiency of a competitive market (Helseth et al., 2023). For other markets the assumption is less clear. A price-maker formulation is used where the producer's bids are large relative to the market, which is more likely in the small reserve markets (Pérez-Díaz et al., 2015; Pérez-Díaz et al., 2020). Abgottspon and Andersson (2013) let prices depend linearly on the quantities a pumped-storage producer trades, whereas Pérez-Díaz et al. (2020) model price-making in the reserve market only. However, the assumed price response is itself uncertain, and it makes the revenue function non-convex. The decomposition methods used for long horizons then need extensions (Füllner and Rebennack, 2025).

Overall, the operation of a plant is valued from its owner's perspective as the expected profit under given forecasts. A price-taking producer is the common choice when the plant is small relative to its markets. The remaining question for this review is therefore not whose value is measured, but which uncertain inputs, markets and plant capacities the studies include.
