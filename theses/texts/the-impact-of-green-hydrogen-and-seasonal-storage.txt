Abstract

Mitigating climate change relies heavily on transforming energy production. For many sectors within
transport and industry, electrification via variable renewable energy sources (VRES) is not a viable
solution. Emission-free hydrogen has emerged as a promising alternative for these hard-to-abate sectors.
This approach has recently gained traction through the European Union's policies and subsidies aimed
at promoting investments in green hydrogen. While these initiatives have the potential to accelerate
investments in VRES and hydrogen storage, there is a gap in research analyzing their impacts on the
European energy system.

   This thesis addresses the challenges in modeling the European energy system, specifically concerning
policy representation and technical modeling decisions. The objectives are to provide valuable insights
into (1) the impact of implementing the European definition of green hydrogen and (2) the effects of
seasonal hydrogen storage on energy system costs and investment decisions in the future European energy
system.

   To address the first problem, we extend the multi-horizon stochastic capacity expansion model EMPIRE
with additional constraints to enforce all hydrogen production to originate from electrolysis that complies
with the European requirements for green hydrogen. The second problem is addressed by introducing
a novel method to represent seasonal storage in multi-horizon stochastic programming by incorporating
tactical stages in the operational horizon to account for seasonal variations and uncertainty.

   Our results show that implementing the European green hydrogen definition will incur considerable
cost increases due to an accelerated shift towards investments in VRES. This is primarily because the
requirement mandates that all hydrogen be produced using power from new renewable power generators.
Integrating seasonal hydrogen storage into energy system modeling can mitigate these costs, as it enables
the transfer of large amounts of energy from summer to winter by using cost-effective VRES. This
is particularly important in systems with high shares of green hydrogen, where hydrogen production
availability is constrained by VRES output. Therefore, a key finding in this thesis is that green hydrogen
and seasonal hydrogen storage are complementary strategies that can accelerate the transition to a
sustainable energy system.

Sammendrag

En viktig faktor i arbeidet med å begrense klimaendringer er omleggingen av energiproduksjonen.
Elektrifisering ved hjelp av fornybare energikilder er ikke en gjennomførbar løsning for mange sektorer
innen transport og industri. Grønt hydrogen er et lovende alternativ for disse sektorene, noe som
understrekes av betydelige subsidier fra EU. Selv om EU sine iniativer har potensial til å akselerere
investeringer i fornybare energikilder og hydrogenlagring, er det et gap i forskningen som analyserer
deres påvirkning på det europeiske energisystemet.

   Denne oppgaven tar for seg utfordringene i modelleringen av det europeiske energisystemet, spesielt
med hensyn til politisk representasjon og tekniske modelleringsbeslutninger. Målene er å gi innsikt i
(1) virkningen av å implementere den europeiske definisjonen av grønn hydrogen og (2) effektene av
langsiktig hydrogenlagring på energisystemkostnader og investeringsbeslutninger i fremtidens europeiske
energisystem.

   For å løse det første problemet, utvider vi den "multi-horizon" stokastiske modellen EMPIRE med
ytterligere beskrankninger for å sikre at all hydrogenproduksjon fra elektrolyse overholder de europeiske
kravene til grønn hydrogen. Det andre problemet løses ved å introdusere en ny metode for å representere
langsiktig lagring i EMPIRE, hvor vi inkludere taktisk usikkerhet ved bruk av "multi-stage" operasjonelle
problemer.

   Resultatene viser at implementering av den europeiske definisjonen av grønt hydrogen vil medføre
betydelige kostnadsøkninger på grunn av en hyppigere skifte mot utslippsfri teknologi. Dette skyldes
primært at kravet innebærer at all hydrogen må produseres ved bruk av strøm fra nye fornybare kraft-
generatorer. Integrering av langsiktig hydrogenlagring i energisystemmodellering kan redusere disse
kostnadene, da det muliggjør overføring av store mengder energi fra sommer til vinter ved bruk av relativt
billigere fornybare energikilder. Dette er spesielt viktig i energisystemer med høy andel grønn hydrogen,
hvor tilgjengeligheten av hydrogenproduksjon er begrenset av fornybar kraft. Et nøkkelfunn i denne
oppgaven er at grønn hydrogen og langsiktig hydrogenlagring er komplementære strategier som kan
akselerere overgangen til et bærekraftig energisystem.

Chapter 1

Introduction

1.1 An European hydrogen future

   Decarbonizing energy and industry is crucial for achieving global climate targets and ensuring a sus-
tainable future. Energy, transport, and industry are the primary sources of CO2 emissions in Europe,
accounting for 81.6% of total emissions in 2021 (International Energy Agency, 2023). Although renew-
able energy generators have become the most cost-effective energy source, offering a clear solution for
reducing emissions in power production, the two latter sectors face more complex challenges.

   In an effort to decarbonize hard-to-abate sectors, hydrogen has been proposed as a solution for decades
(Yap and McLellan, 2023). While hydrogen has periodically experienced surges of interest, driven by
energy security concerns and climate optimism, it has also faced resistance and periods of declining
enthusiasm, influenced by the financial crisis and the rapid advancements in electric vehicle technology.
Due to the unpredictable and variable outlook for hydrogen, some fear that the recent wave of enthusiasm
may be another example of overhyped aspirations (Küfeolu, 2023).

   The current situation for hydrogen is distinguished from the pre-financial crisis era by an increased
focus on energy and resource security, coupled with the urgency of climate mitigation. This has led to
greater multilateral efforts towards developing a European hydrogen economy (Commission, 2024). In
February 2024, the European Hydrogen Bank held a pilot auction for subsidies towards green hydrogen
production with a total budget of =C800 million, ensuring production of 8.8 million tonnes of carbon-
neutral hydrogen during the following decade (European Commission, 2024). Although the budget will
not suffice to produce up to its 10 million ton production targets before 2030, the auction is a major step
for policymakers to demonstrate their commitment to a hydrogen future. It will also enable the EU to
gather reliable data on the competitiveness of such support mechanisms and has laid the groundwork for

                                                              1
future auctions (Europe, 2024).
   Today, hydrogen plays a crucial role in industries such as ammonia, oil refining, and methanol produc-

tion, with an annual consumption of 8 million tonnes in Europe (Observatory, 2023). Although this is a
substantial amount, 87% of the hydrogen is produced and consumed on-site within industrial processes.
Consequently, the market and supply chain for hydrogen in Europe remains relatively small.

   To achieve the EU's ambitious goals, the existing hydrogen supply chain must undergo a major change,
shifting from a decentralized network of mostly fossil-based hydrogen production towards an intricate
network of large-scale electrolysis production. This will include off-grid renewable installations and on-
grid production facilities with pipelines connecting them to European hydrogen valleys. The European
Hydrogen Backbone initiative is a proposed network of hydrogen pipelines aimed at facilitating the large-
scale transport of hydrogen across Europe. The proposed strategy includes repurposing existing natural
gas pipelines and building new infrastructure to create a dedicated hydrogen transport system, promoting
a renewable and low-carbon hydrogen market (Backbone, 2024).

   Hydrogen may serve as a source of energy in hard-to-abate sectors, where decarbonizing through
power or batteries faces major challenges. In shipping and aviation, for example, batteries would be too
heavy for long-range transportation. In industry, electrical power alone would fail to create enough heat
for certain industrial processes. Examples of such industries are cement and steel production, where
temperatures exceed 1,400 degrees Celsius. The potential use areas are plentiful, and some estimates
show that the energy need could grow to an amount equal to half of the power consumption in Europe by
2050 (ENTSO-E, 2022). Additionally, hydrogen may be of major value in stabilizing short and long-term
power markets, as its production may be coordinated with power produced from variable renewable
energy sources (VRES) (Ruhnau, 2022).

   Hydrogen can be produced from various energy sources and methods, resulting in different color
classifications. The most common are gray, blue, and green, displayed in Figure 1.1. Gray and blue
hydrogen are fossil-based, but blue includes capturing CO2 from its production, while green hydrogen
is power-based and carbon-neutral. To prevent greenwashing, the EU implemented rules for labeling
hydrogen as green through The Delegated Acts on Renewable Hydrogen (European Commission, 2023c)
in June 2023. This definition, explored later in this thesis, requires green hydrogen to be sourced from
new, renewable energy used for water electrolysis. This definition is important because the EU has
no intentions of including any large-scale blue or gray hydrogen in their future hydrogen supply chain
ambitions, despite some research pointing at blue hydrogen as a valuable transitory solution to a hydrogen
revolution.

1.2 Energy system modelling

   With the vast array of potential climate mitigation strategies for the energy sector, numerous modeling
approaches are being employed to explore these different possibilities (Bistline et al., 2020). One
such approach employs two-stage linear optimization models. In the first stage, these models assist in
making investment decisions for power generation and transmission. In the second stage, they optimize
operational energy production decisions based on the available invested capacity. Although these models
have primarily focused on power generation and transmission capacity expansion, explicitly modeling
other energy domains has become increasingly apparent as hydrogen becomes more integrated into the
European energy system (Prina et al., 2020). Multi-energy system modeling integrates numerous energy
domains by allowing conversion and energy demand to be covered by either of the sources (Backe et al.,
2021b). Given the potential of hydrogen as a future energy source, numerous papers discuss its potential
value in the future European energy system. The research aims at covering different aspects of the
hydrogen supply chain, such as the effects of hydrogen pipelines for power transmission capacity deferral
(Neumann et al., 2023) and finding an equilibrium for hydrogen production and consumption in different
industrial sectors given an endogenous hydrogen demand (Durakovic et al., 2023).

                                3
1.3 Objective of the thesis

   Our literature review discovered that extensive progress has been achieved over the last decade in
research dedicated to exploring scenarios for a European hydrogen future. While results are highly
dependent on the modeling assumptions, we generally observed that to different degrees, most articles
conclude that blue hydrogen will have a presence in Europe despite EU skepticism. In other words, we
have observed little research aligned with the EU strategy, building on scenarios where emissions-free
hydrogen and a rapid renewable shift are in focus.

   As the formal definition of green hydrogen is a fairly new concept, there is limited research exploring
its effects on the European energy system. Through our master's thesis, we aim to cover the research
gap of the impact of this recent definition of green hydrogen. With the recent hydrogen pilot auction,
we saw it as a valuable addition to the hydrogen research field to quantify the cost of green hydrogen
and its overall energy system impacts. Furthermore, with its ability to stabilize short- and long-term
energy markets, we see the potential of seasonal hydrogen storage as a major resource in the future of the
European energy system.

1.4 Structure of the thesis

   This thesis consists of two different research papers exploring the European energy system in the
context of green hydrogen. After this introduction, we present a broad problem description that presents
current gaps in research, why these gaps are of importance, and how we aim to address these. Chapter 3,
quantifies the cost of green hydrogen when implementing the definition of green hydrogen set by the
EU commission as well as analyzing overall energy system impacts. Following the paper, Chapter 4
reflects on the first paper and how we use this knowledge in the following paper. Chapter 5 extends
the analysis of green hydrogen by researching its potential in balancing seasonal variations in VRES
power production. After presenting the papers, Chapter 6 concludes the findings of our research before
presenting our suggestions for future research.

Chapter 2

Problem description

This chapter provides an overview of the thesis's scope and the methods used to approach it. It briefly
explains and situates the underlying optimization model within a broader context.

   The problem addressed in our thesis is that numerous factors, including costs, policies, and technolo-
gical characteristics, influence European energy investment decisions. To systematically understand these
interconnected decisions, it is essential to gain valuable insights into Europe's future energy landscape.
Energy system models that minimize total system costs are well-suited for this task. These models offer a
holistic view by considering different energy carriers, infrastructure, and end-use sectors. They identify
cost-effective strategies for achieving policy goals such as carbon reduction and increased renewable
energy use. Energy system modeling is a valuable tool for assisting decision-making as it systematically
evaluates the outcomes of future investment strategies and policies, surpassing the limitations of bounded
human rationality.

   Energy system models are constrained by computational limitations and the inability to predict future
conditions such as costs, technological advancements, demand, and weather. Therefore, their reliability
heavily depends on careful consideration of assumptions and modeling approaches. Integrating hydrogen
into these models further exacerbates these challenges, as hydrogen's potential as a large-scale energy
carrier has only recently gained attention. Hydrogen policies and the technical representation of hydro-
gen in energy system models are still evolving. This thesis addresses these challenges by identifying
shortcomings in current research that uses energy system models to investigate hydrogen. We focus on
two main areas of improvement: one policy-driven aspect and one technical modeling enhancement.

   Our research goes beyond theoretical modeling and is implemented into an existing energy system
model. In the following sections, we establish the starting point for our model, setting the context of
the European energy system for our study. We then outline the specific problems we aim to address and
detail our approach to solving them.

                                                              5
2.1 Energy system model

   Our work is based on the energy system model EMPIRE, as presented in Durakovic et al. (2024).
The EMPIRE model is well-suited for addressing our problem because it provides a robust baseline for
understanding future energy decisions. Notably, EMPIRE is a multi-horizon stochastic capacity expansion
model that incorporates multiple energy carriers, allowing for comprehensive analysis of diverse energy
pathways. This stochastic framework increases the reliability of the results by accounting for uncertainties
in future conditions, which is particularly important in the context of hydrogen. Additionally, EMPIRE
is open source, facilitating transparency and reproducibility, and is widely cited in the literature related
to hydrogen, adding credibility to our work.

2.2 Implementing European green hydrogen requirements

   As mentioned in Chapter 1, the European Commission has established a definition for green hydrogen,
which includes specific production requirements for hydrogen to qualify as green. If these production
requirements become mandatory within a European context, their implementation will influence which
technologies become prominent and which countries have favorable conditions for development. Current
energy system models are not fully equipped to address this problem due to a lack of detailed repres-
entations of specific green hydrogen requirements and their implications. In Chapter 3, we address the
policy problem of understanding how the definition of green hydrogen impacts European investments up
to 2048.

   We approach this problem by analyzing investment trends with and without production requirements
for green hydrogen. Our approach includes the following:

    · Detailed policy constraints set by the European Commission for defining green hydrogen, such as
       additionality, spatial correlation, and temporal correlation requirements.

    · Comparative cases, including a base case without green hydrogen requirements and cases where
       the impact of the individual policy elements is explored.

   Through a comparative analysis of different cases, we aim to provide valuable insights for policymakers
on future policy development and for industry stakeholders on the potential effects of current policies on
investment decisions and the overall cost of the future European energy system.

                                                              6
2.3 Enhancing multi-horizon modeling with seasonal energy storage

   Representing seasonal storage in energy system modeling is essential to capture the true value of
renewable energy sources. Energy storage balances seasonal variations in production and demand,
enabling renewable energy to replace current dispatchable energy sources. This is particularly important
to capture the potential value of green hydrogen, as its production depends on the variable output
of VRES. Current models struggle to address the complexities of seasonal energy storage. Properly
representing seasonal storage requires linking operations across representative periods to account for
storage transitions between seasons. It should also account for tactical uncertainties by representing
several potential scenarios, avoiding the assumption that energy storage investments and operations can
be managed with full foresight of future demand profiles and VRES availability. In Chapter 5, we
address the technical problem of representing seasonal energy storage in energy system modeling using
a stochastic multi-horizon framework.

   We approach this problem by introducing a new method to represent seasonal energy storage in
multi-horizon stochastic capacity expansion models. Our approach includes the following:

    · Linking operational periods through multi-stage decision problems, allowing energy storage transfer
       between seasons and accounting for tactical uncertainty through multiple stages.

    · Cases with and without seasonal storage to evaluate its impact on system performance and costs.
    · Cases with variations in hydrogen demand profiles to understand how demand uncertainty affects

       investment and operational decisions.
   The method is implemented in EMPIRE to investigate its impact on investment decisions and operations
in the European energy system.

                                                   Abstract

       The European Commission's new definition of green hydrogen provides clear guidelines and
       legal certainty for producers and consumers. However, the strict criteria for electrolysis pro-
       duction, requiring additionality, temporal correlation, and spatial correlation, could increase
       hydrogen costs, affecting its competitiveness as an energy carrier. This study examines the
       impact of these European regulations using a stochastic capacity expansion model for the
       European energy market up to 2048. We analyze how these requirements influence costs
       and investment decisions. Our results show that green hydrogen production requirements
       will raise system costs by =C82 billion from 2024 to 2048, driven mainly by a rapid transition
       from fossil fuels to renewable energy. The additionality requirement, which mandates the
       use of new renewable energy installations for electrolysis, emerges as the most expensive to
       comply with but also the most effective in accelerating the transition to renewable power,
       particularly before 2030.

Chapter 4

Reflections on the first paper

In this section, we reflect on the strengths and limitations of the paper presented in Chapter 3. The paper
analyzes the additional system costs and investment impacts associated with enforcing green hydrogen
production according to European standards. This analysis is conducted by enhancing the multi-horizon
stochastic energy system model EMPIRE to ensure that power supplied to electrolysis adheres to the
criteria of green hydrogen, namely additionality, spatial, and temporal correlation. As such, we critically
evaluate the chosen modeling frameworks, underlying assumptions, applied data, and the depth of the
analysis to identify areas for potential improvement.

4.1 Identifying research gaps

   The foundation of the research paper presented in Chapter 3 involved identifying gaps in the current
literature concerning hydrogen's role in the European energy system. Initially, we reviewed existing
literature to determine areas where our research could make a valuable contribution. Additionally, through
collaboration with our co-supervisors at Sintef Energy Research and Siemens Gamesa Renewable Energy
A/S, we explored industry needs to ensure our work is relevant and practical. This approach ensured that
our research would be impactful and strongly motivated our study.

   During this process, we identified a gap in the literature regarding the new definition of green hydrogen
in Europe. While the EU has set ambitious targets for green hydrogen as a future energy carrier, the
cost implications of the new policies supporting the economic viability of green hydrogen criteria have
not been adequately addressed. Furthermore, with the recent update to the European definition of green
hydrogen, research on the impacts of this new policy is lacking. Our study aimed to fill this gap by
examining the cost and investment implications of the recent changes in defining green hydrogen in
Europe, thereby linking new policies to practical applications.

                                                             35
4.2 Modeling decisions

   Our starting point for EMPIRE was the model version presented by Durakovic et al. (2024). One of the
model's strengths is its ability to generate endogenous hydrogen demand from industry, a feature rarely
seen in current research. This capability provides deeper insights into the driving factors for hydrogen
uptake, aligned with European ambitions. However, our study aimed to explore the additional costs
associated with requiring green hydrogen production to adhere to the EU definition and to compare these
costs across different requirements. Consequently, we enforced equal hydrogen production quantities
across all cases to compare cases on equal terms. The model retained the flexibility to determine how
the produced hydrogen is utilized, particularly in identifying which industrial processes can substitute
hydrogen for fossil fuels. However, enforcing predetermined production quantities introduced limitations,
as the system must adjust to these fixed hydrogen production levels. A more realistic approach would
allow hydrogen demand and supply to emerge endogenously as the most cost-effective energy carrier to
meet industrial demands and emission constraints.

   We exclusively represented hydrogen production from electrolysis, aligning with the EU definition
of green hydrogen. This ensures that all hydrogen is sourced from renewable energy, simplifying the
control and verification process. Our study aimed to examine the cost implications of green hydrogen
requirements within the European energy system, hence the focus on electrolysis. However, this approach
excludes other potential hydrogen production methods and does not address the broader debate about
incorporating diverse hydrogen sources in the European energy system. Our primary objective was
to quantify the impact of an energy system reliant solely on hydrogen production that meets the green
definition. While this focus aligns with current political strategies, it is somewhat narrow-sighted and may
overlook opportunities presented by other production methods. For instance, blue hydrogen produced
via steam methane reformers with CCS is an alternative method with low emissions. Previous research
using the EMPIRE model has shown that blue hydrogen is notably more competitive than green hydrogen
under current cost and technology assumptions (Durakovic et al., 2023). Consequently, excluding blue
hydrogen from our model could lead to higher hydrogen production costs and fail to consider the interplay
between different production methods.

   We included the option to invest in new hydrogen pipelines and repurpose natural gas pipelines for the
hydrogen transmission network. Extending the EMPIRE model to encompass the repurposing of natural
gas pipelines was necessary, as the European hydrogen backbone envisions this as a key component of
the future hydrogen network. Repurposing natural gas pipelines is essential for accurately identifying the
cost implications of adhering to the European definition of green hydrogen. This definition reduces the

                                                             36
flexibility of hydrogen production, thereby increasing the need for a more extensive hydrogen pipeline
network. Without this representation, the cost increase associated with the new green hydrogen definition
might appear exaggerated. However, introducing repurposing increases the model's complexity and
memory usage, which is already a limiting factor. Therefore, adding this feature comes at the expense
of limiting options for other extensions. Additionally, the feasibility of repurposing natural gas pipelines
is still under investigation, with several uncertainties related to costs and risks. Including repurposing in
the model introduces drawbacks, as it is highly uncertain whether this can be achieved cost-efficiently
and safely.

4.3 Practical experience when solving

   In our first research paper, we extensively studied the EMPIRE model and its numerous extensions
documented in various papers. As EMPIRE has progressed from a power system model to a multi-carrier
energy system model, the computational complexity has also increased.

   Our research identified a critical limitation of the EMPIRE model: the substantial memory capacity
required to solve it rather than the time needed for computation. Although EMPIRE is a linear model and
its computational time scales efficiently with increased complexity, the memory usage approaches the
maximum capacity that current technology can handle. This constraint necessitates careful consideration
of which modeling features are essential for a given study, as further extensions might lead to unmanage-
able memory requirements. The version of EMPIRE discussed in the first paper demanded over 300GB
of memory. A key factor contributing to this high memory requirement is that EMPIRE operates as a
stochastic program. The need to represent multiple future scenarios inherently increases memory usage.

   By highlighting these challenges, our work underscores the importance of optimizing the model's
structure and selectively incorporating features to balance complexity and computational feasibility.

4.4 Identified potentials for future research

   Through our work, we identified opportunities for future research to extend the work presented in
Chapter 3. We present three potentials that we see as most critical:

                                                             37
4.4.1 Off-grid electrolysis:

Both on-grid and off-grid installations for electrolysis are being considered for future investments. Off-
grid installations offer several benefits, such as avoiding grid infrastructure expansions and grid fees,
especially in areas where the power grid is unavailable, weak, or saturated with VRES (Author or
Organization, 2023). Several studies have investigated the costs of off-grid installations because of the
certainty that the power is from renewable sources and the advantage of comparing costs of complete
hydrogen production systems (Gutiérrez-Martín et al., 2021; Janssen et al., 2022).

   Including off-grid electrolysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding of green hydrogen
production's economic and technical viability, helping to identify the most cost-effective and sustainable
strategies for hydrogen production.

4.4.2 Feasibility of Spain as a dominant green hydrogen producer:

The findings presented in Chapter 3 indicate that Spain is to become a leading producer of green
hydrogen in the future. However, assessing the feasibility of such extensive production levels in Spain
and conducting a sensitivity analysis on the factors contributing to Spain's cost-efficiency in green
hydrogen production is essential.

   The current model has several limitations regarding Spain's high hydrogen production. It assumes
electrolysis will rely primarily on solar power, necessitating pronounced land for solar plant expansion.
This assumption may be unrealistic due to limited land availability. Although the model limits solar
capacity, it does not consider the competition for land with other VRES generators, such as onshore
wind. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the land available for VRES generators, accounting for their
interdependency. Additionally, the model should improve spatial resolution to capture region-specific
weather data, as it currently assumes uniform weather conditions across Spain.

   Another area for future research is the potential limitation of freshwater availability. Electrolysis re-
quires freshwater as a feedstock, necessitating a sufficient supply. With recent record-high temperatures
in Southern Europe, Spain faces increased water scarcity (Chaundler, 2023). Extensive hydrogen produc-
tion may not be feasible if freshwater availability is insufficient. A potential solution is the desalination
of seawater. Future research should explore freshwater availability and include the potential for seawater
desalination.

   Furthermore, Spain's dominant hydrogen production necessitates hydrogen transmission to other re-
gions. To ensure the feasibility of transmission dependencies, a reliability check should be conducted.

                                                             38
The hydrogen network must be reliable during component failures, adhering to criteria similar to those
for power and natural gas networks. Additionally, the current model does not limit the distance hydrogen
can be transmitted within an hour, only the transmission capacities. This means hydrogen produced in
Spain could unrealistically be transmitted to distant locations within the same hour, potentially overstating
Spain's competitive advantage in hydrogen production.

   EMPIRE is currently a model with European scope. However, future studies could restructure EMPIRE
to only capture Spain and potentially neighboring countries. This way, it is possible to increase the spatial
resolution of Spain without surpassing the memory limitations mentioned in Section 4.3.

4.4.3 Seasonal energy storage:

The current version of EMPIRE does not support seasonal energy storage due to its model structure,
which treats representative periods for each season independently. Initially, EMPIRE was developed as a
power system model, and seasonal storage was not considered a crucial feature. This decision was based
on the limited recognition of seasonal power storage as an effective energy storage method. However,
as EMPIRE has evolved into a multi-carrier energy system model with sector coupling, the need for
seasonal storage capabilities has become increasingly important. Despite this growing importance, the
development of this feature has not been prioritized within the EMPIRE model.

   This limitation is particularly important for hydrogen modeling. Green hydrogen production is directly
tied to the output of VRES, causing its production potential to fluctuate with VRES generation. This
creates a critical issue: hydrogen, intended to serve as a dispatchable energy source to replace fossil
fuels, will face production deficits during periods of low VRES output. Therefore, the current inability
of EMPIRE to support hydrogen storage across seasons poses a substantial drawback. Without the ability
to store hydrogen produced during periods of excess VRES generation for use during shortages, the
reliability and effectiveness of hydrogen as a dispatchable energy source are severely compromised.

   However, incorporating seasonal storage in the multi-horizon stochastic framework of EMPIRE intro-
duces challenges. As described in Section 4.3, the memory usage of EMPIRE limits the possibilities of
comprehensive extensions. Therefore, introducing seasonal storage in EMPIRE requires consideration
of the model structure and selecting model features to maintain manageable model complexity.

                                                             39
4.5 Introducing the next paper

   Upon seeing the heavy reliance on solar energy for hydrogen production, it is evident that seasonal
variations in VRES production will impact an energy system where hydrogen plays a crucial role in
decarbonizing Europe. As highlighted in previous research, the impact of seasonal imbalances in an
energy system with high shares of VRES should not be underestimated (Gabrielli et al., 2020). Therefore,
we believe that modeling seasonal storage will offer valuable insights into the discussion surrounding the
influence of green hydrogen on the European energy system.

   As mentioned in Section 4.3, the memory requirements for solving the EMPIRE model is a limiting
factor. As a result, we had to critically evaluate methods to reduce the model's complexity, enabling
us to integrate seasonal energy storage. Based on the findings of Hordvei et al. (2024), we determined
which features had the least impact on the overall model results. The following paragraphs present our
alterations to the model to reduce memory usage.

   We observed that EMPIRE initially modeled 30 generators, where 16 generated less than 1% of energy
in the model, thus considered redundant. Generators take up considerable memory space by creating new
decision variables for every hour and generator. Additionally, ramping generators have dependent decision
variables, demanding a considerably greater number of constraints. Most of the removed generators were
specific types of fossil generators with small variations in efficiency and operations. Therefore, we
believed that their removal had little impact on the system.

   The problem size will increase rapidly with the number of nodes, not only due to the numerous
investment and operational decisions required for each node but also because the number of arcs in the
model grows non-linearly. We reduced the number of nodes from 51 to 20. All offshore nodes were
merged into their respective countries. Furthermore, groups of countries with low energy demand or
close geographic centers were merged.

   Although investments in the CCS supply chain are an interesting feature of the model, this was replaced
using future variable CCS cost data instead. Similarly, the gas network was replaced by modeling natural
gas prices, as we did not focus on the interplay between blue and green hydrogen and the effects of natural
gas capacity limits.

   In our thesis, we do not aim to determine endogenous hydrogen quantities. To ensure comparability
across cases, we used consistent hydrogen demand quantities. Thus, we decided to remove endogenous
industrial demand and replace it with hourly hydrogen demand similar to the power load, with data
sourced from gas demand profiles and future annual hydrogen demand forecasts.

                                                   Abstract

          Hydrogen can be crucial for the European net-zero energy transition by 2050. Seasonal
       energy storage is important to balance energy production and demand fluctuations. Uncer-
       tainty in short-term and tactical time horizons can have a notable impact on energy system
       planning with high penetration of renewable energy and seasonal energy storage. To model
       the multi-timescale uncertainty, we introduce tactical stages in stochastic multi-horizon pro-
       gramming. In each investment period, the operational problem with tactical uncertainty is
       a multi-stage stochastic program. We implement this modeling approach in the EMPIRE
       model to analyze the impact of seasonable hydrogen storage on long-term energy system
       infrastructure planning. The results show that: (1) seasonal storage reduces total system
       costs by balancing short- and long-term variations in renewable generation, (2) seasonal
       storage decreases the need for dispatchable power sources and hydrogen pipelines, (3) hydro-
       gen demand profile has a notable impact on infrastructure investments, in particular power
       generators and hydrogen storage.

Due to the high temporal resolution and stochastic
complexity of the model addition, we reduced the
spatial resolution to maintain tractable complex-
ity. The model covers 20 nodes representing coun-
tries and regions in Europe. Smaller countries
with relatively close centers, such as the Benelux
and Baltic states, are aggregated for this reason.
The Balkan countries Bulgaria, North Macedo-
nia, Serbia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and
Slovenia are aggregated due to low impact on hy-
drogen demand and production, as well as rel-
atively similar stochastic profiles. For the same
reason, Switzerland and Austria were aggregated,
and the Czech Republic and Slovakia were aggreg-
ated. Ireland, the United Kingdom (UK), Portugal,
and Spain were kept separate despite relatively
short geographical distances between centers, as
these countries had notable different production
profiles, in addition to high impact on the model
due to hydrogen and power production quantities.
Aggregation of nodes implies summing up total de-
mands and asset capacities and selecting stochastic
profiles from the country with the highest power
load to represent them rather than averaging them
to keep realistic profiles.

Chapter 6

Concluding remarks

The purpose of this master's thesis has been to research the impact of hydrogen on the European energy
system. With hydrogen proposed as an energy source for hard-to-abate sectors, its potential consumption
for future European industry is substantial.

   The first paper investigates the impact of green hydrogen on energy systems by modeling the EU's new
definition of green hydrogen in the linear optimization model EMPIRE. Our studies reveal the following.
(1) Enforcing green hydrogen production in the EU will incur higher system costs, particularly in power
generation, as more VRES are needed to cover the demand. Following our modeling assumptions, this
will incur a cost increase of =C82 billion by 2048. This shows that with the new EU policies, the definition
of green hydrogen in future energy system models must be considered, as system cost differences increase
considerably once it is implemented. Compared to current EU subsidy quantities, it shows that with the
given quantity of hydrogen production in the model, current planned subsidy programs will not suffice
to make hydrogen competitive. (2) Production will be concentrated in countries that benefit from the
requirements of green hydrogen and will export their surplus production to industrial centers in Europe.
As the only country currently achieving over 90% renewable energy on its power grid, our model indicates
that Norway will invest in fossil fuel generators and produce just enough fossil power to remain below its
permitted limit. While it is not up to us to discuss the realism of this strategy, implementing this exemption
should be considered and possibly strengthened by banning the use of fossil fuels for hydrogen production
in 90 percent of the zones. (3) The impact of each requirement varies differently, with spatial and temporal
correlation having the lowest impact on cost. Given the already notable presence of renewable energy
in Europe, meeting the additionality requirement will become the most costly constraint, as the existing
renewable capacity cannot be utilized. It should be noted that the modeled hydrogen production quantities
before 2030 exceed the EU's current ambitions, demanding a more rapid shift than current ambitions.

   The second paper investigates how tactical uncertainty can be modeled in EMPIRE to optimize

                                                             69
seasonal storage and energy infrastructure investments under different hydrogen demand profiles. Our
studies reveal the following. (1) Allowing seasonal storage will consistently reduce system costs. Cheaper,
variable power sources, combined with energy storage, can be used for seasonal balancing of production
and demand, reducing the need for more costly dispatchable power. The resulting increase in temporal
flexibility reduces the need for fossil fuels and hydrogen pipelines. (2) Leveraging seasonal storage adds
substantial value to VRES while simultaneously assisting in reducing overall climate mitigation costs.
Despite high ambitions in offshore hydrogen production, our results show that solar-based hydrogen will
dominate the production mix. Spain, in particular, will become the major producer of green hydrogen due
to highly favorable weather conditions. (3) Hydrogen demand profiles will have a considerable system
impact, with total system costs varying up to 7.7% between different cases. This highlights the importance
of considering the uncertainty in hydrogen demand profiles in a market yet to exist. (4) Stochastic
programming loses its strength when connecting storage across representative weeks due to perfect
information about future conditions. We show that modeling multi-stage problems within investment
periods is an effective approach to preserving stochasticity while enabling the flow of information between
representative weeks.

   These papers highlight the interplay between green hydrogen, seasonal storage, and VRES. From
Chapter 3, we learned that introducing the EU green hydrogen requirements will require substantial
investments in new renewable energy generators. As variability is inherent to VRES, their rapid increase
poses considerable challenges in balancing energy supply and demand, especially over seasonal time
horizons. Implementation of seasonal storage compliments this increase, as it balances the differences
by storing vast amounts of energy between surplus seasons and deficit seasons. This strategy is followed
regardless of the hydrogen demand profile. This illustrates that the deployment of green hydrogen brings
attention to the critical role of seasonal storage, suggesting that both should be central to future research
efforts.

6.1 Future research

   Working on this thesis has given us insights into various aspects of representing energy systems in
optimization models. While our studies have comprehensively covered aspects of green hydrogen and
seasonal storage, many considerations remain within these topics that have yet to be explored.

   Due to the size of our problem, the initial multi-horizon structure of EMPIRE was restructured to a
rolling-horizon problem. Making investments for the entire horizon of the model is a great strength of
the previous model, as there are variations in the cost of fossil, CO2 cap, and lifetime of investments that

                                                             70
would otherwise be overlooked. Future work should aim to reduce the current problem complexity to
take advantage of this property.

   Future studies should look at the possibility of increasing spatial resolution. Firstly, the requirements
for on-grid green hydrogen are defined in bidding areas, with a spatial resolution slightly higher than
the one presented in Chapter 3. Following the price zones defined by Nord Pool would more accurately
reflect the spatial correlation requirement of green hydrogen. Secondly, local conditions for VRES
availability may vary within regions. Increasing the spatial resolution would more accurately capture
weather conditions and could potentially impact results.

   Although it is beyond the scope of this paper, the model presented in Chapter 5 could prove useful in
continuing research on the competitiveness of green hydrogen in cases where blue hydrogen production
is possible. With a reduced model, it should be considered to model a natural gas network in parallel
with the existing model and analyze the interplay between green and blue hydrogen.
