# norland-2025: literature review paragraphs (22)

### 6 Related literature review  (2 paragraphs)

[P01] section 6, pos 1/2
This section reviews relevant research to provide a comprehensive overview of how uncertainty has been addressed in industrial flexibility problems aiming for cost-optimal investments, in line with the research question of this thesis. The main objective is to identify the potential highlighted in the literature and assess the methodological approaches and perspectives through which it has been explored or not. Focus areas include modeling of electricity markets under uncertainty, particularly in a multi-market context, as well as industrial flexibility, energy storage, demand-side management, and decarbonization through capacity investments.

[P02] section 6, pos 2/2
The review emphasizes the relevance and limitations of existing models and insights, and how these align with or diverge from the scope of this thesis. The chapter concludes by identifying gaps in the literature and outlining the specific contributions of this work.

### 6.1 Multi-market participation under uncertainty  (5 paragraphs)

[P03] section 6.1, pos 1/5
Research on multi-market participation in power trading often differs between coordinated bidding and separate bidding. In coordinated bidding, the outcomes of earlier markets influence bidding strategies in subsequent ones, whereas separate bidding treats each market independently. Klæboe and Fosso (2013) review studies and compare these approaches in multi-market operation, highlighting market conditions and competition as important factors. Their findings indicate modest gains (0.1–2%) from coordinated bidding across day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets. Löhndorf and Wozabal (2023) applies a multistage stochastic program in a coordinated bidding setting in Germany, revealing substantial gains for storage systems — particularly for batteries (8.3–8.5%) and pumped hydro storage (10–29.1%). However, they do not consider reserve market participation.

[P04] section 6.1, pos 2/5
Several research papers, on the other hand, incorporate reserve markets into a coordinated bidding framework. Boomsma et al. (2014), Ottesen et al. (2018), Böhringer et al. (2019), Bohlayer et al. (2020), and Paredes et al. (2024) include both day-ahead and reserve markets, combined with participation in different reserve capacity and activation markets, consistently demonstrating improved profitability across various experimental setups. Others extend this to include intraday markets, offering a more complete representation of multi-market participation. Han et al. (2017) includes day-ahead, intraday, and reserve market participation from an aggregator perspective, while Silva et al. (2022), Kraft et al. (2023), and Nolzen et al. (2022) explore the same markets from the view of power plant portfolios, utilizing multi-energy systems. These studies collectively highlight the complementary value of including both day-ahead, intraday, and reserve market participation, yet none assess how such multi-market involvement influences long-term capacity expansion decisions.

[P05] section 6.1, pos 3/5
Miskiw et al. (2025) builds on this by conducting a study on the effects of price-making, in contrast to price-taking in coordinated bidding in sequential electricity markets. The paper also compares a myopic (separate) approach to a coordinated approach, finding an overall relative gain of 18%, with higher relative gains in the winter season. The paper does highlight that ignoring the price impact might overestimate coordinated benefits. However, the study does not address long-term strategic investment decisions.

[P06] section 6.1, pos 4/5
Although coordinated bidding captures realistic information flows and inter-market dependencies, it also increases the computational complexity when uncertainty spans multiple markets. To manage this, several studies apply scenario reduction techniques. Boomsma et al. (2014) uses iterative sampling and stage-wise clustering, while Kraft et al. (2023) and Silva et al. (2022) apply k -means and k -medoids clustering. These techniques help retain stochastic detail while keeping the model computationally solvable.

[P07] section 6.1, pos 5/5
However, in more complex multi-market models, scenario reduction alone is often insufficient to ensure tractability. As a result, many studies simplify the stochastic structure by collapsing real-time operations into a single stage, effectively optimizing with full foresight. Unlike Boomsma et al. (2014), who solves all problems in one large multi-stage structure, others take a more aggregated approach. For instance, Ottesen et al. (2018) reduce 168 hourly stages to seven daily. Although Ottesen et al. (2018) acknowledges this as a simplification, they argue it is reasonable given the overall focus on first-stage reserve market decisions.

### 6.2 Industrial flexibility, storage and DSM  (9 paragraphs)

[P08] section 6.2, pos 1/9
Research on industrial flexibility, storage, and DSM offers valuable insights into how industry can adapt to dynamic energy systems. Mathiesen and Lund (2009) highlight large-scale heat pumps, flexible demand, and electric boilers with Thermal Energy Storage (TES) as promising for renewable energy source integration. Beck et al. (2021) emphasize the potential of power-to-heat with TES to leverage price fluctuations, while Berg et al. (2024) find TES more cost-effective than BESS for industrial energy communities, underscoring the value of shared infrastructure. Elio et al. (2021) provide a broader comparison of storage options, identifying BESS, Pumped Hydro Energy Storage (PHES), and Compressed Air Energy Storage (CAES) as versatile for DSM, though optimal solutions depend on sitespecific conditions. However, these studies do not consider an integrated energy system from a single-industry perspective.

[P09] section 6.2, pos 2/9
Industrial flexibility has emerged as a key enabler for accelerating the decarbonization of heavy industries, particularly in the context of increasing electrification. Foslie et al. (2024) demonstrate that fuel switching, load shedding, and load shifting can alleviate grid congestion and accelerate the electrification of energy-intensive processes. They emphasize the sector's complexity and high electricity demand, requiring both direct and indirect electrification, alongside carbon capture. The study highlights the industry's potential to support the grid due to its large energy share and shows that sector coupling can enhance flexibility and reduce peak load. Flexibility costs are further estimated at 111– 210 EUR/MWh, which is found to be competitive with grid expansion, but with shorter lead times and reduced carbon emissions.

[P10] section 6.2, pos 3/9
Building on this, Richstein and Hosseinioun (2020) investigate how electricity tariffs influence industrial flexibility. They find that peak demand charges, aimed at reflecting grid costs, may discourage DSM by penalizing increased electricity imports. Moreover, high grid tariffs are found to reduce incentives to invest in flexibility technologies like storage and capacity expansion. Foslie et al. (2023) supports this by analyzing electrification strategies for chemical processes, confirming the technical value of flexibility in VRES-dominated systems. They further highlight that the economic viability depends on supportive policies, such as financial incentives. However, they do not consider uncertainty.

[P11] section 6.2, pos 4/9
Together, these studies point at unresolved challenges, particularly the lack of detailed assessments comparing the cost-effectiveness of flexibility measures relative to grid expansion and connection charges.

[P12] section 6.2, pos 5/9
Additionally, while Foslie et al. (2023) emphasizes the value of flexibility, the study overlooks potential revenue from reserve market participation. However, such participation is highly dependent on the duration and response time of flexible assets, making applicability highly technology- and case-specific. Javanshir et al. (2023) compares heat pumps and electric boilers for reserve provision, showing that electric boilers, with no ramping restrictions, are better suited for fast-response markets, while heat pumps should run on partial-to-full load rather than being shut down entirely and restarted. This introduces a modeling trade-off: whether technologies should be explicitly assigned to specific markets based on technical suitability.

[P13] section 6.2, pos 6/9
Manna et al. (2023) further extends this by analyzing industrial flexibility through combined energy storage and reserve market participation, using a MILP model with uncertainty. They find that storage increases profit by optimizing energy schedules and enabling reserve market participation. However, the model excludes intraday market participation and investment decisions.

[P14] section 6.2, pos 7/9
Several studies highlighted the technical and economic potential of DSM in both large and small-scale industries, particularly through load shifting and peak shaving. Golmohamadi (2022) highlights significant DSM potential in energy-intensive sectors like cement and aluminum, while Choobineh and Mohagheghi (2015) demonstrates cost savings for smaller industries by integrating on-site RES and BESS. Further, Duarte et al. (2020) incorporates uncertainty, demonstrating a significant potential in combining DSM, RES, and BESS.

[P15] section 6.2, pos 8/9
Paulus and Borggrefe (2011) assess DSM potential in various industries with reserve market participation in Germany, showing strong technical and economic promise, particularly for energy-intensive sectors. They highlight the role of DSM in balancing VRES with load shifting and shedding as key strategies. Due to high utilization rates, industries typically offer more up- than down-regulation, and shifting is preferred over shedding due to lower opportunity costs. Reserve market activations therefore tend to focus on processes where load can be shifted without major economic loss, as industries are likely to price activation at or above their marginal costs. Helin et al. (2017) extend this with a bottom-up analysis of the pulp and paper industry, where reserve activations are based on marginal cost, with intraday market compensation for lost loads. While the study finds significant potential, it notes barriers like high marginal costs and risk premiums. However, the study focuses solely on DSM and does not consider investments in other flexible resources.

[P16] section 6.2, pos 9/9
Nebel et al. (2020) compare DSM from aluminum electrolysis with other flexibility options (BESS, TES, electric vehicle charging, and hydrogen storage) using a dispatch model of the German energy system. They highlight the value of combining flexibilities, which reduces system costs and curtailment as VRES increases. Electromobility, power-to-heat, and power-to-gas show the greatest impact by 2050. However, the study focuses on aggregate effects and does not assess benefits per unit of installed capacity. Bezold et al. (2024) complement this by comparing industrial flexibility revenues across German reserve markets, finding higher returns in aFRR than mFRR, relatively stable bidding values over time, and that higher bids are generally safer than lower bids. Yet, both studies overlook uncertainty and long-term capacity investment decisions.

### 6.3 Capacity expansion investments and decarbonization pathway  (4 paragraphs)

[P17] section 6.3, pos 1/4
Most research regarding capacity expansion and decarbonization pathways focuses on either local microgrids or national/global systems, with limited attention to industryspecific flexibility and its benefits in mitigating price volatility and emission costs. Levin et al. (2023) emphasizes the importance of energy storage as the share of VRES increases, pointing to overlooked arbitrage opportunities for actors with certain flexibility. The study argues that current models underestimate storage value under volatile prices and higher carbon costs and calls for integrating taxes and emissions into future capacity expansion planning. However, it remains unclear how different industrial sectors will respond, raising questions about long-term investment strategies in flexibility and storage beyond shortterm market signals.

[P18] section 6.3, pos 2/4
Building on this, Haugen et al. (2024) discuss how the increasing share of VRES increases price volatility, directly affecting investment decisions. They highlight the need for longterm planning horizons, in order to capture different market conditions, and argue for modeling storage with degradation, technical constraints, and multi-market participation to accurately provide investment opportunities and limitations. While Haugen et al. (2024) criticizes existing models for their failure to adequately account for uncertainty, particularly in price formation, flexibility, and reserve market participation, it does not propose a solution or method itself. It concludes that future models must include uncertainty in supply, demand, and costs, along with sub-hourly resolution to reflect VRES variability. Similarly, Oree et al. (2017) highlights a shift from cost minimization to multi-objective frameworks that balance cost, emissions, and reliability, emphasizing the need to incorporate variability, uncertainty, and operational flexibility when evaluating future capacity expansion investments. While these studies offer valuable insights into key modeling considerations, they do not propose a methodological framework of their own.

[P19] section 6.3, pos 3/4
Several energy system models have been developed to assess capacity expansion and decarbonization pathways, each with distinct strengths and limitations. As detailed in the project report (Skare et al., 2024), these models vary in their ability to capture uncertainty, flexibility, and different market interactions. While EMPIRE, OSeMOSYS, GENeSYS- MOD, and TIMES provide valuable insights into long-term capacity expansion, they primarily focus on system-wide optimization and often lack the granularity needed to evaluate a single industry's perspective.

[P20] section 6.3, pos 4/4
Given the increasing role of VRES and the need for flexible on-site solutions, as emphasized by Levin et al. (2023), Haugen et al. (2024), and Oree et al. (2017), EnergyModelsX (EMX) is identified as suitable, as it enables detailed modeling of on-site generation, energy storage and technology investments, and flexible loads on-site, with the potential to incorporate stochasticity. The main limitation of EMX is the absence of reserve market participation.

### 6.4 Our contribution  (2 paragraphs)

[P21] section 6.4, pos 1/2
While several studies individually address industrial flexibility, energy storage, DSM, multi-market participation, and decarbonization pathways, few integrate these elements into a unified framework under uncertainty. This thesis fills that gap by developing a multi-stage stochastic programming framework that integrates coordinated bidding across day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets with capacity expansion and operational flexibility, from the perspective of a single industrial actor. The model captures industry-specific demand, on-site generation, multi-market participation, and investment decisions.
