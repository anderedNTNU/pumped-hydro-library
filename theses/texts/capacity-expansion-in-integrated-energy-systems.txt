Abstract

Integrated industrial energy systems are increasingly exposed to volatile electricity prices,
stricter emission regulations, and evolving market opportunities. To remain competitive
under such uncertainty, industrial actors must evaluate strategic long-term investments in
flexible assets that also enable active participation in energy markets beyond their core
operations. To assess whether such investment decisions can improve future profitability,
this thesis develops a multi-stage stochastic optimization model that integrates capacity
expansion, load shifting, energy storage, and multi-market participation with coordinated
bidding across the day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets.

The primary research question investigates how different representations of uncertainty
in prices, operations, and market dynamics affect cost-optimal investment decisions in
integrated energy systems operating across multi-markets. The study also evaluates the
added value of using an advanced optimization framework to support reliable decision-
making under uncertainty. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis is conducted to examine
how future changes in carbon and technology prices impact investment strategies.

To address the research question, a case study is performed for two industrial sectors --
pulp and paper, and aluminum -- highlighting how sector-specific characteristics influence
the investment strategies and the value of modeling uncertainty. Several uncertainty
representations and scenario generation routines are tested to evaluate trade-offs between
instance complexity, solution quality, and computational tractability.

Results show that explicitly modeling uncertainty through stochastic programming can
reduce expected system costs by up to 35%, significantly outperforming deterministic
and rule-of-thumb approaches. Flexibility options, particularly energy storage and reserve
market participation, consistently prove cost-effective, underscoring financial incentives for
providing ancillary services, though the benefits remain highly site-specific. Small scenario
trees (100 scenarios) often fail to capture system variability and risk underinvestment,
unless paired with robust scenario generation. In contrast, larger trees (500­1000 sce-
narios) with systematic sampling prove solution stability and investment accuracy.

The sensitivity analysis highlights how expected changes in carbon costs and technology
prices impact the dispatch order. It further confirms the cost-effectiveness of fuel switching
and the competitiveness of natural gas with CCS in a decarbonized energy system.

Future research should incorporate more technical bottom-up modeling of key system com-
ponents to better capture the impact of operational constraints on investment decisions.
Furthermore, future market trajectories reflecting market reforms, such as quarter-hourly
resolution, tariff structures, and regulatory changes, should be analyzed in light of in-
vestment implications. To ensure broader applicability, the features of reserve market
participation should be incorporated into an open-source modeling framework, such as
EnergyModelsX.

Sammendrag

Integrerte industrielle energisystemer st°ar overfor økende prisvolatilitet, strengere utslipp-
skrav og nye markedsmuligheter. For at industriaktører skal forbli konkurransedyktige
under fremtidig usikkerhet, m°a de vurdere langsiktige, strategiske investeringer i fleksi-
ble ressurser som ogs°a muliggjør deltakelse i flere markeder utover daglig drift. For °a
undersøke hvorvidt slike investeringsbeslutninger kan bidra til fremtidig lønnsomhet, er
det i denne oppgaven utviklet en flerstegs stokastisk optimeringsmodell som inkluderer
kapasitetsutvidelse, lastforskyvning, energilagring og deltakelse i flere markeder, med ko-
ordinert budgivning i day-ahead-, intradag- og reservemarkeder.

Det overordnede forskningsspørsm°alet er hvordan ulike representasjoner av usikkerhet i
priser, drift og markedsdynamikk p°avirker den kostnadsoptimale investeringsbeslutningen.
Studien vurderer ogs°a verdien av °a benytte avansert optimeringsmodellering.

Videre gjennomføres en sensitivitetsanalyse for °a undersøke hvordan fremtidig usikkerhet
i karbon- og teknologipriser p°avirker den optimale investeringsstrategien. For °a besvare
forskningsspørsm°alene gjennomføres en case-studie for to industrisektorer -- papir og alu-
minium -- som belyser hvordan sektorspesifikke egenskaper p°avirker investeringsstrate-
gier, samt verdien av °a modellere usikkerhet. Flere ulike varianter av usikkerhetsrepresen-
tasjon og scenariogenerering testes for °a vurdere avveiningen mellom instanskompleksitet,
løsningskvalitet og løsningstid.

Resultatene viser tydelig at modellering av usikkerhet kan redusere forventede systemkost-
nader med opptil 35% sammenlignet med deterministiske og forenklede tilnærminger.
Fleksibilitet, særlig energilagring i samspill med reservemarkedsdeltakelse, viser seg gjen-
nomg°aende lønnsomt og understreker dermed de økonomiske insentivene for deltakelse,
selv om lønnsomheten er avhengig av industrispesifikke forhold. Sm°a scenariotrær (100
scenarier) viser seg °a være utilstrekkelig for °a fange usikkerheten godt nok, noe som
typisk fører til underinvesteringer med mindre de kombineres med m°alrettede scenari-
ogenereringsrutiner. Større trær (500­1000 scenarier) gir derimot bedre investerings-
beslutninger og økt stabilitet.

Sensitivitetsanalysen viser at forventede endringer i karbonkostnader og teknologipriser
p°avirker rekkefølgen ulike teknologier prioriteres. Den bekrefter ogs°a kostnadseffektiviteten
ved °a skifte mellom energibærere, samt konkurranseevnen til naturgass med karbonfangst
og -lagring i et energisystem preget av høyere karbonpriser.

Fremtidig forskning bør inkludere mer teknisk detaljert modellering av sentrale systemkom-
ponenter for bedre °a kunne vurdere effekten av operasjonelle begrensninger i lys av in-
vesteringsbeslutninger. I tillegg bør fremtidige markedsutviklinger, som kvartersoppløsning,
ulike nettleiestrukturer og regulatoriske endringer, analyseres med hensyn til invester-
ingsimplikasjoner. For °a sette funnene fra denne oppgaven i en større sammenheng, bør
videre arbeid ogs°a inkludere reservemarkedsdeltakelse i et °apent modelleringsrammeverk,
som for eksempel EnergyModelsX.

1 Introduction

The energy sector is undergoing a major transformation driven by the global commitment
to achieve net-zero emissions by 2050. Historically, the energy system is characterized by
centralized, dispatchable generation and relatively stable demands. However, the rapid
deployment of Variable Renewable Energy Sources (VRES), electrification, and stricter
regulations present significant challenges for industrial prosumers, who must navigate in-
creasingly complex market conditions characterized by uncertain and volatile electricity
prices and rising carbon costs. Consequently, robust long-term investment decisions now
require careful consideration of both short-term profitability and long-term uncertainty
in future market trajectories. Robust decision-support tools are therefore essential to
maintain competitiveness with increasingly complex and uncertain energy systems.

Operations research has long supported industrial optimization in areas such as production
planning, network flow optimization, and inventory management (Hillier and Lieberman,
2015). In recent years, optimization of energy consumption and recovery has gained signif-
icant attention. Decarbonization policies and the introduction of emission quotas covering
nearly 90% of industrial activity (Miljødirektoratet, 2023) have increased the importance
of strategic investment planning and operational flexibility, driven by both cost-saving
opportunities and the need to comply with future regulations.

In the Nordic region, the growing share of VRES is projected to supply more than 50%
of electricity generation in 2050 (see Figure 1), while electricity demand is expected to
increase by 80% (Statnett, 2025b). Although Norway continues to derive most of its
electricity from dispatchable renewable generation, the growing share of VRES and rising
electricity demand introduces new challenges for grid stability. Since network expansion
is unlikely to keep pace with demand growth, emerging grid bottlenecks emphasize the
value of flexible consumption.

Given the complexity of electricity markets, future regulatory dynamics and prices, the
industrial sector must evaluate how investments in flexible assets and participation across
multiple markets (e.g., day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets) can improve future prof-
itability. A range of optimization models, both stochastic and deterministic, have exam-
ined related problems at both system-wide and individual levels. Despite the growing
literature on energy optimization, to the authors' knowledge, no research has developed
a stochastic optimization framework that simultaneously evaluates industrial investment
decisions, Demand-Side Management (DSM), and explicit uncertainty across day-ahead,
intraday, and reserve markets.

The central hypothesis is that investment decisions in flexible assets become more robust
when incorporating uncertainty, and more economically viable by exploiting flexibility
and participation in reserve markets. This hypothesis is examined by embedding price
and site-specific uncertainty, along with market dynamics, into a stochastic optimization
model that addresses the following problem statement:

How do alternative representations of uncertainty under a multi-market ap-
proach affect cost-optimal investment decisions in an integrated energy sys-
tem?

Specifically, the analysis investigates the following research questions:

   1. How does the representation of uncertainty affect the quality and robustness of

2 Background

2.1 Introduction to the traditional electricity system

This section provides a brief introduction to the electricity system, starting with an
overview of its structure, highlighting the roles and responsibilities of various actors at
different levels of the grid. Next, the flow of information and financial transactions be-
tween key entities such as Balancing Service Provider (BSP), Balancing Responsible Par-
ties (BRP), Distribution System Operator (DSO), and the Transmission System Operator
(TSO) will be presented. Although the Norwegian power system is used as an example,
the principles are valid for many other countries, with certain adjustments. The terms
electricity market and power market will be used interchangeably throughout this thesis.

2.1.1 The Norwegian electricity system

The Norwegian power grid operates as a natural monopoly regulated by the Norwegian
Energy Regulatory Authority (RME), and is divided into three main levels (RME, 2025).
The transmission grid, operated by Statnett -- the national TSO -- handles high-voltage
electricity, typically at 300 or 420 kV. Further, the voltage level is gradually reduced to
lower voltage levels, through regional and local distribution grids, managed by around 130
DSOs (NVE, 2018). Figure 3 presents a simplified illustration of the power system, where
the regional and local distribution grid is visualized as Distribution grid and consumer
demand is represented by S.

Figure 3: Simplified illustration of the electrical power system. Inspired by Ottesen (2017).

The electricity system in Norway is predominantly renewable, with 98% of its energy de-
rived from renewable sources. Of this, hydropower accounts for 88% of the total production
capacity (Norwegian Ministry of Energy, 2024). In contrast to many other countries, hy-

                                                          4
Master's Thesis     TIØ4905

dropower plants, with their large reservoirs, provide significant flexibility to the system,
allowing more than 75% of production to be classified as dispatchable, meaning that the
output can be adjusted when necessary (NVE, 2018).

However, as detailed in Section 1, the increasing share of VRES in the European energy
mix, such as wind and solar power, introduces new challenges to grid stability. In contrast
to dispatchable hydropower, VRES are characterized by intermittency, uncontrollability,
and difficulty in forecasting, requiring flexible on-demand energy sources to maintain grid
stability (Kondziella and Bruckner, 2016).

Ancillary services are introduced to ensure a continuous balance between supply and de-
mand in the grid. The European Union (2019) defines ancillary services as a service needed
for proper operation of a transmission and distribution system. These services are further
split into frequency and non-frequency control. While non-frequency control includes, e.g.,
voltage control, local grid stability and black-start capabilities, frequency control ensures
electricity balancing through reserve markets (Rancilio et al., 2022). Only the latter will
be discussed in this thesis, and the terms balancing markets and reserve markets will be
used interchangeably throughout this thesis.

Historically, grid stability has been maintained primarily through supply-side response.
However, with the political target of net-zero emissions by 2050, Statnett (2023) anticipates
a growing dependence on dispatchable resources to compensate for the increasing share
of VRES and ensure real-time balancing between supply and demand. This shift creates
opportunities for active market participation also from the demand-side.

2.1.2 Market actors and roles in the Norwegian power system

This section provides a brief overview of the most relevant power market roles illustrated
in Figure 4. A more detailed explanation of each actor can be found in Appendix B.

Nord Pool operates the day-ahead and intraday market across 16 European countries,
offering trading, clearing, settlement, and associated services (Nord Pool, 2020). RME, on
the other hand, regulates the natural monopolies for the TSO and DSOs, and is responsible
for reliability and efficiency at all levels of the power system (NVE, 2024d).

Building on the overview in Section 2.1.1, the TSO's responsibilities include real-time
system balancing and procuring both frequency and non-frequency services (Statnett,
2025c). DSOs operate the regional and distribution grids and set regulated grid tariffs,
designed to promote efficient use of resources.

Power producers sell electricity either through Nord Pool or bilateral contracts. Retailers
purchase electricity and resell it to end users, while ensuring portfolio balance against
actual consumption. To manage imbalances, they contract with BRPs, who carry financial
responsibility (eSett Oy, 2024). BSPs offer flexibility to the TSO by submitting reserve
bids, either through owned or managed assets. A single actor may act as both BSP and
BRP, or these roles can be handled by separate entities.

Consumers range from households to large industries, with some also acting as prosumers
by generating electricity and exporting surplus to the grid. Statnett (2018b) defines an
aggregator as a market participant that consolidates balancing loads from multiple con-

2.2 Electricity markets

Producers and consumers trade electricity in three main markets: the day-ahead and
intraday markets ­-- collectively known as the spot market ­-- and the reserve markets.

Prices in these markets are determined through auction mechanisms, primarily using the
pay-as-cleared principle, where all accepted bids receive the same market clearing price.
This applies to both the spot market and most reserve markets, with the exception of
mFRR-D, which operates under the pay-as-bid principle, where each accepted bid is paid
the price it offered. Market participants will operate as either price-takers, who accept
market prices, or price-setters, who directly influence the price through bids.

Ongoing market reforms are shifting the electricity system toward shorter time resolutions
and greater market integration, particularly through cross-border reserve markets. The
Nordic Balancing Model (NBM) aims to strengthen cooperation among Nordic TSOs.
Notably, on March 4, 2025, the automated Nordic mFRR EAM market was launched.

On March 18, the imbalance settlement period and intraday market transitioned from
hourly to 15-minute resolution, with the day-ahead market set to follow on September 30,
2025 (Nordic Balancing Model, 2025). Additionally, a European platform for both mFRR
(MARI) and aFRR (PICASSO) is scheduled to be established by 2026. However, in early

later for PICASSO (Statnett, 2025d). In response, RME stated in May 2025 that Statnett

2.2.1 Day-ahead market

The day-ahead market, operated by Nord Pool, serves as the main market where producers
and consumers submit bids in a closed auction for each time step of the following day.
The market operates based on the marginal price principle, where an aggregated supply
and demand curve is created for each bidding zone to determine the market-clearing price.
Bids must be submitted by 12:00, and the clearing prices for each time step and bidding
zone are published around 12.45 the day before delivery (Nord Pool, 2024).

While the day-ahead market ensures an initial balance of supply and demand, production
and consumption often depend on external factors such as local weather variations, unex-
pected demand variations, and unforeseen outages, which can cause deviations from the
scheduled plan. Since the day-ahead market lacks the flexibility for real-time adjustments,
participants can adjust their positions closer to delivery by participating in the intraday
market.

2.2.2 Intraday market

The intraday market, operated by Nord Pool, opens after the day-ahead market closes,
allowing participants to adjust their positions as conditions evolve. Although growing
rapidly, intraday trading remains modest in scale -- 114 TWh was traded through Nord
Pool in 2024, representing just over 11% of the day-ahead volume of 1,036 TWh (Nord
Pool, 2025e).

The uncertainty introduced by VRES makes balance prediction after the day-ahead market
more challenging. As a result, intraday trading helps participants refine forecasts, reduce
imbalance costs and lower reliance on reserve markets (Nord Pool, 2025d).

The intraday market can further be divided into a Continuous market, and Intraday auc-
tions.

In the continuous market, trading occurs continuously until one hour before delivery, where
transactions are matched on a first-come, first-served principle. This implies that buyers

                                                          7
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

with the highest bid get to buy first, and sellers offering the lowest price get to sell first,
and that the trade is carried out as soon as a buyer and seller bid matches.

Intraday auctions, introduced in June 2024, follow a structured clearing process at 15:00
and 22:00 the day before operations and at 10:00 on the operating day. The first two
auctions cover all 24 hours of the operational day, while the latter auction includes the
last 12 hours of the operating day (Statnett, 2024b).

The intraday market has an important role in improving system flexibility by bridging
the gap between the day-ahead market and reserve markets. While it reduces the need
for last-minute balancing volume, it cannot fully substitute reserve markets, which remain
essential for real-time system stability (Nord Pool, 2025c).

2.2.3 Reserve markets

The reserve markets, coordinated by Statnett, are designed to correct real-time imbalances
and maintain system frequency at 50 Hz (Statnett, 2024d).

The different reserve products, presented in Appendix A (Table 22), are categorized and
procured primarily on their response time. Fast frequency reserves (FFR) activate au-
tomatically within 1 second when frequency deviates by ± 0.3 Hz, slowing down the
frequency drop/rise. Primary reserves (FCR), also automatically activated within sec-
onds, provide instant response to frequency deviations and include two distinct products
with different activation patterns. If imbalances persists, secondary reserves (aFRR) auto-
matically activates within minutes, allowing primary reserves to rest. If further correction
is needed, tertiary reserves (mFRR) are manually activated within 15 minutes based on a
merit order to maximize social welfare (Statnett, 2024d). Figure 5 shows the sequence of
reserve product activation.

3 Power system flexibility from an industrial perspective

The market actors shown in Figure 4 pursue different objectives. While Statnett aims to
develop a power system resilient to imbalances, other seek to maximize trading profits.
For instance, an aggregator may target revenue optimization in reserve markets, whereas
its industrial prosumers prioritize stable product delivery. These potentially conflicting
objectives, combined with the limited literature on industrial perspectives, motivate a
methodological study to ensure the modeling framework reflects real-world dynamics.

This section presents insights from interviews with four Norwegian industrial actors -
representing the aluminum, pulp and paper, chemical, and district heating sectors - who
actively participate in or consider entering reserve markets. The interviews were structured
with the following core questions, supplemented with industry-specific follow-ups:

    · What type of power purchase agreement do you have and how does it affect your
       position in the day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets?

    · How is reserve market participation managed in practice, regarding bidding strate-
       gies, planning, and activation? What are the current opportunities and challenges,
       also considering the shift to shorter time resolution?

    · What are the operational impacts, such as opportunity costs or production disrup-
       tions?

    · Which reserve markets and direction (up-/down-regulation) do you participate in,
       and have others been considered based on the characteristics of your production?

    · Which flexible loads do you use (e.g., batteries, load adjustment, etc.), and are new
       investments being considered?

    · How do you see the interaction between heat, electricity, and storage in relation
       market bidding?

The following sections present key industrial insights, supported by findings from the
methodological study. These findings are not explicitly cited.

3.1 Power market trading for industrial actors

As discussed in Section 2.1.2, larger consumers can procure electricity through producers,
the spot-market or, through retailers -- the latter found most relevant. Unlike smaller
consumers (e.g., households), industrial actors are typically responsible for demand balanc-
ing, reporting projected consumption to the BRP, and covering any real-time imbalances
under bilateral agreements.

Industrial actors typically hold long-term fixed-price Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs)
with retailers, securing price stability for part or all of their demand. As these contracts

                                                         10
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

are bilaterally negotiated and often reflect historical spot prices, it is found reasonable
to model electricity prices using spot prices, and omit explicit modeling of long-term
contracts.

Any deviation from the contracted fixed-price volume, can be purchased or sold at spot-
price in the day-ahead market within a deadline. Real-time imbalances are charged ac-
cording to tertiary reserve (mFRR) prices in the operational period, plus an imbalance
fee. As the mFRR-prices are revealed post-operation, there is no incentive to speculate
on imbalances. If there is no imbalance in the system, the mFRR-price and spot-price
aligns, and only the imbalance fee applies as consequence of deviating. As the mFRR-
price is equal to the reserve activation price, both imbalance and activation are charged
accordingly.

Although not widely adopted among industrial actors, intraday trading can reduce imbal-
ance cost by adjusting consumption closer to real-time. However, as neither the intraday
nor the mFRR-price is known at the time of day-ahead bidding, the value of deviating
from full hedging remains uncertain.

According to a Norwegian BRP, reserve market participation imposes constraints on spot-
market bidding as the BRP knows the maximum capacity the BSP can handle. An analogy
is that if the BSP projects 80 MW consumption, which is also the maximum intake ca-
pacity, while simultaneously placing a 20 MW commitment for down-regulation, the BRP
restricts the spot-purchase to 60 MW. This ensures the BSP can fulfill its commitments
and prevents speculative behavior based on uncertain activation outcomes. It also restricts
real-time consumption adjustments without incurring imbalance fees.

These restrictions highlight important modeling implications. Allowing unrestricted real-
time adjustments could enable the model to bypass reserve obligations by accepting im-
balance costs when profitable -- behavior that does not reflect operational or regulatory
realities. Upon activation, Statnett enforces strict compliance, and repeated delivery fail-
ures can lead to exclusions from future bidding (Statnett, 2024e). This imposes that there
are no practical incentives in speculating between delivering on commitments and paying
the imbalance fee.

To reflect the link between capacity bids and operational consequences, the model must
avoid exploiting unreal arbitrage opportunities. Real-time adjustments should therefore
be included to ensure feasibility, but heavily penalized to discourage its use in the optimal
solution.

3.2 Industrial flexibility through Demand Side Management

DSM is defined by the ability of consumers to adjust energy use in response to price
signals or incentives, aiming to reduce consumption during periods of high demand or grid
congestion (Degefa et al., 2020). DSM can be incentive-based, where load is adjusted to
generate income (e.g., reserve market participation), or price-based, where consumption
is adjusted in response to volatile price signals (Gelazanskas and Gamage, 2014).

In this thesis, DSM is defined as an industrial actor's ability to adjust demand in response
to incentive-based or price-based programs. This includes, but is not limited to, adjusting
consumption without compromising total production, further referred to as load shifting.

Load shifting potential varies by industry. Golmohamadi (2022) highlights that batch-
processing industries like cement or metals can shift operations with minimal impact
on production, while industries with multiple shifts or production lines may reschedule
operations more easily (Degefa et al., 2020). This aligns with the interview insights, which
indicate that pulp and paper have greater flexibility in rescheduling without production
losses compared to aluminum and chemical sectors. District heating systems also provide
flexibility by coupling power and heat. Operating multiple boilers to meet peak demand
allows them to shift between fuels, enabling demand response both through load shifting
and by switching energy sources. Shoreh et al. (2016) further characterizes food industry,
data centers and heating and cooling processes, which are present in many industries, as
highly flexible processes.

DSM potential further depends on the interdependence of sequential processes. Without
intermediate storage, upstream adjustments may disrupt downstream operations (Shoreh
et al., 2016). Demand response through support services can be achieved more easily, in
contrast to production processes. However, these loads account for a small share of total
consumption (e.g. 8-12%).

DSM may be less profitable for industries with strict supply security and high production
loss costs from power interruption. Statnett (2018a) finds that some industrial actors
reduce consumption during high spot price periods. Their internal mapping of Nordic
industries estimates the cost of one-hour outage resulting in demand curve across sectors,
shown in Figure 8. Industries with stable demand profiles -- such as aluminum, chemical
and petroleum -- face high outage cost due to limited flexibility. In contrast, industries
like pulp and paper incur lower costs, due to their ability to reschedule operations. These
findings align well with insights from the interviews. It is therefore crucial to distinguish
load shedding from load shifting: when demand can be redistributed across time without
production loss, the associated costs are substantially lower, and often negligible.

A key insight is that flexibility affecting production typically requires manual adjustment,

constraints like rest times vary between sectors -- for example, aluminum may require
8 hours of recovery following one hour of load adjustments, unlike pulp and paper.

3.3 Political and regulatory barriers to industrial flexibility

Due to the operational burden associated with demand-side flexibility, strong incentives
are needed to motivate participation. A review by Leinauer et al. (2022) identifies key
barriers in the German industry as economic (e.g. limited savings, production risks),
regulatory (e.g. grid fees) and technical (e.g. inflexible processes, conflicts with efficiency
goals). This section highlights two regulatory aspects particularly relevant to industrial
flexibility: grid tariffs and connection charges.

3.3.1 Grid tariff and its implications

Grid tariffs, set by DSOs and regulated by RME, covers the costs of building, operating
and maintaining the grid infrastructure (Norgesnett AS, 2025). For large consumers (over
100 MWh annually), tariffs include a fixed part, energy part and power part -- only the
two latter is considered relevant for modeling as fixed costs does not impact the optimal
solution, only the objective value.

Today's tariffs inadequately reflect the actual grid costs, and provide limited incentives
for flexible consumption (Statnett, 2025a). Additionally, static tariff structures, as cur-
rently applied in Norway, provide limited incentives for demand-side flexibility compared
to dynamically adjustable tariffs, which have been demonstrated as both cost-efficient
and socially beneficial (Backe et al., 2020). Especially, actors offering down-regulation by

                                                         14
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

increasing power consumption potentially risk higher tariff costs due to increased peak
demand. This creates conflicting signals: industries may be rewarded in reserve markets
but penalized through increased grid fees.

Statnett (2025a) has proposed tariff changes, with implementation expected from 2026.
These include the introduction of a dynamic capacity-based tariff and incentives encour-
aging large consumers to actively participate in reserve markets. Based on this, it could
be relevant to model varying tariff structures to analyze their impact on future investment
decisions. However, this is left for future research.

3.3.2 Connection charges

Resulting from increased pressure on existing infrastructure, industrial electrification often
requires grid upgrades, triggering connection charges that require customers to cover the
cost of grid upgrades. These are shared based on the formula (NVE, 2024a):

Cost base = construction costs-reinvestment costs+accelerated costs+investigation costs

Construction costs cover new capacity, reinvestment costs reflect upgrades that would
occur regardless of expansion, acceleration costs accounts for asset replacement before it
has reached its lifetime, and investigation costs relate to assessing the upgrade need.

Although this serves as a price signal to limit unnecessary expansion and encourage loca-
tion in areas with available capacity (NVE, 2024a), the connection process is slow, ranging
from 0.5 to 8 years and costs from 0.2-13 MEUR (lnett, 2025). This can potentially delay
projects and drive industry relocation.

As an alternative, Statnett offers connection on terms, allowing grid access under cur-
tailment conditions without triggering network investments. This can reduce costs and
lead times, offering a trade-off between guaranteed access and flexibility-based curtailment
(NVE, 2024e).

A potential strategy is to combine flexible investments with connection on terms to re-
duce reliance on grid expansion. This may enable faster grid access and enable new grid
connections. These trade-offs can be analyzed in the framework proposed in this thesis
through capacity constraints, though assessing opportunity cost and a robust comparison
baseline requires detailed, case-specific data.

3.4 Key findings

Key findings of the methodological study include that industrial actors aim to electrify
their process as part of the decarbonization pathway. However, since maintaining required
production levels remains the highest priority, there are limited incentives to invest in flex-
ible assets solely for use within the industrial process, unless they provide clear economic
or operational benefits. Furthermore, storing product between production processes is

                 15
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

found to be most viable, as it enables load shifting without requiring major investments
in new technologies.

A key distinction emerges between industries with stable demand profiles and those with
inherently flexible processes. The latter show greater potential for peak shaving and
rescheduling, improving the economic case for flexibility investments.

Another key finding is the concern of the introduction of automatic activation markets, as
many industrial processes still require manual operation and therefore depend on manual
activation. Finally, offering up-regulation is more common across the industries, as the
potential for downward load shifting generally exceeds that for upward flexibility.

However, the attractiveness of fossil-based technologies is constrained by increased O&M
costs, high CO2-prices, and growing expectations for fossil-free production.

The project report conducted a comprehensive analysis of technologies relevant for indus-
trial actors, categorized into conversion technologies, energy storage technologies and on-
site power generation technologies (Skare et al., 2024). The following sections will present
the key modeling characteristics for each category supported by load shifting modeling.

4.1 Modeling energy storage

Modeling energy storage requires representation of key parameters such as energy capacity,
charge/discharge rates, efficiencies, and losses. While round-trip efficiency can capture
overall losses, it neglects individual process and storage efficiencies. To capture these
dynamics, this thesis incorporates individual efficiencies.

Since simultaneous charging and discharging is unrealistic, this must be addressed in
the model. Fixing the product of charge and discharge to zero restricts activity to one
direction for each time step, but results in a non-linear formulation. Another solution is
introducing binary variables representing if the model charges or discharges in a specific
time step, increasing computational complexity (Degefa et al., 2020). To retain linearity,
this thesis limits simultaneous activity by constraining the sum of charging and discharging
by a common maximum rate. This approach, illustrated in Figure 9 with qcharge and
qdischarge representing the amount charged and discharged respectively and P max reflecting
the maximum rate, encourages the model to prioritize the most valuable action at each
time step.

Energy storage modeling is possible with different levels of complexity. Efficiencies vary
during ramping, but are modeled constant in this thesis to keep the model linear. In
addition, the entire energy capacity range is available for use, disregarding operational
limits such as maintaining the state of charge within a specified interval. As demonstrated
in the project report (Skare et al., 2024), the impact of charge and discharge behavior
when O&M costs are included suggests that this could be decisive, particularly for longer
time horizons. In addition, degradation should be captured to avoid frequent charge and
discharge, especially for Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS).

Degradation is a result of a complex combination of electrochemical and mechanical pro-
cesses, leading to capacity decrease and power fading for a lithium-ion battery (Hentunen
et al., 2017). Several factors affect the cycle life and calendar life of a battery, such as

                                                         18
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

ambient conditions, the depth and frequency of the charge and discharge cycles, and the
state of charge. The report proposes to introduce a cost of using the storage, based on
factors such as the depth of discharge and the number of cycles. Degefa et al. (2020)
proposes indirect modeling through input parameters, by applying a linear reduction to
the maximum energy content of storage systems over the planning period, using a time
index. However, this would not capture the cost aspect of using the storage. Another
possible method is to dynamically let the use affect the power and energy capacity of the
battery throughout the planning period.

Modeling degradation by introducing a cost per cycle is also considered in this thesis.
The idea is that a storage has a fixed cycle lifetime, and the accumulated cost of use
should cover reinvestment when the lifetime is exceeded. This approach is adopted by
Haugen et al. (2021), who introduces variables to represent a full equivalent cycle, state-
of-health, and a cyclic aging function, incorporating both calendar and cyclic aging. Berg
et al. (2022) proposes an alternative method, where the degradation cost represents the
replacement cost of the storage and is linked to cycle depth through a cycle depth stress
function. However, since the invested capacity in this thesis is modeled endogenously,
these approaches result in non-linear formulations and are therefore not selected.

To avoid non-linearity and the use of binary variables, this thesis proposes degradation
modeling by introducing a cost of discharging. The cost parameter can additionally cover
the O&M costs and other technology-specific aspects such as CO2-emissions. The param-
eter can further be post-processed if, e.g., the number of cycles/frequency and operational
pattern is unrealistic, taking into account the number of dimensioned cycles per lifetime.

4.2 Modeling load shift

While simultaneous charging and discharging are meaningless in conventional storage sys-
tems, simultaneous load shifting -- where one process ramps up while another ramps down
-- can be meaningful. Discussions with industrial actors revealed that production lines
typically operate near full capacity, driven by economic efficiency and the cost of under-
utilized assets. As a result, this thesis models load shifting using separate parameters for
up-shift and down-shift, which can be tailored to sector-specific characteristics.

The inconvenience of shifting loads can be modeled in several ways. One approach is to
model efficiencies, such that operating away from the design point is less efficient and would
require increased energy use per unit output. Another method penalizes unrecoverable
production, effectively capturing output losses. Moreover, flexible and inflexible loads
can be represented using different fuels. Inspired by Paulus and Borggrefe (2011), this
thesis adopts a linear cost for shifting, discouraging unrealistic cycles and enabling post-
processing adjustments if needed.

Degefa et al. (2021) emphasize that flexibility characteristics -- such as power capacity,
ramp duration, service time, and rest time -- match flexible resources to different an-
cillary markets. As the importance of the various characteristics vary across different
ancillary services, this should also be considered when modeling load shift. Degefa et al.

Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

(2020) introduce a flexible node balance approach where flexible demand is modeled as the
reference demand adjusted for up-shift, down-shift, curtailment, and load not supplied.
Compensation is proposed for each of these actions. Since the model enforces balance
between up-shift and down-shift over the planning horizon, compensation for load shifting
applies only to downward shifts.

This thesis assumes that the planned production must be met and that energy consumption
scales linearly with output. Therefore, total flexible demand over the planning period
must match the reference demand. Consequently, load shedding and load curtailment are
excluded. As the cost of load shifting varies across industry actors, the framework allows
for case-specific initialization of opportunity costs.

4.3 Modeling generation-, conversion- and grid technologies

The modeling of generation-, conversion- and grid technologies is proposed generalized in
this thesis, incorporating efficiencies and availability factors. When modeling heat pumps
specifically, both electricity and excess heat should be considered as input factors. Beck
et al. (2021) proposes a model using the Carnot equation, where the heat output is decided
based on the temperature difference between the source and sink, coefficient of performance
(COP) and electricity input. The heat output cannot exceed the installed capacity and,
from an energy balance perspective, should be limited by the sum of the excess heat and
the added electricity. Consequently, additional constraints should be added to the model,
representing the need for excess heat to run the heat pump. An assumption made is that
excess heat is available at the same time step as heat is supplied, which may misrepresent
batch processes, where excess heat often occurs after heat is supplied to the batch (Beck
et al., 2021).

To capture the economic benefit of technologies capable of operating with multiple fuels,
this thesis introduces modes of operation for each technology, inspired by Lo¨ffler et al.
(2017). Each mode defines specific input-output fuel combinations along with associated
operational parameters, enabling a flexible and accurate representation of technologies
that can switch between or combine different fuels. To ensure that the energy output does
not exceed the installed capacity, the sum of energy outputs across all modes is constrained
by the installed capacity. This approach enables distinct modes for grid import and grid
export, ensuring a structured representation of energy flows. Costs of import and export
are managed by assigning a cost parameter to each operational mode.

4.4 Cost normalization and discounting methods

To accurately represent the total costs of investing and operating the system, all costs
should be discounted to net present value, and investment costs normalized to the planning
period. In this thesis, normalization refers to allocating costs to the planning period
based on the lifetime and investment cost, rather than accounting for the full upfront
cost. Additionally, salvage values could be included to represent remaining value after the
exceeded lifetime but are omitted in this thesis.

The Equivalent Annual Cost method (EAC) is used in this thesis to annualize investment
costs. It accounts for the investment cost (I), asset lifetime (n) and annual discount rate
(r), resulting in the following formula:

                                                                       I ·r
                                  Annual capital cost = 1 - (1 + r)-n

The annualized costs can be proportionally allocated to shorter periods, or the formula
can be adapted using monthly or daily discount rates. The choice of discount rate depends
on the investment type and the company policy, and may reflect the weighted average cost
of capital (WACC), the risk-free interest rate, or the internal rate of return (IRR).

Several other methods could also be used to discount costs, potentially affecting model
outcomes. Examples include the declining balance, sum of the years digit, progressive, unit
of production, sinking fund and straight-line methods. The latter two are implemented in
GENeSYS-MOD (Lo¨ffler et al., 2017). However, as this thesis focuses on the impact of
uncertainty representation on investment decisions, the influence of depreciation methods
is left for further work.

For the purpose of this thesis, discounting of operational costs is neglected, as this would
mainly impact the results when modeling longer periods than anticipated.

5 Mathematical programming

Mathematical programming, rooted in operations research, provides decision support by
optimizing an objective function through the selection of optimal variables within an
allowed opportunity space, and is categorized by problem structure (Hillier and Lieberman,
2015).

In Linear Programming (LP), a linear objective is optimized over continuous variables
within a convex feasible region defined by linear constraints. Solutions are found at the
region's vertices, allowing for efficient and scalable algorithms. Mixed Integer Linear Pro-
gramming (MILP) extends LP by including integer variables, enabling modeling of discrete
decisions like capacity expansion or piecewise linear relations such as step-wise efficiencies
or investment costs. The added complexity makes MILP non-convex and computationally
intensive, requiring methods such as branch-and-bound and cutting planes. Non-Linear
Programming (NLP) problems involve at least one non-linear function in the objective
or constraints, capturing inherently non-linear relationships. Its complexity depends on
whether the problem is convex, and it is well-suited for modeling quadratic relations,
efficiency curves, or non-linear cost functions.

5.1 Stochastic programming

Classical optimization models are deterministic, assuming that all parameters are known
with certainty. However, this might not be the case in reality. VRES generation, demand,
and energy prices are examples of parameters that might be unknown at the time of
decision and will only provide optimal solutions if the realization of the parameters matches
the forecast. Stochastic programming explicitly addresses uncertainty when solving the
problem, typically aiming to optimize the expected value of the objective function over
the long run (Hillier and Lieberman, 2015). To solve a stochastic program numerically, a
common approach is to approximate the underlying continuous probability distribution by
discretizing it into a finite set of outcomes. These outcomes are typically structured into
a scenario tree, which represents possible realizations of the uncertain parameters across
stages (Birge and Louveaux, 2011). The resulting deterministic formulation, encompassing
all scenarios, is known as the deterministic equivalent problem.

In a two-stage stochastic problem, initial decisions are made in the first stage, before
the uncertain is revealed, followed by possible recourse actions in the second stage. The
concept of recourse captures the model's ability to adapt to different realizations of uncer-
tainty. In multi-stage stochastic problems, this structure is extended: decisions are made
sequentially across several stages, with uncertainty gradually revealed over time (Birge
and Louveaux, 2011).

Recourse can be classified based on how the model handles realization of uncertainty,
while ensuring feasible solutions. Complete recourse guarantees a feasible solution for
every possible realization, while relatively complete recourse requires a carefully chosen
first-stage decision to ensure feasibility. This distinction is important to ensure that the

                                                         23
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

model remains reliable given the chosen uncertainty representation when formulating the
mathematical model (Birge and Louveaux, 2011).

Deterministic equivalent models for multistage problems are computationally challenging
due to exponential growth in the number of scenarios, variables, and constraints. As
traditional solution methods eventually scale poorly, decomposition techniques such as
Benders decomposition, Lagrangean relaxation and Stochastic dual dynamic programming
are often applied to exploit the problem structure in stochastic programs (Birge and
Louveaux, 2011).

5.1.1 Multi-Stage and Multi-Horizon stochastic programming

While stochastic programming with recourse is commonly structured as either a two-stage
or multi-stage model, multi-horizon modeling enables the consideration of uncertainties
across multiple distinct timescales. It is particularly useful when aiming to capture both
short-term operational uncertainties and long-term strategic uncertainties within the same
framework (Kaut et al., 2014).

Incorporating both a high number of sequential decision stages and the interplay between
strategic and operational uncertainty significantly increases model complexity. This sug-
gests a trade-off between broad and detailed uncertainty representation, and incorporating
several strategic periods. While multi-stage stochastic programming limits direct repre-
sentation of evolving strategic uncertainty and the ability to revise these decisions over
time, sensitivity to long-term strategic parameters can still be explored by varying these
parameters explicitly.

5.1.2 Node-formulation and Scenario-formulation

Vitali et al. (2021) highlight node-formulation and scenario-formulation as two common
approaches for formulating multi-stage stochastic programs. Both use nodes to represent
unfolding uncertainty over time, but differ in how uncertainty is mathematically expressed.

In the scenario formulation, each node is indexed by both stage and scenario, resulting in a
two-dimensional structure as depicted in Figure 13. While suitable for two-stage problems,
this approach necessitates multiple replications of variables and constraints in multi-stage
settings and requires explicit non-anticipativity constraints to ensure consistency across
scenarios sharing the same history.

Conversely, the node formulation uses a single index per node, yielding a one-dimensional
structure. This simplifies the structure and avoids the need for non-anticipativity con-
straints, as variables and constraints are defined only once per node. Vitali et al. (2021)
show that this significantly reduces model size by approximately 32% and 55% for two-
stage and three-stage models, respectively. As this formulation reduces mathematical
complexity, it allows more computational resources to be allocated to representing uncer-
tainty in greater detail.

5.1.3 Stability test

A critical aspect in stochastic programming is ensuring that solutions are robust and
not artifacts of poor Scenario Generation Routines (SGRs). Stability testing provides a
systematic way to assess how sensitive model outcomes are to input variations. King and
Wallace (2012) distinguish between in-sample and out-of-sample stability.

In-sample stability evaluates whether the optimal objective value remains consistent across
different scenario trees generated by the same SGR. This checks that the model is not
overly sensitive to how uncertainty is discretized, and that the model behaves consistently
when sampling from the same dataset. Since distinct solutions can yield similar outcomes
due to degeneracy in large instances, comparing objective values is more meaningful than
directly comparing decision variables.

Out-of-sample stability refers to whether solutions derived from different in-sample tests
performs similarly when tested against the true distribution, typically represented by a
more detailed discretization. By testing the in-sample solutions on this expanded scenario
tree, one can compare their relative performance, assessing whether the in-sample solution
is biased by the SGR or performs consistently under a richer representation of uncertainty.

6 Related literature review

This section reviews relevant research to provide a comprehensive overview of how uncer-
tainty has been addressed in industrial flexibility problems aiming for cost-optimal invest-
ments, in line with the research question of this thesis. The main objective is to identify
the potential highlighted in the literature and assess the methodological approaches and
perspectives through which it has been explored or not. Focus areas include modeling
of electricity markets under uncertainty, particularly in a multi-market context, as well
as industrial flexibility, energy storage, demand-side management, and decarbonization
through capacity investments.

The review emphasizes the relevance and limitations of existing models and insights, and
how these align with or diverge from the scope of this thesis. The chapter concludes by
identifying gaps in the literature and outlining the specific contributions of this work.

6.1 Multi-market participation under uncertainty

Research on multi-market participation in power trading often differs between coordinated
bidding and separate bidding. In coordinated bidding, the outcomes of earlier markets in-
fluence bidding strategies in subsequent ones, whereas separate bidding treats each market
independently. Klæboe and Fosso (2013) review studies and compare these approaches
in multi-market operation, highlighting market conditions and competition as important
factors. Their findings indicate modest gains (0.1­2%) from coordinated bidding across
day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets. Lo¨hndorf and Wozabal (2023) applies a multi-
stage stochastic program in a coordinated bidding setting in Germany, revealing substan-
tial gains for storage systems -- particularly for batteries (8.3­8.5%) and pumped hydro
storage (10­29.1%). However, they do not consider reserve market participation.

Several research papers, on the other hand, incorporate reserve markets into a coordi-
nated bidding framework. Boomsma et al. (2014), Ottesen et al. (2018), Bo¨hringer et al.
(2019), Bohlayer et al. (2020), and Paredes et al. (2024) include both day-ahead and re-
serve markets, combined with participation in different reserve capacity and activation
markets, consistently demonstrating improved profitability across various experimental
setups. Others extend this to include intraday markets, offering a more complete repre-
sentation of multi-market participation. Han et al. (2017) includes day-ahead, intraday,
and reserve market participation from an aggregator perspective, while Silva et al. (2022),
Kraft et al. (2023), and Nolzen et al. (2022) explore the same markets from the view of
power plant portfolios, utilizing multi-energy systems. These studies collectively highlight
the complementary value of including both day-ahead, intraday, and reserve market partic-
ipation, yet none assess how such multi-market involvement influences long-term capacity
expansion decisions.

Miskiw et al. (2025) builds on this by conducting a study on the effects of price-making,
in contrast to price-taking in coordinated bidding in sequential electricity markets. The
paper also compares a myopic (separate) approach to a coordinated approach, finding an

                                                         26
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

overall relative gain of 18%, with higher relative gains in the winter season. The paper
does highlight that ignoring the price impact might overestimate coordinated benefits.
However, the study does not address long-term strategic investment decisions.

Although coordinated bidding captures realistic information flows and inter-market depen-
dencies, it also increases the computational complexity when uncertainty spans multiple
markets. To manage this, several studies apply scenario reduction techniques. Boomsma
et al. (2014) uses iterative sampling and stage-wise clustering, while Kraft et al. (2023)
and Silva et al. (2022) apply k -means and k -medoids clustering. These techniques help
retain stochastic detail while keeping the model computationally solvable.

However, in more complex multi-market models, scenario reduction alone is often insuf-
ficient to ensure tractability. As a result, many studies simplify the stochastic structure
by collapsing real-time operations into a single stage, effectively optimizing with full fore-
sight. Unlike Boomsma et al. (2014), who solves all problems in one large multi-stage
structure, others take a more aggregated approach. For instance, Ottesen et al. (2018)
reduce 168 hourly stages to seven daily. Although Ottesen et al. (2018) acknowledges this
as a simplification, they argue it is reasonable given the overall focus on first-stage reserve
market decisions.

6.2 Industrial flexibility, storage and DSM

Research on industrial flexibility, storage, and DSM offers valuable insights into how indus-
try can adapt to dynamic energy systems. Mathiesen and Lund (2009) highlight large-scale
heat pumps, flexible demand, and electric boilers with Thermal Energy Storage (TES) as
promising for renewable energy source integration. Beck et al. (2021) emphasize the poten-
tial of power-to-heat with TES to leverage price fluctuations, while Berg et al. (2024) find
TES more cost-effective than BESS for industrial energy communities, underscoring the
value of shared infrastructure. Elio et al. (2021) provide a broader comparison of storage
options, identifying BESS, Pumped Hydro Energy Storage (PHES), and Compressed Air
Energy Storage (CAES) as versatile for DSM, though optimal solutions depend on site-
specific conditions. However, these studies do not consider an integrated energy system
from a single-industry perspective.

Industrial flexibility has emerged as a key enabler for accelerating the decarbonization
of heavy industries, particularly in the context of increasing electrification. Foslie et al.
(2024) demonstrate that fuel switching, load shedding, and load shifting can alleviate grid
congestion and accelerate the electrification of energy-intensive processes. They emphasize
the sector's complexity and high electricity demand, requiring both direct and indirect
electrification, alongside carbon capture. The study highlights the industry's potential
to support the grid due to its large energy share and shows that sector coupling can
enhance flexibility and reduce peak load. Flexibility costs are further estimated at 111­
210 EUR/MWh, which is found to be competitive with grid expansion, but with shorter
lead times and reduced carbon emissions.

Building on this, Richstein and Hosseinioun (2020) investigate how electricity tariffs in-

Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

fluence industrial flexibility. They find that peak demand charges, aimed at reflecting
grid costs, may discourage DSM by penalizing increased electricity imports. Moreover,
high grid tariffs are found to reduce incentives to invest in flexibility technologies like
storage and capacity expansion. Foslie et al. (2023) supports this by analyzing electri-
fication strategies for chemical processes, confirming the technical value of flexibility in
VRES-dominated systems. They further highlight that the economic viability depends on
supportive policies, such as financial incentives. However, they do not consider uncertainty.

Together, these studies point at unresolved challenges, particularly the lack of detailed as-
sessments comparing the cost-effectiveness of flexibility measures relative to grid expansion
and connection charges.

Additionally, while Foslie et al. (2023) emphasizes the value of flexibility, the study over-

is highly dependent on the duration and response time of flexible assets, making applica-
bility highly technology- and case-specific. Javanshir et al. (2023) compares heat pumps
and electric boilers for reserve provision, showing that electric boilers, with no ramping
restrictions, are better suited for fast-response markets, while heat pumps should run on
partial-to-full load rather than being shut down entirely and restarted. This introduces a
modeling trade-off: whether technologies should be explicitly assigned to specific markets
based on technical suitability.

Manna et al. (2023) further extends this by analyzing industrial flexibility through com-
bined energy storage and reserve market participation, using a MILP model with uncer-
tainty. They find that storage increases profit by optimizing energy schedules and enabling
reserve market participation. However, the model excludes intraday market participation
and investment decisions.

Several studies highlighted the technical and economic potential of DSM in both large and
small-scale industries, particularly through load shifting and peak shaving. Golmohamadi
(2022) highlights significant DSM potential in energy-intensive sectors like cement and
aluminum, while Choobineh and Mohagheghi (2015) demonstrates cost savings for smaller
industries by integrating on-site RES and BESS. Further, Duarte et al. (2020) incorporates
uncertainty, demonstrating a significant potential in combining DSM, RES, and BESS.

Paulus and Borggrefe (2011) assess DSM potential in various industries with reserve mar-
ket participation in Germany, showing strong technical and economic promise, particularly
for energy-intensive sectors. They highlight the role of DSM in balancing VRES with load
shifting and shedding as key strategies. Due to high utilization rates, industries typically
offer more up- than down-regulation, and shifting is preferred over shedding due to lower
opportunity costs. Reserve market activations therefore tend to focus on processes where
load can be shifted without major economic loss, as industries are likely to price activation
at or above their marginal costs. Helin et al. (2017) extend this with a bottom-up analysis
of the pulp and paper industry, where reserve activations are based on marginal cost, with
intraday market compensation for lost loads. While the study finds significant potential,
it notes barriers like high marginal costs and risk premiums. However, the study focuses
solely on DSM and does not consider investments in other flexible resources.

Nebel et al. (2020) compare DSM from aluminum electrolysis with other flexibility options

6.3 Capacity expansion investments and decarbonization pathway

Most research regarding capacity expansion and decarbonization pathways focuses on
either local microgrids or national/global systems, with limited attention to industry-
specific flexibility and its benefits in mitigating price volatility and emission costs. Levin
et al. (2023) emphasizes the importance of energy storage as the share of VRES increases,
pointing to overlooked arbitrage opportunities for actors with certain flexibility. The study
argues that current models underestimate storage value under volatile prices and higher
carbon costs and calls for integrating taxes and emissions into future capacity expansion
planning. However, it remains unclear how different industrial sectors will respond, raising
questions about long-term investment strategies in flexibility and storage beyond short-
term market signals.

Building on this, Haugen et al. (2024) discuss how the increasing share of VRES increases
price volatility, directly affecting investment decisions. They highlight the need for long-
term planning horizons, in order to capture different market conditions, and argue for
modeling storage with degradation, technical constraints, and multi-market participation
to accurately provide investment opportunities and limitations. While Haugen et al. (2024)
criticizes existing models for their failure to adequately account for uncertainty, particu-
larly in price formation, flexibility, and reserve market participation, it does not propose
a solution or method itself. It concludes that future models must include uncertainty in
supply, demand, and costs, along with sub-hourly resolution to reflect VRES variability.
Similarly, Oree et al. (2017) highlights a shift from cost minimization to multi-objective
frameworks that balance cost, emissions, and reliability, emphasizing the need to incor-
porate variability, uncertainty, and operational flexibility when evaluating future capacity
expansion investments. While these studies offer valuable insights into key modeling con-
siderations, they do not propose a methodological framework of their own.

carbonization pathways, each with distinct strengths and limitations. As detailed in the
project report (Skare et al., 2024), these models vary in their ability to capture uncertainty,

6.4 Our contribution

While several studies individually address industrial flexibility, energy storage, DSM,
multi-market participation, and decarbonization pathways, few integrate these elements
into a unified framework under uncertainty. This thesis fills that gap by developing a
multi-stage stochastic programming framework that integrates coordinated bidding across
day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets with capacity expansion and operational flexibil-
ity, from the perspective of a single industrial actor. The model captures industry-specific
demand, on-site generation, multi-market participation, and investment decisions.

7 Problem description

The power system has historically been characterized by stable costs with limited short-
term volatility. However, rapid deployment of VRES, electrification, and stricter regula-
tions have introduced volatile electricity prices and rising carbon costs. Industrial actors
are now expected to actively support grid stability and respond to market signals -- in-
troducing both operational challenges and new revenue opportunities through flexibility.

Long-term investment decisions are particularly challenging due to long asset lifetimes
and high upfront costs. Therefore, their adaptability to future market developments and
regulatory changes is important. To remain competitive and align with decarbonization
targets, investment decisions must balance short-term profitability with long-term uncer-
tainty. However, future profitability of flexible assets depends on uncertain electricity
prices, market structures, and regulatory developments -- unknown at the time of invest-
ment.

As a consequence, investment assessments should include scenario-based evaluations rather
than simple payback times or static cost assessments. Additionally, emerging intraday and
ancillary markets offer new profit potential by enabling dynamic power trading closer to
real-time. Although market mechanisms are signaling increased value of flexible operation,
the overall complexity makes it difficult for actors to identify where and how to capture
these revenue streams.

Beyond uncertainty, the industry must deal with multiple electricity markets, each with
different temporal resolutions, pricing structures, and criteria. Additionally, the interde-
pendencies between fuel usage, conversion technologies, and the operational planning of
flexible assets -- such as batteries capable of peak shaving, spot market arbitrage, and
ancillary service provision -- further complicate the challenge of making robust investment
decisions.

These developments necessitate robust decision support accounting for uncertain market
trajectories, technology interactions, and site-specific constraints. We hypothesize that
explicitly modeling uncertainty improves investment decisions by accounting for varying
prices and future market dynamics.

This thesis aims to support industrial actors in making sound investment decisions under
uncertainty by enhancing flexibility potential and enabling participation in multiple energy
markets. To minimize total system cost, this thesis develops a stochastic optimization
framework that integrates investments and operational planning while accounting for site-
specific constraints such as energy demand profiles, flexibility limitations, and available
technologies.

8 Mathematical model

8.1 Model description

To properly assess the research questions, the framework must support various representa-
tions of uncertainty. A LP formulation is therefore chosen to ensure scalability within the
given scope (see Section 5). Further, a node formulation is applied, as it is computationally
cheaper than scenario formulation as detailed in Section 5.1.2. All electricity markets --
day-ahead, intraday, and reserve market -- are modeled within a single stage, meaning no
recourse actions occur after market realization. The framework includes reserve capacity
commitments and potential activations, but explicit activation market bidding is omitted.

While this setup limits the model's ability to reflect sequential bidding, it reflects the reality
that industrial actors submit bids without knowing future outcomes (see Section 3). Real-
time adjustments can be implemented through operational modes in the model, though
penalties should be applied to prevent unrealistic arbitrage behavior, as discussed in Sec-
tion 3.1.

Since future prices are unpredictable, industrial actors make binding commitments before
realizations are known, facing both short- and long-term uncertainty. A flexible multi-
stage stochastic model is developed, allowing user-defined input for the number of stages,
scenarios, temporal resolution, and technologies. This enables detailed modeling of com-
mitments and operations across day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets. The first stage
determines investment decisions, the second stage plans the initial period, and subsequent
stages handle planning and operational dispatch based on unfolding information. Given
the research question and the discussion on uncertainty representation in Section 5, the
model does not incorporate a multi-horizon approach. However, long-term strategic pa-
rameters can still be explored within the existing framework. Obligations in intraday
and reserve markets are modeled through binary acceptance and activation parameters,
capturing the inherent risk of activation in each time step.

Figure 14 depicts how uncertainty is progressively revealed, actions committed at each
stage, and how stages map to operational periods. In the final stage, only real-time
decisions remain, as all planning commitments have already been determined. Capacity
prices are assumed known, and uncertainty in prices and binary activation parameters
ensures that the model makes a weighted decision against possible realizations to ensure
feasibility and profitability.

The industrial actor is modeled as a price-taker, assuming all submitted bids are accepted
at the market clearing price (pay-as-cleared). This avoids binary bidding decisions de-
pending on price, which could otherwise overestimate profitability by enabling large bid
volumes without affecting prices. To mitigate this, market access is restricted by a par-
ticipation limit, where the capacity bid is proposed limited to the least of the indivisible
bid size and a fraction of the total market volume. While input data should reflect a risk
of activation at each time step, forcing the model to weigh the probability of activation
when allocating capacity, a cap corresponding to full utilization of all flexible assets is
imposed to ensure bids correspond with available flexibility. Furthermore, the industrial
actor is assumed risk-neutral, focusing on minimizing expected costs without including
market preferences.

Multi-energy flexibility is modeled through load shifting, conversion, storage, and on-
site generation, each constrained by efficiencies, capacities, availability, and ramp rates.
Heat pumps require both electricity and waste heat, the latter limited to a fraction of
heat demand (see Section 4.3). Energy storage systems account for charge/discharge
limits, efficiencies, and losses. Load shifting is restricted by time windows and volume
constraints, allowing temporal redistribution while ensuring total demand is met within
industry-specific constraints.

Figure 15 illustrates the intended on-site energy flows, incorporating auxiliary functions,
technologies, fuels, and storage. Possible interactions are defined by user-defined sets
and parameters. Each fuel has an exogenously defined demand that, adjusted for load
shift, must be met each time step. As demand can be set to zero, this set-up allows
the model to utilize alternative fuels if economically favorable. The model is flexible,
allowing easy adaptation to specific case studies by adjusting the number of technologies,
fuels, and storage options. Auxiliary constraints are directly linked to their corresponding
technologies.

For each technology, the output energy flow is restricted by the installed peak power mul-
tiplied by an availability factor. This factor is particularly important for VRES, as it
accounts for site-specific conditions affecting generation, such as solar irradiation. For
other technologies, the availability factor may reflect planned maintenance, unplanned
outages, or general reliability. To better represent the operational behavior of each tech-
nology, degradation and efficiency losses could also be incorporated into the model, either
as part of the cost of use or through adjustments in the availability factors.

A cost component is included for each technology, representing expenses related to auxil-
iary services, operation, and maintenance. Each technology includes a set of operational
modes, each assigned a carbon intensity value to discourage the use of carbon-intensive
options. Furthermore, a maximum emission cap is introduced as a hard constraint. This
enables the integration of Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) technologies in the frame-
work, either as an extension to specific technologies or as stand-alone investment options
assigned a negative carbon intensity. The grid -technologies are modeled such that one
mode represents import and another represents export.

An energy balance is included for each fuel, time step and node, controlling the energy
flow between demand, conversion, storage, and energy markets. Specifically for electricity,
power grid import and export are represented by the activated commitments across the
day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets. The model supports fuel switching by allowing
multiple input­output conversion paths, driven by cost and availability.

9 Case study and input data

To evaluate investment decisions under uncertainty for industrial actors with differing on-
site demand and flexibility potential, the case study focuses on two sectors introduced
in Section 3.2: pulp and paper, and aluminum. These industries vary in flexibility, load
profiles, heat levels, and relative share of heat and electricity demand -- factors that
may lead to different exposure to uncertainty and distinct optimal technology mixes and
capacity choices.

To address research question No. 1, investment decisions are evaluated across different
uncertainty representations to assess the need for advanced optimization models to ensure
robust outcomes. Research question No. 2 is then analyzed using the most stable and
near-optimal solution identified through out-of-sample tests.

The following sections present the scenario generation process, followed by a description
of the computational instances, assumptions, input data, and relevant case setups. An
exchange rate of 11.7 NOK/EUR is used throughout this thesis. Each stage in the scenario
tree consists of 24 time steps representing one day with hourly resolution.

9.1 Scenario generation

Scenario generation is crucial in stochastic programming, as the selected scenarios directly
influence the quality and robustness of the solution. Inadequate scenario representation
may lead to suboptimal or misleading outcomes. This is especially critical in multistage
stochastic programming, where the number of scenarios grows exponentially with the
number of stages. As emphasized by Birge and Louveaux (2011), efficient SGR is necessary
to maintain computational tractability while preserving key characteristics of uncertainty.

In this study, uncertainty is primarily driven by site-specific demand, electricity prices,
VRES availability, intraday market acceptance, and reserve market activation. Given
the exponential growth in scenarios, it is computationally infeasible to represent the full
operational lifetime of the technologies, under uncertainty, at an hourly resolution with
one day per stage. Therefore, representative sampling and clustering are used to reduce
dimensionality without losing relevant information.

To address this, a historical data analysis is performed, evaluating electricity prices and
volumes across the day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets, as well as the cross-market
interdependencies relevant for activation and acceptance modeling. Based on observed
patterns, it is considered reasonable to cluster the dataset into two broader seasons --
summer (April - September) and winter (October - March) -- as other seasonal variations
are relatively minor. The underlying analysis is presented in Appendix C. To ensure an
unbiased comparison, a random clustering of the full year is also included.

To investigate research question No. 1, four distinct SGR methods are applied:

    · Random: daily realizations are sampled randomly from the full year without con-

                                                         47
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

       straints.

    · Seasonal: random sampling from the summer-cluster in the upper half of the sce-
       nario tree, and winter in the bottom half.

    · Concentrated: keeps seasonal clustering and includes a "filter" to reduce variability
       across sampled child nodes at each branch, based on daily peak demand.

    · Diversified: enforces diversity by sampling child nodes that include high, low, and
       average load, explicitly increasing variability.

All SGRs systematically include peak loads in the scenario tree to preserve sufficient
representation of the range of realizations -- particularly relevant for smaller scenario trees
with limited branching. A simplified representation of the SGR algorithms is illustrated
in Figure 16.

                  Figure 16: Illustration of the SGRs applied in the case study.

The Concentrated SGR is designed to sample data based on on-site MT heat demand, as it
directly and significantly affects the required on-site capacity, and also correlates well with
LT and electricity demand. It assumes that industrial actors can reasonably forecast the
next day's demand profile. For each child node, one representative day is selected based
on daily peak demand, assuming a consistent shape and magnitude scaling. One child
node is first sampled and the remaining are restricted to lie within ±10% of this value,
imposing a relative constraint to reflect limited short-term uncertainty. This approach
balances local predictability with global variability across the tree, as all stages remain
statistically independent.

The motivation for this approach stems from the hypothesis that unconstrained sampling
-- such as in the Random and Seasonal SGRs -- can introduce unrealistic variability
among the realizations of child nodes at each branching point. This can potentially result
in overly robust investment solutions, as the model must make non-anticipative parent-
node decisions (e.g. day-ahead commitments). Consequently, it may be forced to invest
in more capacity to handle the large variations. By constraining local variations, the Con-
centrated SGR may better reflect operational reality under a cost-minimization approach.

                                                         48
Master's Thesis                                             TIØ4905

The resulting scenario tree structure is shown in Figure 17c, where similar colors represent
Concentrated samples within the dataset.

Although the hypothesis aligns from an operational point of view, its impact on the optimal
solution and effect on long-term investment decisions remain unclear.

To evaluate this, the Diversified SGR is included to ensure variability in each branch by
capturing high- and low-demand realizations drawn from the full dataset, as shown in
Figure 17d. Although this may be less realistic for short-term forecasting, it serves as
a useful benchmark for comparison. While the Diversified SGR is particularly relevant
for two-stage formulations, where the full uncertainty range must be represented within a
single stage, the SGR remains a valuable reference in this multi-stage setting for assessing
the impact on the overall solution.

All SGRs applied in this case study combine sampling and simulation to ensure realistic
and representative input data. The sampling captures electricity prices, renewable gen-
eration profiles, and relevant reserve market prices and volumes primarily based on NO1
data in 2024. To preserve temporal autocorrelation within each day, entire days consisting
of 24 consecutive time steps are sampled. Furthermore, cross-correlation is preserved by
sampling the different parameters from the same day and hour in the dataset, thereby
reflecting the interdependencies between them. Regardless of SGR, all nodes within a
given stage are assigned the same probability factors, meaning that the total probability
across all nodes in each stage sums up to one.

Simulation is used to generate reserve market activation and intraday acceptance param-
eters, based on predefined rules. While the binary acceptance parameters are simulated
more randomly, the simulation algorithm for reserve market activation factors has the
following rules:

                                                         49
Master's Thesis                      TIØ4905

   1. No simultaneous up- and down-regulation activation within the same node and time
       step.

   2. At least one activation must occur among the child nodes for each parent node at
       every time step, representing the inherent risk of activation.

The pseudo code for the simulation algorithms is provided in Appendix D.

sector's HT demand is assumed met by CH4, which is a commonly used fuel in processes

Electricity market prices and volumes are based on 2024 NO1 data, with daily values
sampled per node according to the SGRs in Section 9.1. Day-ahead prices and mFRR

As full intraday data is unavailable, a representative seven-day period (March 28-April 3,

are penalized with costs of 250 EUR/MWh for import and 50 EUR/MWh for export.
These penalties correspond to the 99th percentile of the maximum combined compensation
from capacity and activation markets for up- and down-regulation, respectively. The
approach aims to discourage arbitrage while preserving real-time flexibility.

9.5 Deterministic case setup

To properly address the value of using stochastic programming, two deterministic instances
are run and evaluated in the modeling framework: Expected values solution (EEV), and
a deterministic alternative tested on the full dataset.

9.5.1 Expected value solution

The value of the stochastic solution (VSS) measures the value of explicitly including uncer-
tainty compared to a deterministic approach with expected values (Birge and Louveaux,
2011). To construct the expected value (EV) problem for the scenario tree, all scenarios
are replaced by a single scenario, holding the expected realizations of the random vari-
ables across the initial scenarios. The first-stage solution of the EV is further tested in

                                                         59
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

the stochastic model, resulting in the expected value of the EV solution (EEV). The VSS
is then calculated as the difference between the EEV and the stochastic problem (SP).

To properly address the VSS, the EV problem must be constructed on reasonable terms.
As the acceptance and activation parameters are binary, these complicates the construc-
tion of a representative EV problem. Direct expectation of binary parameters yields
fractional values, which lack the intended dynamics by modeling average activation rather
than discrete outcomes as described in Section 8.1. Other approaches, such as constant
activation, zero activation, or rounding the fractional values were considered. However,
as the resulting cost component for market participation is assumed dominated by load
shedding, the direct expectation is used for simplicity.

The VSS is calculated using the reference tree, collapsing 6,250 scenarios into a single
one. Applying the same approach to individual SGRs could reveal how VSS scales with
problem size and scenario tree structure. However, this is left for further work.

9.5.2 Deterministic alternative

The deterministic alternative is tested with full sequential foresight of the entire dataset,
with each day modeled as a stage incorporated as a single scenario.

Due to its heuristic nature, this setup is not used to calculate the value of incorporating
stochasticity. Instead, it serves to illustrate how a deterministic solution tested on the full
dataset performs compared to a stochastic solution based only on representative scenario
characteristics. Therefore, the comparison is indicative rather than directly comparable
in equal terms.

10.3.4 Comparative analysis of the aluminum industry

There are notable differences between the results for the pulp and paper and the aluminum
industry. Aluminum shows significantly better out-of-sample stability in all instances,
largely due to differences in specific cost components, as demonstrated in Table 19.

Since the aluminum industry has minimal LT and MT demand relative to pulp and paper,
the first-stage decision has less impact on the overall solution and out-of-sample stability,
provided that load shedding is avoided. With both electricity and CH4 (HT demand)
supplied through pre-installed grids, the first-stage decision accounts for only 0.49%, on
average, of the out-of-sample objective value.

In contrast, the pulp and paper industry relies heavily on LT and MT capacity, with first-
stage decisions representing 12.24% (on average) of the objective value out-of-sample.
Consequently, this increases potential variability in out-of-sample evaluations. Insufficient
investments in such cases result in costly load shedding, while aluminum's reliance on the
pre-installed grid enhances its stability. Additionally, the more volatile demand profile in
pulp and paper shifts its cost shares away from the day-ahead and toward other OPEX
components, as gas boilers are frequently used during peak hours.

Both industries benefit from reserve market participation under the given assumptions,
but differ in electricity storage investments. Due to its volatile demand, pulp and paper
find electricity storage more cost beneficial, with an average power capacity investment of
9.50 MW, compared to 5.62 MW for aluminum. The aluminum industry, characterized
with relatively stable demand throughout the year, has less incentive to invest in such
on-site storage for operational peak shaving.

Although aluminum faces stricter hourly load-shifting constraints, its larger scale and
ability to shift loads across the entire day reduce reliance on storage, as load-shifting
alone can support reserve market participation. Additionally, limited TSO procurement
volumes in reserve markets reduce the value of adding more flexibility. For pulp and paper,
storage is more beneficial for peak shaving, price arbitrage, and lowering grid tariffs --
justifying the higher capacity investment. Power-to-heat technologies and thermal storage
further support reserve market participation by enabling heat demand shifting.

11 Discussion of methodology and assumptions

This section discusses key insights and assumptions, and their implications and limitations
within the modeling framework, structured into categorized subsections.

11.1 Modeling assumptions and limitations

To properly address the problem statement, the framework prioritizes stochastic scalability
over detailed process modeling. The model is therefore kept fully linear -- avoiding binary
variables and non-linear relations (see Section 5.1) -- to ensure computational tractability
and allow testing across multiple instances. As a result, investment costs and efficiencies
are modeled as linear and constant, respectively. This implies that investment costs scale
proportionally, omitting economies of scale, and technologies operate at fixed efficiency
regardless of load. In some instances, this leads to minor, impractical investment levels,
which could be avoided by introducing binary variables for fixed costs upon investment or
piecewise linear cost structures.

For the same reasons, conversion technologies are operated across their full capacity range
without penalizing deviation from the design point, which may overstate the economic po-
tential and performance of technologies. Furthermore, load shifting possibility is proposed
penalized with a cost of down-shifting, which, without site-specific input data may over- or
underestimate costs, wear and tear on equipment, and production fulfillment. The same
applies to the cost of degradation, which could more accurately represent actual costs by
incorporating cyclic behavior. However, as the capacities are endogenous variables in this
framework, this would imply non-linear relations. The model also omits a multi-horizon
structure to preserve stochastic scalability, at the expense of directly capturing evolv-
ing strategic uncertainty (see Section 5.1.1). These simplifications highlight the trade-off
between detailed process modeling and scalability under uncertainty.

Another trade-off concerns the representation of ramping and the distinction between
power and energy delivered per time step, reflecting the average power output. Capturing
realistic ramp rates requires shorter time steps to avoid making the ramping constraints
redundant. It could also allow technology compatibility mapping towards response time
in certain reserve markets. Alternatively, ramping can be represented by limiting energy
output per time step to account for ramping duration with less energy supply, which could
be implemented using the same constraints. However, this is hypothesized to have limited
impact, as technologies can either ramp before operation or slightly oversize capacity to
bypass the constraint -- particularly for gas boilers which are cost-effective and show rapid
fluctuation. The same logic applies to downward ramping.

Since both costs and efficiencies depend on the specific site and system configuration, in-
corporating site-specific cost estimates or higher temperature resolution in heat demand
could improve the accuracy of the results. As generalized technologies are applied in this
case study, more accurate and site-specific initialization could improve the value for in-
dustrial decision makers. The results indicate that hot water tanks are viable for heat

                                                         79
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

storage, supporting the potential for power-to-heat sector coupling, in line with findings
from Berg et al. (2024). As the case study omits a complete mapping of available tech-
nologies, it is hypothesized that other technologies such as MT heat storage, discussed in
Beck et al. (2021), could further improve system performance. Given increasing volatility
in electricity prices, this trend is assumed to persist.

11.2 Flexibility, technologies, and market participation

To ensure recourse and feasible solutions, the model incorporates real-time adjustment in
the in-sample test and load shedding in the out-of-sample test. Real-time adjustments
allow actors to deviate from their obligations, which must be the case in real-life set-
tings (Ottesen, 2017). To discourage speculation on reserve market arbitrage, the cost of
real-time imbalance is set sufficiently high to mitigate such behavior, as discussed in Sec-
tion 9.3.3. Notably, if the penalty becomes sufficiently high, it may be beneficial within
the model to dump excess energy on-site to meet market commitments. Although not
included in the study, a side effect of this could be the emerging potential for small-scale
H2-production on site. An instance tested with nonzero export compensation suggest
potential for additional revenue streams through H2 under the current assumptions. How-
ever, this assumes continuous grid access for export, whereas in practice H2 may require
storage, supporting the decision to exclude it and focus on other system dynamics. In
real life, consumers are discouraged to speculate on prices as they remain unknown until
after operation. Therefore, an interesting extension would be to compare behavior under
reserve market participation with a fixed imbalance cost (as in this thesis) versus a setup
without reserve participation, using the mFRR price plus imbalance fee as the imbalance
cost (see Section 3.1).

One possible refinement would be to allow real-time adjustment at the dynamic price when
activation does not occur -- and vice versa using binary variables. The hypothesis is that
this would encourage greater use of electricity-based technologies to meet demand, as it
increases operational flexibility within each scenario branch without relying on weighted
commitments decided up-front. The results indicate that commitments often reflect a
conservative choice between the lower and higher values in each branch, incentivizing other
technologies (such as gas boilers) as flexibility measures, as this is more cost-efficient than
the imbalance price. This effect becomes particularly prominent when the sampling values
are highly volatile, as in the case study where heat demand profiles are generated using the
framework of Holmes et al. (2025). This results in electricity-based technologies covering
base-load, and more flexible (under the assumption of up-front commitment decisions)
technologies covering peak loads. A valuable extension to the work would be to assess the
impact of sampling from more stable demands.

Intraday trading offers limited value in the case study, primarily due to restricted accessi-
bility. With only 20% of market volume assumed available and limited access to historical
data, as described in Section 9.3.3, the actual potential may be underestimated. More-
over, binary acceptance parameters with uncertain realization of consistent commitments
make strategic bidding difficult. As a result, intraday trading yields minimal cost sav-

                                                         80
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

ings or operational flexibility. None of the interviewed industrial actors reported current
participation in the intraday market, but with anticipated increase in price volatility and
imbalance costs, trading volumes in closer-to-real-time markets could rise. However, the
impact on model outcomes remains uncertain.

Further, load shedding in the out-of-sample is strongly penalized, reflecting the premise of
always meeting demand. The load shedding cost highly impacts the volatility in the out-of-
sample test, as discussed in Section 10. However, this is justified to properly distinguish
instances with unmet load. Although the case study used a higher load shedding cost
than proposed in many studies (e.g. Cambridge Economic Policy Associates Ltd (2018)),
the cost is set sufficiently high to prevent the model from treating load shedding in late-
stage low-probability nodes as a cost-effective strategy. This ensures that load shedding
significantly impacts the objective value and is only used for complete recourse reasons,
since the on-site capacity is fixed in the out-of-sample evaluation.

The assumptions of risk neutrality and price-taker perspective may yield optimistic results,
not reflecting the weighted preference for participation in different markets. As industry
discussions indicate that maintaining production and mass balance is a top priority (see
Section 3.1), incorporating varying risk profiles could add value. Industrial actors may
prefer the more predictable day-ahead market, while the price-taker assumption might
provide an upper estimate of revenue from reserve market participation. If the actors
behaved as a price-setter, their bids could lower the market-clearing price and reduce
actual income. On the other hand, conversation with industrial actors suggests they expect
infrequent activation and therefore submit consistently larger bids than the optimization
model would suggest under the assumption of constant activation risk -- aligning with
operational uncertainty as discussed in Section 9.1.

Moreover, the framework incorporates realization of reserve market activation using binary
activation parameters, rather than explicit modeling of bids in the activation market. To
avoid tactical bidding without risk of activation, the input data reflect a risk of activation
in each time step. In real life, bidders submit an activation bid following the capacity bid,
where tactical bids can reduce the risk of activation. Consequently, the input data could
overestimate the economic benefit of activation, as the risk of activation could be higher
than for tactical real-life bidders.

11.3 Scenario generation and uncertainty representation

The scenario generation is based on the underlying dataset, containing historical prices and
volumes primarily from 2024, along with simulated intraday prices. An important remark
is that this not necessarily represent all possible realizations. Volatile prices and shorter
time resolutions in markets may lead to more extreme prices, resulting in other dynamics
and model behavior. Levin et al. (2023) argues that many studies underestimate the value
of flexibility by overlooking potential future increase in price volatility and carbon costs.
By incorporating greater volatility into the dataset, we hypothesize that constructing more
sophisticated SGRs becomes prominent to capture this variability, while utilizing flexibility

                 81
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

by e.g., price arbitrage and larger reserve market commitments, which potentially could
give other results.

The SGRs are clustered based on MT demand, due to its impact on the invested capacity
(see Section 9.1). Other parameters are omitted in this thesis, and it is uncertain how
this would impact the results. With higher volatility, clustering on electricity prices could
be viable. Moreover, clustering on VRES generation by ensuring variable availability
in each sample could be interesting, in addition to clustering on multiple parameters
simultaneously. Another interesting aspect would be to vary node-probabilities within
each stage to reflect low-probability events, such as production stops. In such cases,
the industrial actor may need to manage unexpectedly high electricity volume purchases
through real-time adjustments or alternative sinks such as storage.

The SGRs will, when based on random sampling, potentially produce a large variety of
tree configurations due to the high number of combinatorial outcomes. This increases the
variability, especially for smaller trees, where randomness dominates the representation.
This is further highlighted in the results. In contrast, stratified approaches like Seasonal
and Diversified impose structure that limits such configuration variety and consequently
reduces the risk of extreme outliers. However, methods like Concentrated may remain
highly sensitive to such randomness, as the systematic sampling approach potentially
makes it even more vulnerable to outlier effects in both directions, an effect particularly
evident in the results for the Wide-Concentrated structure.

As discussed in Section 9.2, the reference tree is clustered using the Seasonal SGR and has a
relatively balanced scenario tree structure. This design is used to most accurately represent
the actual uncertainty and capture the characteristics of the dataset. However, this could
possibly cause biases, especially for the Balanced-Seasonal instance configurations in the
out-of-sample test. As this configurations has a relatively similar design, but smaller, it
could potentially have benefits in the reference tree. However, this is uncertain, as the
result doesn't show any clear correlation. Backe et al. (2021) raises similar questions,
but finds worse performance for the SGR used in the reference tree than the others.
Thus, the study suggest that other sophisticated sampling methods may outperform the
reference SGR. This thesis support their findings, as Wide-Seasonal performs best among
all configurations in the Large size.

Finally, the model doesn't account for long-term stationary uncertainty, including de-
velopments in prices, weather conditions and regulatory policies. Section 3.3 points at
measures such as grid tariff changes and flexibility incentives, which could impact the
results. Furthermore, developments in carbon prices is identified as important, as higher
OPEX reduces the incentive for reserve market participation, as discussed in Section 10.4.
Jerez et al. (2015) further points at changing solar radiation, directly linked to the PV
potential. The study estimates a change in the range of -14 to +2% by the end of the
century, compared to today's conditions.

12 Conclusion

This thesis adopts a multi-stage stochastic program to analyze how uncertainty representa-
tion influences investment decisions in integrated industrial energy systems. The analysis
includes participation in the day-ahead, intraday, and reserve markets, while assuming
constant prices for other fuels. A case study of a pulp and paper facility and an aluminum
plant is conducted, providing insights into how uncertainty affects solutions in industries
with contrasting characteristics.

Firstly, the value of the stochastic solution is evaluated. This analysis shows that neglect-
ing uncertainty, particularly through the expected value problem, leads to a significant
underestimation in variability and, consequently, costly load shedding. The stochastic
problem, by contrast, yield solutions that better capture variability across realizations,
supporting the hypothesis that modeling with uncertainty improves the investment deci-
sion under varying market and industrial conditions.

Further, a dummy case is included in order to assess whether an optimization framework
yields more cost-efficient investment decisions. When tested out-of-sample, the dummy
solution highlights clear shortcomings by ignoring system interdependencies such as load
shifting, sector coupling of power and heat, storage integration, fuel switching, spot-price
arbitrage, and multi-market participation. These absences lead to suboptimal system
performance, underscoring the value of an optimization-based approach for complex in-
tegrated energy systems. In the case study, the stochastic problem achieves an objective
value 35% lower than the dummy solution in the out-of-sample evaluation.

A central part of the study focuses on how variation in representation of uncertainty affects
the solution quality and stability. This involves varying size and structure of the scenario
tree, as well as different scenario generation routines, designed to either randomly or
systematically sample data while preserving temporal autocorrelation and cross-correlation
of the stochastic processes.

In terms of stability, smaller scenario trees (100 scenarios) are generally found insuffi-
cient to cover the variability in the underlying dataset, often underestimating the required
capacity for on-site conversion technologies. However, if smaller scenario trees are nec-
essary, e.g. for computational reasons, a diversified scenario generation routine improves
stability, but may lead to overestimation of capacity in larger trees (500-1000 scenarios)
with respect to other routines introduced in the case study.

Conversely, for larger scenario trees, more systematic and targeted scenario generation
routines outperforms random sampling approaches. When having sufficient number of
scenarios, these routines better reflect the true variability of the dataset without either
over- or underestimating the needed capacity on-site, supporting the hypothesis that limit-
ing local variability prevents overinvestment. Overall, medium-sized trees (500 scenarios)
are found sufficient to achieve out-of-sample stability, while larger trees (1000 scenarios)
offer limited additional improvement.

A particularly interesting observation is that the in-sample investment decision has lim-

                                                         83
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

ited impact on the out-of-sample objective value, provided sufficient capacity is installed
to avoid load shedding, as cost related to grid expansion are neglected in the case study.
This suggests that the value of such optimization framework is highest in industries with
significant heat demand and high variability -- traits more characteristic of the pulp and
paper sector. In this sector, first-stage decisions accounts for an average of 12% of the
total objective value, compared to only 0.5% in the aluminum sector. The aluminum
plant exhibit stable performance even with Small scenario trees, reflecting its lower opera-
tional uncertainty. The above highlights that uncertainty might have greater influence on
short-term operational decisions rather than long-term investment strategies, especially in
systems with stable demand. Further research is however needed to explore this distinction
in greater depth.

A consistent and notable finding is the strong economic reason for reserve market partic-
ipation. Across all configurations, reserve market revenues remain positive, suggesting a
clear incentive for flexible industries to engage in ancillary services. Storage investments,
especially when linked to reserve markets, also prove economically viable, indicating that
industries considering energy storage have a financial motivation to optimize both energy-
and power capacity with this in mind. However, the value of reserve market participation
is highly site-specific. As operational costs increase, the same reserve market revenues
become less attractive, increasing the relative benefit of using flexibility for on-site peak
shaving instead. Thus, reserve market participation should be evaluated in light of op-
erational expenditures, which inherently affects the threshold for profitable engagement.
Moreover, the attractiveness of such markets depends on future prices and volumes relative
to the investment cost of flexibility, which are uncertain.

Lastly, the sensitivity analysis on carbon costs and technology prices reveal how optimal
solutions change with varying cost assumptions. Under 2025 technology costs and low
to expected carbon prices, the model favors electric boilers and heat pumps to supply
baseload, with conventional gas boilers handling peaks. By 2050, the dispatch order
changes. Heat pumps and CCS-equipped gas boilers serve baseload, followed by electric
boilers, with conventional gas boilers retained for peaks. This emphasizes the strategic
value of fuel switching in integrated energy systems. Notably, the model consistently
select natural gas (CH4) for CCS units and biogas for conventional gas boilers when CCS
is economically viable, underscoring both the cost-effectiveness of fuel switching, as well
as the competitiveness of natural gas in a decarbonizing system with CCS integration.

In conclusion, this thesis demonstrates that a multi-stage stochastic optimization model
offers insights that enable integrated industrial energy systems to make more robust, flex-
ible, and economically sound investment decisions. By explicitly modeling uncertainty
and system interdependencies, the framework captures value that deterministic or rule-
of-thumb approaches ignore. While the results are influenced by site-specific conditions,
scenario generation, and technology assumptions, the overarching conclusion remains clear.
Accounting for uncertainty significantly improves the decision quality, particularly in in-
tegrated energy systems characterized by high flexibility and variability.

13 Future research

Building on the findings, limitations and modeling framework of this thesis, several promis-
ing directions for future research are identified, including both methodological refinements
and broader system-level implications.

While this thesis prioritizes stochastic scalability in line with the problem statement and
captures key interdependencies within a multi-market and integrated energy system, future
work should explore the value of incorporating more technically detailed representations
of key components to better support industrial investment decisions. In particular, inte-
grating a unit commitment formulation would enable more realistic modeling of industry-
specific operational constraints, such as minimum rest periods, process-related inflexibility,
and technology-specific flexibility, including bottom-up load-shifting potential.

Additionally, a more advanced and potentially non-linear representation of technology
features like storage degradation, cyclic behavior, and minimum investment thresholds
should be considered to more accurately capture operational and economic implications.
A comprehensive technology mapping and the inclusion of multiple reserve markets could
further enhance the value of the framework. Finally, evaluating eligibility of technologies
across different markets under detailed operational constraints could offer deeper insight
into their impact on investment choices and system performance.

Following this, the value of the framework could be further enhanced by systematically
integrating bottom-up unit modeling into a well-established energy modeling platform.
This would support more robust validation and promote future reuse of the methodology
and insights. As highlighted in Section 6, the EMX framework is well-suited for this
purpose, offering a compatible modeling environment for incorporating the findings of this
thesis -- especially by incorporating reserve market modeling as an extension in EMX.

Existing research on coordinated multi-market participation often reduces complexity by
collapsing operational stages while keeping market decisions as separate stages or, con-
versely, consolidating markets while preserving operational stages. As discussed in Sec-
tion 3.1, a practical simplification for industrial actors operating under BRP deadlines is
to collapse day ahead, intraday, and capacity reserve bidding into a single stage. Alterna-
tively, a sequential approach could first decide capacity commitments, followed by separate
bidding stages for day-ahead, intraday and reserve market activation bids, based on un-
folding realizations. Evaluating the consequence and impact on long-term investments of
collapsing all markets into one stage could be a valuable extension of this thesis.

Future research should provide a more comprehensive evaluation of investment decisions
across various potential market trajectories, given the inherent uncertainty in market
development. This should incorporate anticipated trends in energy markets, including
examination of price scenarios with greater volatility and differing activation patterns. A
promising direction of further research is the impact of the transition to a quarter-hourly
resolution in the imbalance settlement period and intraday market, with the day-ahead
market set to follow in September, as outlined in Section 2.2. The shift to this format,

Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

along with evolving price and volume patterns, may influence investment strategies.

The expected shift in trading volumes from the day-ahead market toward mFRR EAM,
driven by the Nordic integration into the European balancing platform (see Section 2.2.3),
also calls for an assessment of its implications on long-term investment strategies. Future
work should further consider the inclusion of a multi-horizon approach to better consider
multiple investment periods to assess how this flexibility impacts overall decision-making.
Identifying the threshold where investments remain viable, balancing expected market-
driven prices and volumes against developments in technology prices could add further
value. Site-specific investment costs and efficiencies resulting from process integration
may further impact the optimal technology mix.

Furthermore, future research should examine how climate quotas and emission trading
schemes influence investment decisions. Conducting sensitivity analyses on technology
prices, efficiencies, and regulatory frameworks could help identify the conditions under
which certain technologies become economically viable. An interesting extension could
be to include emission quota allocation in the model to see how investments are affected
when quotas either exceed emissions, offering additional revenues streams, or are more
restrictive, driving investments toward low-carbon technologies.

Another interesting area of future research is the impact of political and regulatory
changes, such as Statnett's proposed tariff design changes (Statnett, 2025a), on investment
decisions, energy market participation, and the value of flexibility. This could provide valu-
able insights into how evolving regulatory incentives can promote investments in flexible
assets more effectively compared to the static grid tariff considered in this thesis. Further-
more, the trade-off between combining flexible investments with connection on terms to
reduce reliance on grid expansion should be systematically compared in site-specific cases.

Finally, expanding the perspective beyond strategic investments focused on short-term
uncertainty, the future outlook for Norwegian industry actors is shaped by geopolitical
developments, competitiveness in their core business areas, shifts in energy- and climate
policies, and technological breakthroughs. Statnett (2025b) highlights the evolving balance
between supply and demand, emerging industries, and the role of alternative fuels as key
factors for the coming years, all serving as important research areas for industrial actors.

B Market actors and roles in the Norwegian power system

Nord Pool
After the deregulation of the Norwegian power market in 1991, Statnett Marked started
trading in the spot market. Cross-boarder power trading was established through the
Norwegian-Swedish power exchange in 1996, introducing the name Nord Pool (Nord Pool,
2020). Statnett still holds an 11% ownership in the exchange through their ownership
TSO Holding. Today, Nord Pool is licensed by RME to organize the power exchange
for the day-ahead and intraday markets across 16 European countries, offering trading,
clearing, settlement and associated services. The dynamics in the different markets are
further described in Section 2.2.

Regulator
As discussed in Section 2.1.1, RME regulates the natural monopolies for the TSO and
DSOs in the power grid. Their main objective is to ensure reliable and efficient energy
markets across all levels while promoting developments that serve societal and environ-
mental interests. This includes managing network revenues, market access, network tariffs,
market conduct and ensuring efficient operation (NVE, 2024d).

TSO
TSOs has a natural monopoly on operating the transmission system. In Norway, the state
enterprise Statnett operates as the TSO, holding the responsibility for building, operating
and maintaining the Norwegian power system. Statnett aims to ensure reliable power
supply, and is responsible for maintaining balance in the power system through interaction
with power producers, retailers and other TSOs (Statnett, 2025c). The Nordic imbalance
settlement is calculated by eSett Oy, ensuring that each market actor is paid and invoiced
correctly (eSett Oy, 2025). Statnett also manages congestion revenues occurring from
physical transmission limitations called bottlenecks, resulting in price differences between
bidding zones or countries (NVE, 2024b). As Statnett's income is regulated, these revenues
are returned to customers through reduced grid tariffs. Moreover, flexibility can limit the
pressure on these bottlenecks, contributing to more stable prices across bidding areas.

DSO
The DSOs are grid companies responsible for operating and owning the regional and
distribution grids in a geographical area. As there is no need for multiple grids covering the
same geographical area, the DSOs are natural monopolies in their respective areas under
regulation by NVE. The DSOs are obliged to connect customers on request, as discussed
in Section 3.3.2. The grid companies also sets the grid tariffs, based on regulations from
NVE, which are designed to promote efficient use of resources and cover operation and
maintenance costs (NVE, 2025). Metering and report of production, consumption and
exchange is also part of the DSO responsibility. Regulations mandate that DSOs must
be structurally and functionally separated from competitive activities, restricting them
from acting as BSP in the reserve market or participate in other trading activities (NVE,
2022b). However, local flexibility markets also emerges, where consumer flexibility is
traded between customers and DSOs (Euroflex, 2025). Currently this is only a demo

project, and will not be considered further in this thesis.

Power producers and retailers
Power producers generates and sell electrical energy either via Nord Pool or directly to
retailers, by bidding a volume at a certain price. Retailers consequently purchase and
sell electricity to the consumers through spot exchange, bilateral agreements with power
producers, or through other retailers. Retailers are responsible for balancing purchased
electricity with actual consumption in their portfolio of customers, and therefore holds an
agreement with a BRP (eSett Oy, 2024). In turn, the producers are responsible for produc-
tion balancing while the retailer is responsible for demand balancing, and the consequence
of imbalance is distributed accordingly.

In general, retailers offer three different power purchase agreements to the end users:
fixed-price contracts, contracts with standard variable price, and spot price agreement
with a certain surcharge to the retailer. While the two latter fluctuates with short-term
developments in the power market, fixed price contracts is a type of financial contract
providing the end user price security for the duration of the contract, which could vary.
The supplier is obliged to deliver power at the agreed price, regardless of the fluctuations in
market price. The risk premium for price security is represented by the difference between
the fixed price and the expected market price during the period (ENERGIFAKTANORGE,
2024). Fixed-price contracts will be further discussed from an industry perspective in
Section 3.1.

Consumers and prosumers
As end users in the electricity supply chain, consumers purchase power to cover their
own demand. In general, consumers are divided into the sectors households, business
and industrial consumers. While smaller consumers normally purchase power from the
retailers, larger consumers could also choose to purchase directly from the spot market or
from power producers through bilateral agreements (ENERGIFAKTANORGE, 2024).

With the increasing need for flexibility arising from the increasing share of VRES, pro-
sumers have an important role in the power system. Botelho et al. (2021) defines prosumers
as "a customer who produces electricity primarily for his own needs but can also sell the
excess electricity". Prosumers are therefore connected to the distribution grid, and can
both import and export electricity.

Balance Service Provider and Balance Responsible Party
A Balance Service Provider (BSP) is a market participant holding an agreement with eSett
and the TSO or is bound by terms and conditions set by the TSO. The BSP has reserve-
providing units or reserve-providing groups able to provide balancing services to the TSO.
The Balance Responsible Party (BRP) holds a valid Imbalance Settlement Agreement
with eSett and a Balance Agreement with the TSO. The BRP is obliged to balance supply
and demand on its own behalf or on behalf of other producers, consumers, prosumers, or
traders of electricity (eSett Oy, 2024). The BRP is therefore responsible for the finan-
cial settlement of any arising imbalances. The costs are subsequently distributed to the
units/actors posing the imbalance, and the final settlement is determined based on the
contracual agreements between the BRP, BSP and the regulatory objects, which could

Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

also include surcharge cost for the BRP.

From a reserve market perspective, the BSP is responsible for submitting bids and receiving
settlement for them, while the BRP is responsible for imbalances and trades in the energy
markets. For a resource participating in the reserve markets, these roles can either be
covered by the same entity or by two different entities. Statnett requires that the BSP
must either own the regulating asset itself, or have an agreement with the owner to manage
it. Further, the BSP must be a BRP or have a bilateral agreement for financial settlement
with the BRP accruing to the resource providing flexibility (Statnett, 2025e). Various
reserve markets have different requirements for participation between BSPs and BRP, and
updated terms are currently being developed (Statnett, 2024c).

Aggregator
According to Statnett (2018b), an aggregator is a market participant that combines balanc-
ing loads from multiple consumers or prosumers belonging to the same BRP, for purchase
or auction in any organized electricity market. This is typically done through contracts
between the aggregator and the flexibility vendor, outlining the operational and economi-
cal conditions. Aggregation of loads is typically done to deliver flexibility at a larger scale,
and the aggregator could serve as both BSP and BRP, or as a contracted independent ag-
gregator. Ottesen (2017) describes the aggregator role as an intermediate between smaller
prosumers and the market, typically aiming to maximize profits by trading in multiple
markets on behalf of their flexibility portfolio. This both provides market access for the
smaller prosumers, and offers increased volume of flexibility to the market.

Flexibility service providers
In addition to the actors described above, holding physical positions in the market actions,
several companies are offering flexibility services and energy management systems, aiming
to support and optimize the energy use at both the consumer/prosumer, DSO and TSO
level. Some examples are TGN Energy (2025) and °A Entelios (2025).

    activation coverage at each timestep across child nodes

3 for t  1 to ntimesteps do

7   Randomly shuffle Ap(t);
