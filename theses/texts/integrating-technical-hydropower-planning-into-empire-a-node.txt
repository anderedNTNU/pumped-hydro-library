Abstract

The EU has set ambitious climate goals towards 2050 to mitigate the negative effects of human-induced
greenhouse gas emissions and transition to a decarbonized European power system. To aid policymakers
in this transition, long-term power system optimization models are frequently used to explore optimal
pathways for capacity expansion in the European power system. Hydropower is predicted to play a
vital role in the future power system because of its enormous potential for providing flexibility services.
Therefore, it is crucial for such models to fully capture the intricacies of hydropower planning.
The European Model for Power system Investments with Renewable Energy (EMPIRE) is one such
power system model. Its objective is to minimize the total investments- and annualized operational sys-
tem costs until 2060. In the original EMPIRE model, the potential of hydropower is not adequately
represented as each season is modeled independently, preventing inter-seasonal storage. This master's
thesis seeks to enhance the tactical hydropower formulation of the model. The enhancement involves
leveraging a node formulation to facilitate an inter-seasonal connection, enabling the model to transfer
information between seasons while incorporating a degree of uncertainty. This improves the model's
ability to capture the dynamic interactions between different seasons and better represent the complex-
ities of the power system. The inclusion of inter-seasonal connection involves creating a new type
of storage which consists of variables such as reservoir level, hydropower production, charging to the
reservoir through pumped hydro, inflow, and spillage.
Through extensive testing, it is demonstrated that the enhanced EMPIRE formulation has significantly
improved the model's tactical hydropower management. By interconnecting the seasons, the model
can now fully utilize hydropower's flexibility. This improvement is evident in the model's objective
value, which is reduced by approximately 5%. Furthermore, the reliance on non-renewable generation,
particularly Open Cycle Gas Turbine (OCGT), is decreased. The new hydropower storage balance more
accurately reflects real-world hydropower dynamics, showing a promising trend of storing excess energy
for periods of high demand.

Sammendrag

EU har satt ambisiøse klimama°l for 2050 for a° redusere de negative effektene av menneskeskapte klima-
gassutslipp og sikre at Europas kraftsystem i hovedsak besta°r av fornybar energi. For a° hjelpe beslut-
ningstakere med denne overgangen benyttes langsiktige kraftmarkeds-optimeringsmodeller for a° sikre
at sa° riktige som mulig beslutninger blir tatt. Vannkraft er spa°dd a° spille en viktig rolle i det fremtidige
kraftsystemet pa° grunn av sitt enorme potensial for lagring og evne til a° tilby fleksibilitet. Derfor er
det avgjørende at slike optimeringsmodeller fanger opp vannkraftens egenskaper fullt ut, slik at den kan
modelleres best mulig og tilby den fleksibiliteten den har i den virkelige verden.
The European Model for Power system Investments with Renewable Energy (EMPIRE) er et eksem-
pel pa° en slik kraftmarkedsmodell. Ma°let med modellen er a° minimere investeringskostnader og de
totale a°rlige operasjonelle kostnadene i det europeiske energisystemet frem til 2060. I den opprinnelige
EMPIRE-modellen blir ikke potensialet til vannkraft tilstrekkelig ivaretatt, ettersom hver sesong mod-
elleres uavhengig, noe som forhindrer lagring over flere sesonger. For a° forbedre dette forsøker denne
masteroppgaven a° endre den taktiske vannkraftformuleringen i modellen. Forbedringen innebærer a°
benytte en nodeformulering som muliggjør en forbindelse mellom alle sesongene innad i et a°r, slik
at modellen kan overføre informasjon mellom sesonger og samtidig ha en viss grad av usikkerhet i den
operasjonelle delen av modellen. Dette øker modellens evne til a° bedre representere kraftsystemets kom-
pleksiteter. Inkluderingen av lagring mellom sesonger innebærer a° lage en ny lagerbalanse som besta°r av
variabler som reservoarniva°, vannkraftproduksjon, pumping til reservoaret gjennom pumpekraft, tilsig
og flomtap.
Omfattende testing av den nye formuleringen viser at den har forbedret modellens taktiske vannkraft-
splanlegging betydelig. Ved a° koble sammen sesongene kan modellen fullt ut utnytte vannkraftens
fleksibilitet. Denne forbedringen er tydelig i modellens objektivverdi, som er redusert med omtrent 5%.
Videre er avhengigheten av ikke-fornybar produksjon, spesielt gassturbiner med a°pen syklus (OCGT),
redusert. Den nye vannkraftlagringsbalansen gjenspeiler mer nøyaktig virkelighetens vannkraftsplan-
legging, og viser en lovende trend med a° lagre overskuddsenergi til perioder med høy etterspørsel.

Introduction

In the past decades, the dominance of non-renewable energy sources has shaped the global energy sys-
tem. This heavy reliance on fossil fuels has brought adverse impacts across the environmental, eco-
nomic, and social domains worldwide. The extraction and combustion of resources like coal, oil, and
natural gas unleash significant volumes of greenhouse gases, hastening the pace of climate change. Con-
sequently, a heightened occurrence of extreme weather events, rising sea levels, and ecosystem disrup-
tions are witnessed. In response to these challenges, governments worldwide are increasingly shifting
towards renewable energy sources, aligning with ambitious targets such as the EU's 2030 Climate Target
Plan. This initiative aims to cut greenhouse gas emissions by at least 55% below 1990 levels. Looking
ahead to 2050, the European Commission wants to achieve climate neutrality, necessitating a more am-
bitious and cost-efficient path. Through policies incentivizing renewable adoption, increased funding for
clean energy research, and stricter emissions regulations, the energy system is gradually steered away
from non-renewable sources. The main goal is to reduce harm to the environment, create more jobs
in eco-friendly industries, and kickstart worldwide efforts to have energy sources that are better for the
planet [European Environment Agency, 2024].

Expanding clean energy sources offers environmental benefits, but their intermittent nature poses chal-
lenges for the energy system. Solar and wind power rely on weather conditions, making their output
unpredictable. As their share grows, power system planning becomes less reliable. To address this,
storing surplus energy during peak production is vital. Storage offers flexibility and reliability in energy
supply, bridging gaps between high and low generating periods. It optimizes renewable energy utiliza-
tion, reducing the need for backup from fossil fuels. Developing storage technologies is crucial for a
sustainable energy future.

In today's energy landscape, various methods for storing energy exist. This thesis specifically focuses on
the analysis of hydro reservoirs, including pumped hydro systems. Hydro reservoirs serve as long-term
energy storage systems, and understanding the effect of this storage type is crucial for the development
of a fully functional energy system.

To investigate the rapidly changing power system, power system optimization models play a crucial role.

Chapter 1. Introduction

These models are essential for achieving future climate targets while simultaneously keeping costs as low
as possible. They include a range of techniques and technologies aimed at optimizing the generation,
distribution, and consumption of electricity. Given the complexity of the model and data availability,
simplification of data representation is necessary. This often involves reducing temporal aggregation to
allow the model to solve the problem within a reasonable timeframe. Embracing optimization methods
is essential for building a sustainable power system capable of meeting the growing energy needs of
society while safeguarding the environment for generations to come.

The European Model for Power system Investments with Renewable Energy (EMPIRE) is a multi-
horizon stochastic optimization tool designed to minimize both investment and operational costs within
the future European power system. Due to the complexity and size of the problem, the model has a
significant drawback in its formulation. It lacks inter-seasonal connections, preventing the transfer of
information from one season to the next. This deficiency significantly impacts the storage balance, par-
ticularly in the case of hydropower. Without inter-seasonal connections, there is no possibility of storing
water for later seasons, and the model loses some of the flexibility this long-term storage provides.
Consequently, this limitation may hinder the utilization of more renewable, unregulated sources while
simultaneously phasing out fossil fuels, due to the unpredictable nature of the unregulated sources.

This master's thesis is partly based on the project report [Ulsaker et al., 2023], which introduced a node
formulation as an alternative to a scenario formulation which is present in the original EMPIRE model.
The node formulation aimed to establish inter-seasonal connections while retaining some level of un-
certainty in the operational stage of the model. Building upon this foundation, this thesis is delving
deeper into and further developing the node formulation. A key aspect of this involves developing
hydropower constraints that facilitate the storage of water for subsequent seasons. This enhances the
tactical (medium-term) hydropower planning within the model, thereby advancing its capabilities in
optimizing power system operations.

This report contains ten chapters, collectively contributing to an extensive analysis of the improved EM-
PIRE formulation. Chapter 2 delves into the background, providing essential context for understanding
the topic. The literature relevant to the study is reviewed in Chapter 3. Chapter 4 introduces the EMPIRE
Model, serving as the theoretical framework for further analysis. In Chapter 5 a problem description is
offered. Chapter 6 presents the mathematical formulation, laying the groundwork for subsequent inves-
tigations. Data collection methodologies are detailed in Chapter 7. Chapter 8 focuses on an enhanced
scenario generation methodology. A computational study is conducted in Chapter 9, providing results
and discussions. Finally, Chapter 10 summarizes the findings and concludes the master's thesis.

2.1 European Power System

The European power system is intricate, consisting of lots of different energy sources. Electricity gen-
erated from various sources is transmitted across long distances through the European power grid to
reach consumers. Presently, the power system is segmented into distinct bidding zones due to transmis-
sion capacities and bottlenecks, resulting in varying electricity prices as illustrated in Figure 2.1. Over
the past decade, there has been a notable increase in the transfer of large quantities of power between
these price zones [Silva-Rodriguez et al., 2022]. Such transfers are enabled either through High Voltage
Alternating Current (HVAC) cables traversing the air or High Voltage Direct Current (HVDC) cables
submerged underwater. Such transfers between price zones lead to more similar prices as zones with
a power surplus can transfer power to those with a deficit. In an ideal case with no congestion, where
there are no limitations on transfer capacities and no losses, the electricity prices would be equal all over
Europe.
The foundation of the electricity prices in the power market rests on forecasts of electricity demand
and production for the following day. The day-ahead market is the most significant portion of the
power market. Given the inherent uncertainties in demand and production forecasts, an intraday mar-
ket exists where power producers and retailers can engage in trading activities throughout the day
[Silva-Rodriguez et al., 2022]. Additionally, various other auction mechanisms are employed, as de-
picted in Figure 2.2. Apart from these markets, certain power producers receive compensation for re-
serving capacity to address regulatory actions, a measure necessitated by forecast uncertainties. Reg-
ulators within the power system are tasked with ensuring that market demand is adequately met. In
the Norwegian power market, this responsibility often falls upon larger hydropower plants capable of
swiftly adjusting power production to maintain system balance [Kirchner, 2022].

2.1.1 Future Trends in the European Power System

To reach the EU's climate neutrality goal in 2050 [European Environment Agency, 2024] the role of
electrical power from renewables will be more important in the years to come. This leads to new chal-
lenges in the power system. The following part highlights some of the key challenges encountered.
The electricity demand in the European market is expected to increase in the coming years
[Silva-Rodriguez et al., 2022]. According to the European Commission, electricity consumption in the
EU is expected to increase by around 60% between 2023 and 2030 [European Comission, 2023]. A key
factor driving this surge in demand is the ongoing transition towards a more sustainable and electrified
economy. An example of this is the electrification of the transport sector and other parts of the industry.
Electrification plays a key role in reaching the goal of climate neutrality in 2050.
Another trend of great importance for the future power system is the projected increase in VRES, such
as wind- and solar power [Silva-Rodriguez et al., 2022]. Already in 2023, the EU saw a record fall in
coal- and gas power production. Meanwhile, renewables reached a 44% share in the electricity mix
making the EU's electricity mix cleaner than ever [Brown and Jones, 2024]. For the first time, wind
power produced more than gas in the EU. However, this change in supply has its disadvantages. The
main one is the intermittent and uncertain nature of it. By increasing the share of renewables in the
electricity mix, the amount of power produced is more dependent on weather conditions than before. A
consequence of this is more frequent and larger variations in the electricity price and even the possibility
of a negative price if there is a big surplus of energy in the system. For example in Norway in the summer
of 2023 the electricity price was negative due to large amounts of solar- and wind power in Europe and
a small demand [Mathisen, 2023]. Figure 2.3 shows a projected European energy mix until 2050.

2.1.2 Solutions to the Future Challenges of the European Power System

As a consequence of future challenges outlined earlier, investments in power production and the capacity
of electricity transfer have to be increased substantially. In addition to the increased capacity of the
supply side, two different solutions are introduced to solve the future challenges. The first solution
involves enhancing the capacity of the European power grid, while the other solution deals with the
utilization of climate-friendly flexibility, with a specific focus on hydropower in Norway.
Increased Capacity in the European Power Grid
Capacity expansion in the European power grid holds numerous potential advantages. First, the in-
creased capacity enables more efficient transmission of electricity across longer distances and between
diverse regions, fundamentally reshaping market dynamics. Moreover, the additional capacity holds the
potential to mitigate transmission bottlenecks and enhance the reliability of electricity supply. Second,
another notable consequence is the ability to harness more VRES, typically generated in regions abun-
dant in natural resources and distributed to consumption centres. This shift towards greater reliance
on VRES from regions with optimal resources promises a smoother and more dependable supply of
renewable energy, ultimately diminishing dependence on fossil fuels. Third, the increased grid capac-
ity is expected to foster a more competitive electricity market, enabling electricity providers access to
cross-border electricity trading and aiding in the harmonization of prices between different regions.
In essence, the increased capacity has the potential to create a more interconnected and sustainable power
system, promoting greater interdependence among European nations. The collaboration will be essen-
tial to meet the collective demand. According to a report by ENTSO-E, countries will need to rely on
each other more than ever through cross-border exchanges with the increased share of renewables in the
electricity mix [ENTSO-E, 2019]. As depicted in Figure 2.4, current and planned sea cables exemplify
this trend. However, along with the benefits come certain drawbacks, namely the financial costs and the
consequences of heightened interdependence. The European Commission states that C584 billion of
investments are necessary to upgrade the ageing European electricity grid [European Comission, 2023].
Outdated grid infrastructure needs to be upgraded to facilitate cross-border exchanges. By linking na-
tions together, the challenges faced by one country can be transferred to others. For instance, a sig-
nificant energy deficit and soaring prices in one country may ripple across borders. The installation of
HVDC connections to Great Britain and Germany sparked considerable debate in Norway, with some
advocating for their decommissioning due to perceived negative impacts on Norwegian electricity prices
[Molnes, 2022].

Importance of Climate-friendly Flexibility
The growing presence of VRES in the European electricity grid underscores the necessity for enhanced
flexibility within the power system. Unlike other energy sources, VRES, such as wind and solar energy,
are non-dispatchable, meaning they cannot generate power consistently. Flexibility in power production
refers to the ability to adjust power output by increasing or decreasing it rapidly. The power system
always has to balance supply and demand, and the demand side will always be uncertain. The European
power system was originally designed based on centralized predictable generation ensuring steady power
flows, but now it has progressively evolved to integrate more decentralized power and more VRES. This
makes the supply of electricity more uncertain and increases the need for sources of flexibility.
Gas- or coal power plants serve as prime examples of flexible generators in the power market today.
These plants can modulate their power output instantaneously based on the amount of fuel input, allow-
ing them to adapt to changing demand or supply rapidly. In the current power market, such generators
cover the peak load, which represents the remaining portion of power demand after the base load has
been met. This concept is depicted in Figure 2.5, where the fluctuations in solar and wind power supply
are contrasted with a typical residential demand profile. Noticeable gaps between supply and demand,
particularly in the morning and evening, must be addressed by employing more flexible energy sources
to maintain system balance.

Chapter 2. Background

                   Figure 2.5: Supply of wind and solar to a regular house [Palmer, 2021]

Nonetheless, coal and gas power plants come with significant drawbacks, including high fuel costs and
carbon emissions, especially when compared to VRES. To achieve the EU's goal of climate neutrality by
2050, it is imperative to phase out non-renewable energy plants. This necessitates exploring alternative
options that offer flexibility without contributing to CO2 emissions. Nuclear power presents one such
option; although not subject to weather conditions like VRES, it still poses challenges such as nuclear
waste disposal and the inability to adjust power output instantly [Elliott, 2018].
A more viable alternative is therefore hydropower. Like VRES, hydropower relies on weather condi-
tions, such as precipitation. However, unlike VRES, hydropower allows for regulation, enabling oper-
ators to control both when and how much power is produced at all times. This regulation is achieved
through reservoir management, which offers the flexibility to adjust water flow through the turbine. Cur-
rently, hydropower generates more electricity than all other renewables combined, a trend projected to
continue well into the 2030s, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA) [Bojek, 2023]. Be-
yond this, hydropower is expected to maintain its pivotal role in decarbonizing the power system and
enhancing system flexibility.
In addition, the IEA also highlights the potential of pumped hydro as a crucial tool for balancing fluctua-
tions in solar and wind generation. However, without significant policy adjustments, global expansion of
hydropower is forecasted to decelerate in the coming decade [Bojek, 2023]. In 2020 the EU parliament
voted in favor of a report on energy strategy that hydropower technology is going to play a crucial part
in energy storage and that "the EU is not exploiting the full potential of this carbon-neutral and highly
efficient way of storing energy". The report urges further exploration and utilization of pumped storage
hydropower within the EU [International hydropower association, 2020].
The Role of Hydropower in Norway
Norway's abundant hydroelectric power resources already play a crucial role in the power system, a role
set to expand further in the future. Norway's extensive hydroelectric capacity serves as a reservoir for

Chapter 2. Background
storing surplus electricity during times of abundance. This stored energy can then be used during periods
of high demand and low VRES generation, effectively functioning as a giant battery.
According to the Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Directorate (NVE), the Norwegian energy
mix comprises approximately 150 TWh, with hydropower contributing around 96%. Within this mix,
approximately 75% is derived from regulated hydropower [NVE, 2021b], which allows for controlled
adjustments in power generation. In contrast, there are also Run-of-River (RoR) power plants that lack
regulation mechanisms and generate electricity based solely on the volume of water flowing in the river.

therefore gives a more in-depth overview of the different storage technologies in the power system.
Energy storage technologies enable the capture of energy for later use [NVE, 2019]. Presently, there
exists a variety of energy storage technologies, including batteries, power-to-gas systems, and mechan-

2.2.1 Hydropower Storage

Hydropower storage possesses the capability to store large quantities of energy with minimal loss. Un-
like other mentioned storage technologies, hydropower storage does not necessitate electrical energy
input. Instead, it stores energy as potential energy in water before converting it into electrical energy
through turbines and generators. Despite its advantages, hydropower also has drawbacks, notably its
environmental impact. Dam constructions and regulated rivers can disrupt local ecosystems and harm
fish populations [Askari et al., 2015]. Nevertheless, compared to gas and coal, hydropower stands out as
a much more climate-friendly option. A basic hydropower plant functions as depicted in Figure 2.8. The
principle involves regulating the flow of water into the inlet tunnel, causing variations in the reservoir
level. However, for safety and environmental reasons, there are predetermined maximum and minimum
regulated water levels for the reservoir. Maintaining these levels is critical as excessively low levels
can lead to power shortages or dry up surrounding rivers, impacting local ecosystems. Conversely, ex-
cessively high levels can pose flood risks, resulting in significant societal costs. A recent example of
flooding occurred in the summer of 2023 in eastern Norway, causing extensive damage [Kessel, 2024].
Conversely, instances of power shortages, such as the winters of 2020 and 2021 which caused high elec-
tricity prices, underscore the importance of maintaining a stable power supply [Holstad, 2022]. These
examples illustrate the potential benefits of tactical hydropower management, as the consequences of
such scenarios can be mitigated.

Various strategies exist to enhance the tactical management of hydropower. One such approach is to

2.2.2 Batteries

Batteries are one of the most common types of energy storage today. The technology has been develop-
ing rapidly in the last 5-10 years because of the increased production of electric vehicles. According to
an article from McKinsey, the Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) market is expected to more than
double in size by 2030 and play a vital role in enabling VRES generation [Jarbratt et al., 2023].

Batteries as seasonal storage are too expensive today compared to hydropower. Batteries also have a
strict operating temperature range [Jin et al., 2023]. Furthermore, compared to a PHES, batteries have a
much smaller capacity. Currently, BESS is used mainly for short-term storage to provide system flex-
ibility and reliability in systems with a high share of VRES production [Bowen et al., 2019]. Batteries
therefore have a fast response time and can help stabilize the grid frequency. In Sweden, Fortum has
connected batteries to two different hydropower plants [Barstad, 2021]. This is a test project to see what
kind of effect this could have on the power grid. This was first and foremost installed to be able to
regulate the grid to 50 Hz in a better way.

Batteries can also be useful for peak shaving/load management. Users can store cheap energy in the
off-peak periods to be used in the high-demand peak hours to reduce electricity costs [EDINA, 2024].

2.3 Power System Optimization Models

Power system optimization models are mathematical models employed to analyze power systems over
a long time horizon, possibly several decades. They are crucial for investigating the impact of the
increasing share of VRES in the power system or the effect of political regulation and policies. These
models can include both long-term investments in different technologies like transmission, generators
and storage as well as the short-term operations of power systems. The temporal resolution of both
long-term and short-term aspects varies among models, as does their geographical coverage. While
some models focus on individual countries, others encompass broader regions such as Europe.

2.3.1 Objectives of the Models

These models have several objectives, where one of the main ones being to analyze different strategies
to decarbonize the power system [Backe et al., 2022]. They have the capability to simulate the effects
of various policy instruments, aiding policymakers in understanding the potential impact of different

                                                                                                                   12
Chapter 2. Background

regulatory approaches. These versatile tools can be utilized for both policy development and capacity
expansion planning, which entails determining the most advantageous timing and scale for investments
in generation, storage, and transmission capacity. In addition, these models can clarify the significance
and contributions of various technologies within the power system and indicate the optimal periods for
investments to minimize system costs [Heuberger et al., 2017].
Ensuring an adequate capacity in the power system is a key objective of long-term power system models.
Their significance extends to the optimal integration of renewable energy sources, offering insights into
the most effective ways to incorporate these sources into the energy mix.
Depending on the scope and detail of the model it can include coupling between multiple sectors such
as electricity, gas, heat industry and transport. Examining these interdependencies sheds light on how
different sectors influence one another. This however substantially increases the complexity and size of
the model.

2.3.2 Temporal and Spatial Representation

Given the size of such models, computational tractability is maintained by adopting a simplified spatial
and temporal representation of the system. The level of spatial resolution is determined by the model's
focus. For example, by looking at countries at a national level, merging all of the country's resources
into one. The spatial resolution can vary all the way from a global scope to a specific electrical grid
in a confined area. Instead of modeling all hours in a year, power system models usually analyze a
limited number of hours of the year meant to represent a full year. These hours are carefully selected to
realistically represent the inherent uncertainties of power systems such as load, solar, wind and inflow
variations throughout a year. Various methods exist for selecting these representative hours, each with
its strengths and weaknesses. An argument for not considering every hour of the year, except for the
increase in complexity, is that assessing the daily dispatch in detail might create a false precision given
the overall uncertainties associated with long-term projections [Welsch et al., 2022].

3.1.1 Model Structure

One of the main challenges for an optimization model is to strike a balance between accurately repre-
senting reality and avoiding excessive complexity, which could lead to long computational times. As
Table 3.1 illustrates there are several possibilities for how to represent the real-world power system
through an optimization model. All models are constructed to allow for investments to be made at spe-
cific time steps, serving as a simplification of reality. The time between each investment step is given by
the investment periods column in Table 3.1. Furthermore, the operational granularity column denotes the
period over which a model simulates an entire year, while the time horizon column shows the duration
of each model.

The table shows that the power system optimization models have different motivations based on granu-
larity in operational steps, investment, and time horizon. Optimization models with shorter time horizons
typically feature shorter investment steps and higher operational granularity. Conversely, models with
longer time horizons usually offer a more simplified description of operational- and investment steps.
For instance, the EMMA-model [Hirth, 2017] considers a significantly shorter time horizon than the
LIMES-model [Osorio et al., 2021]. Hence, the EMMA model captures operational effects in the power
system in a better way than LIMES. On the other hand, the LIMES model results provide more insights
into long-term trends and give a better overview of strategic solutions in the future power market.

The selection of input sampling data is done in different ways to represent a large time horizon with
less data. The most common ways to do the selection are through load duration curve approaches and
clustering algorithms. The load duration curve approach in general tries to approximate the total load
duration curve for a given period with fewer hours. On the other hand, clustering algorithms sort the pos-
sible scenarios into subgroups based on different parameters. Optimization models like Dyn-ELMOND
[Gerbaulet and Lorenz, 2017] use a version of the load duration curve to represent years in the model. In
that case, the model chooses every 25th hour of the year, accurately representing the total load duration
curve of the year. The advantage of this method is that it represents all aspects of the total year precisely
as a sample of almost every day of the year is taken. It also makes it possible to model functionalities
that are connected throughout the year, such as ramping- and storage levels. Other optimization mod-
els, such as EMPIRE and LIMES, represent their scenarios through a clustering approach. The biggest
advantage of this type of technique is the ability to represent different versions of the future year, in
contrast to the load duration approach where the most accurate scenario based on parameters is chosen.
However, it is difficult to capture seasonal variations in a good way. EMPIRE therefore chose to cluster
possible scenarios based on seasons and not a whole year, representing seasonal variations to some de-
gree. Anyway, this method makes connections between seasons more challenging since not all parts of
the year are represented.

In summary, the structural aspects of optimization models within the power system exhibit varying
strengths and weaknesses depending on the specific area in focus. Based on their abilities, functionalities
regarding short- and long-term effects in the power system are implemented in different ways. This
means that some functionalities are more difficult to implement in some optimization models than others.

3.1.2 Uncertainty

All power system models involve uncertainty. Deriving from factors such as load demand, VRES,
inflow, and prices of fuels. The growing prevalence of VRES in the power system amplifies the need
to consider uncertainty in the models. Integrating uncertainty is crucial for accurately representing the
power system dynamics. As noted in [Yue et al., 2018] "Without adequately addressing uncertainties,
the model insights may be limited, lack robustness, and may mislead decision makers". Yet, the inclusion
of uncertainty also substantially increases the complexity and thus the computational time of the models.
There is often a trade-off between aspects like the representation of uncertainty and the technical detail
of the model. How this is handled depends on the purpose of the model.

Various approaches exist for handling uncertainty in power system models. Models can adopt deter-
ministic or stochastic methodologies across both long-term and short-term time horizons, or utilize a
combination of both. For example, the original EMPIRE model employs multi-horizon stochastic pro-
gramming, making long-term investment decisions under perfect foresight while accounting for short-
term uncertainty through the inclusion of multiple scenarios for each season. The main advantage of this
method is reduced computational complexity while still representing the uncertain variability of VRES.
However, the main disadvantage is the long solution time and the lack of connection between seasons in a
year. In contrast, most other explored power system models like GENeSYS-MOD [Lo¨ffler et al., 2017],
POWER [Frew et al., 2016], PRIMES [National Technical University of Athens, 2018], LIMES
[Osorio et al., 2021], Dyn-ELMOD [Gerbaulet and Lorenz, 2017], US-REGEN [Young, 2020], URBS-
EU [Schaber et al., 2012] and EMMA [Hirth, 2017] are completely deterministic in both long- and
short-term time horizons. In these models, the inclusion of uncertainty is sacrificed to enhance the
representation of other aspects of the model like the spatial or temporal resolution and technical detail.
For example, EMMA, represents every hour of the year but is fully deterministic in both the long- and
short-term. Nonetheless, the developers have tried to include some of the uncertain nature of the VRES
by imposing a reserve requirement and by charging VRES generators balancing costs. The LIMES
model incorporates the uncertainty by choosing six days that represent the variation in VRES produc-
tion and load demand throughout the year in each region in the model [Osorio et al., 2021]. Both E2M2
[Swider and Weber, 2006] and TIMES [Rosenberg et al., 2020] have the possibility to be both determin-
istic and stochastic, depending on the preference of the user.

To recap, uncertainty in power system modeling is typically addressed in three ways: a fully stochastic
model, a fully deterministic model, or a combination of both. Each method has both advantages and
disadvantages, making it difficult to conclude which is superior as it depends on the motivation of the
model. However, most of the studied models are deterministic, indicating that this may be the most
commonly used and preferred method. This is probably because of the reduction in problem complexity,
which is crucial for these big long-term models. Despite being deterministic, they all try to include
uncertainty through different methods.

3.1.3 Hydropower Management

In the reviewed literature there are generally two ways of modeling regulated hydropower, either as a
generator or as a storage. The modeling approach chosen depends on the model's level of detail and its
specific focus. Each method offers its own set of advantages and disadvantages.

The Dyn-ELMOD model has implemented regulated hydro as a storage. It has a storage balance that is
connected throughout the year. This approach introduces complexity, as the storage level in any given
hour is influenced by preceding decisions. The approach is done in combination with a deterministic
approach, simplifying the model considerably. The main benefit is the increased flexibility this storage
introduces in the long term, mirroring real-world practices where hydropower producers store water
during summer for heightened electricity demand in winter months. However, a major flaw of Dyn-
ELMODs approach is the lack of uncertainty when it comes to inflow. The amount of inflow into
the reservoirs can vary greatly from year to year in the real world and by ignoring this the model can
perfectly adjust its regulated hydro production. In real life, hydro production planners have to consider
the possibility of overflow or lack of water when planning production.

To solve this issue regarding perfect information in hydropower management certain possibilities have
been suggested. [Kotzur et al., 2018] describes a more complex method based on the superposition of
inter-period and intra-periods that enables the possibility of implementing seasonal differences without
facilitating connections between seasons. The idea is that an inter-period variable creates seasonal vari-
ation, whereas the intra-period variable takes care of the operational differences within that season. This
method can be applied to many different power system configurations and is applicable for hydropower
as it is the most widely used form of seasonal storage. The main downside to this method is the increased
complexity of the operational steps of the problem.

To avoid this type of complexity, an alternative approach could be to treat regulated hydropower as an
unregulated generator, similar to the method used in the LIMES model. However, this greatly underes-
timates the flexibility potential of regulated hydropower as it cannot store energy for future utilization.
As discussed in the section about uncertainty there is often a trade-off between the technical detail and
other aspects of the model. In long-term power system models with extended time horizons, detailed
spatial representation and high temporal resolution are prioritized over a comprehensive hydropower
formulation. While the ideal model would encompass both aspects, achieving this balance may prove
challenging or unfeasible.

Another aspect of hydropower management in power system models is how they consider the separation
between pumped hydro and regulated hydro. While in reality, these two share reservoirs, this may
not always be feasible in modelling. One of the reasons that they usually are separated is because of
their characteristics. Regulated hydro relies solely on uncertain and uncontrollable inflow for reservoir
replenishment, whereas pumped hydro can actively pump water at any time if economically viable.
Consequently, models like the LIMES model, implement regulated hydro as a generator and pumped
hydro as a storage separately [Osorio et al., 2021]. However, this separation may result in countries with
a predominant share of regulated hydro, such as Norway, missing out on the significant flexibility that

                                                                                                                   19
Chapter 3. Related Literature
hydropower could offer. Conversely, a few models, such as the Dyn-ELMOD model, have combined
the two into a single storage [Gerbaulet and Lorenz, 2017]. This storage can be filled up by both inflows
and by pumping water up from a lower reservoir. With this combined storage the model can utilize
the full flexibility potential of the regulated hydro reservoirs and pumped hydro plants. Nevertheless, a
drawback of this method is that the model can now fill up reservoirs through pumping that do not have
PHES.

3.2 This Thesis Contribution to the Power System Optimization Research

To the best of the authors' knowledge, there are very few or no other power system models that consider
the combination of connection between periods within a year with uncertainty in the operational stage.
While the E2M2 model has a connection between periods and is modeled in two ways, both stochastic
and deterministic, the stochasticity is related to wind intermittency, whereas the focus of this master's
thesis is hydropower. The combination of connection between seasons and uncertainty is particularly
interesting to model as it may more realistically represent the flexibility that hydropower can provide in
long-term power system models with high shares of renewable energy sources. As presented in Section
2.1, hydropower is crucial for enabling the integration of VRES by providing balancing and flexibility
services [IRENA, 2023]. Thus, it is important to model it as realistic as possible.
Based on the literature review, it appears that the primary challenge in implementing this concept within
existing medium- and long-term power system optimization models lies in the combination of two key
issues. The first one is the difficulty of managing stochasticity, and the second is capturing the flexibility
potential of hydropower without making the model too complex. The combination of these two issues is
difficult to model simply and realistically. It is therefore the main challenge this extension of EMPIRE
faces.

4.1 Introduction to EMPIRE

The EMPIRE model is designed for evaluating optimal capacity investments and system operations
across a long-term planning period, typically spanning 40-50 years. It includes 31 European countries,
with each country represented by a node at a national level. The exception is Norway, which is divided
into 5 regions, representing each of the price zones. Additionally, the model offers the option to incor-
porate nodes in the North Sea, simulating offshore wind production, which this thesis uses. Figure 4.1
illustrates the node structure of the EMPIRE model. The nodes of Norway are simplified to one node in
the illustration.

To effectively explore pathways for decarbonizing the energy system, it is crucial to consider two main
aspects: the techno-economic features over the long term and the operational dynamics in the short term.
This dual focus is necessary because long-term strategic decisions are often shaped by the realities of
short-term operations. The EMPIRE model adopts a mathematical framework based on multi-horizon
stochastic programming. This structure allows long-term investment decisions to be influenced by short-
term uncertainties arising from different operational scenarios. These uncertainties stem from VRES
production and variations in inflow and demand.
The objective of the EMPIRE model is to minimize the costs associated with both investment and op-
eration across the entire system and all time periods. Within this framework, investments can be made
in generators, storages, and transmission capacities, subject to predefined capacity limits. Investment
costs consist of capital costs as well as annual fixed Operating and Maintenance (O&M) expenses for
the asset's operational lifespan. Operational costs include the expenses related to power production and
the cost of lost load during each operational hour. Lost load costs arise when the system fails to meet the
entire demand at a node, resulting in load shedding, which is economically unfavorable. The EMPIRE
model operates under various constraints, including energy asset lifetimes, load dispatch, generator
ramping, energy storage balance, operational losses, asset capacity constraints, and policy constraints
such as carbon emission. The model's output consists of investment and operational decisions concern-
ing energy system assets, including generation, storage, and transmission infrastructure.
EMPIRE includes various generator types to accurately reflect the future energy system. Some of these
generator types include regulated hydro, run-of-river, solar, wind, bio, waste, nuclear, coal, gas, and
lignite. The version of EMPIRE utilized in this thesis features only two types of storage: pumped
hydropower and Li-ion BESS. A noteworthy aspect is that the regulated hydropower generator and the
pumped hydro storage operate independently without mutual influence. This divergence from real-world

dynamics constitutes a weakness in the current model formulation, as these two technologies typically
share the same reservoir and exhibit close interconnection.

4.2 Temporal Aggregation

To reduce the size and computational time, two types of temporal aggregation schemes have been ap-
plied. Given the primary focus on the long-term expansion of the system, some dynamic granularity is
traded off by adopting five-year time blocks instead of annual steps for the investment period. Capacity
investments are available from the start of the same period the investments are made.
The second approach to reduce the problem's size involves limiting the number of operational hours.
Rather than computing system dispatch over the full 8760 hours of a year, a reduced time series is
subdivided into seasons. This series comprises the four regular seasons--winter, spring, summer, and
fall--alongside two peak seasons representing periods of heightened demand. The first peak season
starts at the hour with the highest combined load across all nodes in a randomly chosen year between
2015 and 2019, while peak season two is established on the hour with the highest load of a single node in
a randomly chosen year in the same range. These peak seasons are important for assessing the necessity
for backup capacity during periods of peak demand.
The EMPIRE model has some flexibility regarding the temporal resolution. It is possible to adjust the
number of investment periods (maximum eight), the duration of representative time periods and the
number of stochastic scenarios. The standard is to use 168 hours (1 week) to represent regular seasons
and 24 hours for the peak seasons within each stochastic scenario. Typically three to ten scenarios per
season are considered in each investment period.

4.3 Scenario Sampling Methods

There are currently three different scenario sampling methods implemented in EMPIRE. The first one
is completely random, meaning it randomly chooses an hour in a season in a year between 2015 and

                                                                                                                   23
Chapter 4. EMPIRE Model

2019. The scenario is made by taking the sample hour and the next 167 hours for a regular season and
the next 23 hours for a peak season and using historical data on load demand and power production for
the selected sample to create the scenarios [Skar et al., 2016].
The second sampling approach employs a clustering technique, specifically k-means clustering. The
various outcomes are grouped into k distinct clusters using aggregated electricity load data. The par-
titioning is done by using mean load and Wasserstein distance. Wasserstein distance is a tool used to
measure the difference between probability distributions [Panaretos and Zemel, 2020], and is mainly
used to compare how similar two scenarios are based on different characteristics. Then scenarios are
sampled randomly for each season independently while ensuring that at least one scenario from each
cluster is represented in each investment period in the scenario tree.
The third method is called moment matching. This approach focuses on ensuring that the electricity
load in the scenarios statistically resembles historical electricity load data. The first step is to run the
random sampling scheme T times to create T scenario trees. Then the moment matching method selects
the scenario tree of the T different candidates that has its four moments most similar to all historical data
[Backe et al., 2021].

4.4 Model Structure

As described earlier in this chapter, EMPIRE adopts a mathematical framework based on multi-horizon
stochastic programming. The concept behind multi-horizon stochastic programming is aimed at reduc-
ing the complexity found in stochastic optimization models. This approach differs from multi-stage
stochastic programming in its reliance on the operational stage, as highlighted by [Kaut et al., 2013].
In the multi-horizon framework, decisions at the operational level are exclusively linked to the strate-
gic node corresponding to their particular time step. This distinction is depicted in Figures 4.2 and

4.4.1 Scenario Formulation

The EMPIRE model holds a scenario formulation. A detailed depiction of the structure of one strategic
period can be seen in Figure 4.5. As mentioned previously the model contains six different seasons;
winter, spring, summer, autumn, and two peak seasons. Winter seasons are sampled from January,
February or March, spring seasons are sampled from April, May or June and so on for summer and fall.
The figure only shows three scenarios per season, but the user can choose to generate up to ten scenarios.

              Figure 4.5: Operational structure for one investment period and three scenarios

The yellow squares represent one season in one scenario, meaning it contains all hours within each sea-
son. It is important to note that these hours are interconnected, meaning that information from one hour
influences the subsequent hours within the same season. However, there is no transfer of information
between seasons or scenarios, as illustrated in the figure. This implies that each season and scenario
operates independently, and decisions made within one season or scenario do not impact the others.
The scenario formulation in EMPIRE presents both strengths and weaknesses. On the positive side,
it adeptly captures uncertainty by modeling multiple potential future scenarios. This approach enables
decision-makers to explore a spectrum of outcomes and adapt their strategies accordingly, fostering the
development of robust and flexible solutions. However, despite its strengths, this formulation falls short
in addressing tactical considerations spanning multiple seasons. The lack of inter-seasonal connections
poses a challenge for effective hydropower planning, as this technology heavily relies on storage from
one season to the next. Consequently, the inability to account for such interconnections limits the for-

                                                                                                                   26
Chapter 4. EMPIRE Model
mulation's ability to accurately represent real-world hydropower dynamics.

4.4.2 Node Formulation

The project report [Ulsaker et al., 2023] addresses the absence of inter-seasonal connections in the sce-
nario formulation by introducing a node formulation with branches in the scenario tree of the operational
stage. This transition from a scenario formulation to a node formulation is motivated by the complex-
ity inherent in branching within a scenario formulation. The scenario formulation requires numerous
non-anticipativity constraints and duplicates of variables and equations, significantly increasing model
complexity. In contrast, the node formulation simplifies the model formulation by solely requiring the
node index to map them together, without the need for non-anticipativity constraints, which enhances
model efficiency and reduces complexity [Vitali et al., 2020]. The node formulation presented in the
project report [Ulsaker et al., 2023] solves the issue of lacking inter-seasonal connections in the sce-
nario formulation while simultaneously accommodating a degree of uncertainty. The upcoming section
delves into some of the details of this node formulation, as the rest of this thesis is built upon this
foundational concept.
Figure 4.6 presents one example of what the scenario tree could look like with a node formulation. The
scenario tree is divided into different stages. A new stage starts when new information is disclosed. Stage
0, is the investment node. Since the node formulation focuses on improving the operational stage, the
investment node does not play any significant role in this formulation. Additional stages are incorporated
if the scenario tree exhibits a branching during the operational stage.

Problem Description

The primary objective of this master's thesis is to enhance the EMPIRE model by integrating tactical
hydropower management through inter-seasonal connections while preserving stochastic elements in
operational decisions. This challenge was initially addressed in the project report [Ulsaker et al., 2023].
As elaborated in Section 4.4.2, a node formulation was proposed for the operational stage, departing
from the scenario formulation of the original model. This formulation ensures the incorporation of both
inter-seasonal connections and uncertainty.

The inter-seasonal connections facilitated by the node formulation allow for improvements in other
functionalities of regulated hydropower management in EMPIRE. As discussed in chapter 2, Pumped
Hydro Energy Storage (PHES) is crucial for tactical hydropower management. In the original EMPIRE
model, PHES is separated from regulated hydropower production, making these functionalities distinct.
Additionally, the regulated hydropower production functionality is based on historical production data.
The primary advantage of this generation type is its ability to control production, which should not be
influenced by historical production patterns. Therefore, this thesis aims to improve these functionalities.
The mathematical improvements are outlined in chapter 6 and the data collection is outlined in chapter 7.

The implementation of the node formulation introduces new challenges. First, the length of a single
operational scenario has changed from one season to a year, creating a new challenge regarding how the
seasons in an operational year should be connected. In the project report, a simple solution to this issue
was introduced, but it is not considered thorough enough to yield realistic results [Ulsaker et al., 2023].
This thesis investigates how the structure of the operational year can be improved. A detailed explanation
is provided in chapter 8. Additionally, with scenarios spanning a year, decisions about the initial and
final hydropower reservoir levels must be made, which are further described in chapter 6.

To sum up, the scope of this master's thesis is to improve the tactical hydropower decisions in EM-
PIRE. With the implementation of the node formulation [Ulsaker et al., 2023], new opportunities and
challenges arise. This thesis aims to leverage these new opportunities and address these challenges to
create the most realistic hydropower functionality possible.

6.2.1 Explanation of New Modeling Approach

Before the mathematical formulation of the new constraints is presented an explanation of the modeling
approach for the node formulation is given, for the reader to get a better understanding.
Initial and Final Storage Level
In the node formulation, it is decided to have different initial- and final storage reservoir levels depending
on the scenario. A fair simplification, which is done in the scenario formulation, is to set the storage
levels equal at the beginning and the end of each season in every scenario. However, since all the seasons
are connected in the node formulation it is prudent to do this part more thoroughly. As the sampled
winter season can be selected as a random week in January, February, or March, the actual reservoir
levels can significantly differ between individual nodes. This variation can lead to less realistic results in
the optimization model if the initial regulated hydropower storage level is set equally for all nodes. The
same argument counts for the final storage level. Figure 6.1 illustrates how different reservoir levels can
be because of the way the scenarios are generated. For instance, for the initial reservoir levels, the level
can vary from around 70% to around 30% depending on which period the initial node is gathered from.

                  Figure 6.1: Illustration of annual reservoir levels in Norway [NVE, 2024]

Discharge of the Hydro Reservoir Battery Type
With the inter-seasonal connections resulting from the node formulation, there is an opportunity to
implement a new storage balance that integrates all elements interacting with the reservoir. In this
context, a new battery type called "Hydro Reservoir" is introduced, combining regulated hydropower
production and pumped hydro production. This means that the model allows the discharge of the new

                                                                                                                   38
Chapter 6. Mathematical Formulation

battery type through two different variables, both the regulated hydropower production variable and the
pumped hydro discharge variable. The reason why these variables are kept as two separate variables is
because of the investments in pumped hydropower and regulated hydropower production. In EMPIRE
it is assumed that if the model invests in charging capacity it also invests in discharging capacity, since
investments in the operating tunnel affect both the charging and discharging capacity of the plant. To
be able to maintain this functionality, and at the same time make it possible to invest in only regulated
hydropower capacity (in regulated hydropower plants without PHES) the discharge of the regulated
hydropower storage has to be split into two variables.

Inflow Data

In the scenario formulation, historical hydropower production is used to limit the production. However,
to make the node formulation more realistic, a decision is made to utilize inflow data instead. It is worth
mentioning that the available inflow data is provided on a weekly basis only. Consequently, the model
receives hourly inflow values distributed evenly throughout each week to compensate for the absence
of detailed hourly data. The inflow is distributed hourly instead of weekly to achieve a realistic storage
balance and reduce the risk of reservoir overflow due to the large spikes in inflow that occur by weekly
generated data.

7.2 Maximum and Minimum Storage Levels

In the original EMPIRE model, energy is stored exclusively through either pumped hydro or batteries.
Consequently, the maximum or minimum storage levels are directly associated with these two storage
technologies. However, in the updated storage balance, energy can now be stored through a combination
of pumped hydro and regulated hydro storage, as elaborated in Section 6.2.2. This change necessitates
adjustments to the maximum and minimum limits for hydropower, as they now encompass larger reser-
voirs. The limits for Li-Ion BESS remain unchanged, as the focus of this thesis is tactical hydropower
planning, and batteries are mostly used in a short-term perspective.
The data collection process for this section primarily involves obtaining data for the maximum and
minimum limits of hydro reservoirs in all countries. However, reliable data is only found for Norway
[NVE, 2024]. Consequently, a method to estimate the reservoir limits for other countries becomes nec-
essary. For Norway, the Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Directorate (NVE) utilizes the terms
"highest regulated water level" (HRV) and "lowest regulated water level" (LRV). These terms denote
the highest and lowest permissible water levels within reservoirs. Figure 7.3 graphically illustrates the
reservoir filling levels for Norway for each year during the period 2015-2019. When the filling level
reaches 100%, the reservoir has attained its HRV. Throughout these five years, the highest observed
filling level is approximately 93% (as indicated by the blue line).

                                                                                                                   47
Chapter 7. Data Collection

                   Figure 7.3: Reservoir filling level for Norway 2015-2019 [NVE, 2024]
When estimating the maximum water level for other countries, a simplification is employed. It assumes
that the highest filling level observed during the 5-year period is 93% for all other countries as well. For
countries, where reliable values for stored energy levels have been collected from ENTSO-e, the highest
energy level during this period, is selected. It is then presumed that this value corresponds to 93% of
the country's highest regulated water level. To determine the maximum level, the highest energy level
is therefore divided by 0.93. However, this method does not apply to countries where the stored energy
level is unknown. For these countries, reference countries and their associated ratios are utilized. To
derive the HRV in these countries, the HRV of the reference country is multiplied by the ratio. It is
important to acknowledge that this is a simplification and might not hold for all countries, but as already
discussed this might not have that big of an impact on the model since the share these countries held is
so small. The estimated highest legal energy storage levels in GWh for all countries with hydropower in

Given that one week is utilized to represent an entire season, the values depicted in Figure 7.4 are

Several considerations suggest that the model should not allow the water level in the reservoir to be
drained down to zero. Firstly, environmental regulations mandated by governments may dictate a min-
imum downstream flow rate and reservoir level to protect wildlife habitats. Secondly, the inlet may be
positioned higher up compared to the reservoir's bottom to prevent sediment flow, which could poten-
tially damage the system's components. Consequently, allowing the water level to drop too low may
prevent water from reaching the inlet. Furthermore, a low reservoir level results in a smaller hydraulic
head, as less water in the reservoir generates less pressure, subsequently leading to reduced energy pro-
duction [NVE, 2009]. If the reservoir level for Norway in Figure 7.3 is at 0%, it indicates that the lowest
regulated water level has been reached [NVE, 2023a]. Essentially, there is still water in the reservoir, but
none that can be utilized to generate energy, meaning that a filling rate of 0% signifies that there is no
usable energy remaining in the reservoir. While this understanding is specific to Norway, it is assumed to
apply to other countries as well. Consequently, the model is permitted to deplete the reservoir level down
to a 0% filling rate. In reality, this does not imply the complete absence of water in the reservoir, but
rather the absence of water available for production purposes. This approach ensures that environmental
and technical constraints are taken into account.

7.3 Initial and Final Reservoir Levels

In the EMPIRE formulation outlined in the project report [Ulsaker et al., 2023], the initial storage level
for each period is 50% of the maximum reservoir level. Subsequently, the final storage level is also set to
be the same as the initial level, implying that the year starts and ends at the same energy level. However,
as argued in Section 6.2.1, this approach may not accurately reflect reality since storage levels typically
vary within a single season. To address this limitation, the proposal is to determine both the initial and
final storage levels based on the days chosen to represent the season. During the scenario generation
process, a random historical hour within the season is selected, and then one season is modeled to
encompass this hour and the subsequent 167 hours. For most countries (blue and red), the reservoir level
for each week is known, as explained in Section 7.1. The initial reservoir level can be obtained for these
countries by identifying the reservoir level corresponding to the week to which the selected hour belongs
and then dividing it by the maximum capacity. For countries without known reservoir levels (yellow),
the initial level is assumed to be the same as that of the reference country.

7.4 Cost of Spillage

As described in Chapter 6 there has also been added a possibility of spilling water in the regulated
hydropower storage balance. In reality, spillage is done to reduce the risk of flood, which can cause
severe damage to both the hydropower plant and surrounding settlements. At times of a power surplus
in the system, the hydropower producers are willing to pay consumers to use electricity to reduce the
water level in the reservoirs to mitigate the risk of costly damages caused by floods. This can lead to
negative electricity prices, which have been observed in the European power market several times. The
cost of spillage is set to 6.1 EUR/MWh as -6.1 EUR/MWh is the lowest power price that has occurred
in Norway [Blaker, 2023]. This cost then represents the price the hydropower producers are willing
to pay to spill excess water that could have been saved for later use in the model. The spillage cost
value is subject to uncertainty, as it can be challenging to precisely determine the exact cost incurred
by hydropower producers when spilling water. Therefore, the value presented is a pessimistic estimate
rather than a definitive number, intended to provide a approximation of the costs during extreme spillage
events.

Scenario Generation

In this chapter, the new scenario generation method is outlined. The chapter first investigates possible
correlations between seasons based on historical inflow data for Norway. Based on the results of this
investigation, a new scenario generation routine is developed. This routine incorporates elements of
k-means clustering, as used in the scenario formulation, and a new method that more accurately weights
the scenarios according to a probability distribution. After that, a discussion of when to split into new
branches to best capture the uncertainty from hydrological inflow follows. Finally, there is a debate on
where the peak seasons should be placed in the scenario.

8.1 Scenario Generation Process From the Project Report

Certain characteristics of the node formulation presented in the project report [Ulsaker et al., 2023], such
as the scenario generation, are found to be suboptimal. The scenario generation process from the project
report [Ulsaker et al., 2023] is depicted in Figure 8.1 and contains three start nodes and one branching
as an example. The nodes are generated using k-means clustering based on hydrological inflow data
instead of electrical load data as the original EMPIRE model does, as detailed in Chapter 4. This is
because hydropower planning is the main focus in this thesis. For regular seasons, a simplification
assumed that wet seasons are followed by wet seasons, and vice versa for dry seasons. First, three
winter nodes are generated and chosen from one cluster each, arranged from the wettest (far left) to
driest (far right) in the figure. A similar approach is applied to the nine spring seasons, with the wettest
spring node placed under the wettest winter node, and so forth for the other seasons. Peak season nodes
are generated randomly. Each node and scenario are assigned equal probabilities of occurrence, which
in this case is approximately 11% as illustrated at the bottom of the figure, which may not accurately
reflect reality. Therefore, the objective of this section is to explore potential correlations between inflow
from one season to the next and discuss how this affects the scenario generation in the model, to make it
more realistic.

8.2 Exploring Seasonal Correlations

A method for finding the probability of possible outcomes is explored. This method is built on weekly
hydrological inflow data for Norway from 1959 to 2023. The reason only data for Norway is used is
because of data availability and since Norway has the largest hydropower resources it is most important
to use accurate data for this country.
To find the desired probabilities the initial step involves aggregating the weekly hydrological inflow data
into seasonal periods. The first thirteen weeks of the year are defined as winter, the next thirteen as
spring, and so forth, like in the EMPIRE model. Subsequently, the data is sorted into different groups
depending on the amount of inflow. The number of groups corresponds to the number of start nodes; for
instance, if there are three start nodes, the data is divided into three groups called "Wet" (W), "Medium"
(M) and "Dry" (D). Each group contains equally many data points. Further, it is investigated how many
times each outcome appears in the data. For example how many wet spring seasons succeed a wet
winter season and so on. Only the probabilities with three start nodes are presented here. However, the
approach is similar for two, four, and five start nodes. An overview of all these probabilities is presented
in Appendix D.
An important side note is that the weekly inflow data is slightly altered. Analysis of the inflow data
reveals a progressive increase in the total inflow volume throughout the years as can be observed in the
left plot of Figure 8.2. This is attributed to climate change, with yearly inflow levels rising over time
[Koestler et al., 2019]. To adjust for the climate change trend a linear regression analysis is conducted to
normalize the inflow data and ensure comparability across different years, thus smoothing out variations
in inflow volumes over time as can be seen in the right part of Figure 8.2.

Figures 8.3, 8.4 and 8.5 reveal significant variations in probabilities across the seasons. A relatively
balanced distribution among categories is evident in the transition from winter to spring (Figure 8.3).
Particularly, during wet winter seasons, outcomes in spring appear almost random. Conversely, follow-
ing dry winter seasons, a wet spring season is more probable. This phenomenon is likely attributable to
snow melting, contributing to increased water flow in the spring.

The transition from spring to summer (Figure 8.4) presents a more extreme shift, notably reflected in
the increased probability of transitioning from a wet spring to a wet summer, at 50%, and from a dry
spring to a dry summer, at 57%. In contrast, there is only a 9% likelihood of transitioning to a dry
summer following a wet spring, and a 19% probability of transitioning to a wet summer following a dry
spring. This observation suggests that once established, certain hydrological conditions tend to persist.
This persistence may be due to lingering atmospheric patterns and the fact that if the ground is dry, it
absorbs water more readily, preventing it from reaching the reservoirs as effectively as it would if the
ground were wet [Melteig, 2023]. Consequently, if one season is wet, it increases the likelihood that the
following season will also be wet. Similarly, if one season is dry, the following season is likely to also
be dry.

In transitioning from summer to fall (Figure 8.5), a similar pattern emerges, although not as extreme as
in the spring-summer transition. A dry summer season is probably followed by a dry fall season, while
it is unlikely for a wet summer season to be followed by a dry fall season.

8.3 Enhanced Scenario Generation Process

With the new method, how the selection of nodes happens varies depending on both the season and the
location of the branching. K-means clustering is still employed, with the number of clusters matching
the number of start nodes, which in Figure 8.6 is three. Each cluster generates one start node, resulting
in one wet, one medium, and one dry winter node. For simplicity, it is assumed that these nodes have an
equal probability of occurring, at 33%.
Two different scenario generation approaches are used when generating nodes for the spring, summer
and fall seasons based on whether there is a branching before the season or not. If a branching occurs
before the subsequent season, one node is selected from each cluster for each branch, resulting in a total
of three wet spring nodes, three medium ones, and three dry nodes in this case, placed in one branch
each. Different probabilities, based on the previously described method, are associated with different
scenarios. For instance, the probability of having a scenario with a wet winter followed by a wet spring
is 11.9%, as indicated at the bottom of the figure. These probabilities are factored into the objective
function to weigh the importance of different scenarios appropriately.
This weighting is only applied when a branching occurs before the subsequent season. In cases where
there is no branching, the nodes in the next season are selected by using the previously described proba-
bilities. For instance, when transitioning from a wet spring node, there is a 50% chance of selecting the
summer node from the wettest cluster, a 41% chance for the medium cluster, and a 9% chance for the
driest cluster. These probabilities are conditional, meaning each probability is determined based on the
outcome of the previous node.
The method involving probabilities is exclusively applied to the regular seasons. For peak seasons,
which are limited to five options (one from each year in the period 2015-2019), clustering is not feasible
due to this insufficient data. Random selection is therefore deemed the most appropriate solution for

8.4 Branching

In the updated formulation, the scenario tree is divided into new branches between each regular sea-
son. However, it is not computationally feasible to have a branching after each season. This raises the
question of where to introduce branching to best capture the uncertainty of hydrological inflow.
It is most sensible to introduce branching where the probabilities of the various outcomes differ the most.
For example in the case of three start nodes, it is more rational to introduce a branching between spring
and summer than between winter and spring as some of the outcomes are more extreme from spring to
summer, as can be observed in Figures 8.3 and 8.4. When the probabilities of outcomes are relatively
equal, it is close to having a completely random scenario tree. This means that the first branching should
be chosen at the seasonal transition that experiences the most uncertainty, with subsequent branchings
occurring at the transitions with the next highest levels of uncertainty, and so on.

8.5 Peak seasons

The peak seasons are, as explained in Section 4.2, included to represent the necessity for backup capacity
during periods of high demand. In the node formulation, all the seasons are connected to represent a full
year. This leads to an issue of where to put the peak seasons as they do not have a naturally given order
in the year as the other seasons.

                                                                                                                   57
Chapter 8. Scenario Generation

The most natural solution to this is placing them either before the winter season at the beginning of the
year or after the fall season at the end of the year. The reasoning for this is that this is usually the time
of year experiencing the highest electrical demand, primarily driven by heating needs. This can be seen
from the selection of peak season hours in Table 8.1. There are only 5 samples possible for each of the
peak seasons because there is only one maximum value for each year.

                                Table 8.1: Peak season samples

An argument for putting them in the end after fall is that hydropower producers usually save water
from the warm spring and summer seasons for the colder fall and winter seasons. If peak seasons are
situated at the beginning of the year, the model will lack the opportunity to leverage stored water from
the preceding spring and summer, as each year is treated independently and commences with winter.

Therefore, the decision to position the peak seasons towards the end of the year is grounded in both
the likelihood of peak season occurrences and the capacity to fill up the regulated hydro storage in
preparation to meet the anticipated demand.

report [Ulsaker et al., 2023] due to its more realistic probabilities. However, since comparing the two
scenario generation routines requires a significant amount of testing, this comparison was considered to
be outside the scope and will therefore not undergo further testing.

9.2.1 Argumentation of the Chosen Scenario Tree

As previously discussed, there is a trade-off between computational efficiency and the representation
of uncertainty. Ideally, the scenario tree should include numerous start nodes and branching between
each season to best capture the uncertainty. However, due to computational constraints, this is not
feasible. Therefore, it is necessary to determine the optimal number of start nodes and branches that
balance uncertainty representation with acceptable run times. Comprehensive testing to identify the best
combination has not been conducted, but various combinations have been run once each to estimate
approximate run times, as shown in Table 9.2. A run time exceeding 24 hours is considered inefficient.
Based on this criterion, the scenario tree is configured with three start nodes and one branching, resulting
in nine scenarios in total. This combination is believed to adequately represent seasonal uncertainty the
best while maintaining computational efficiency.

In Chapter 8, it was determined that a branching should occur where the most extreme correlations
between subsequent seasons are observed. This was identified as being between spring and summer.
Therefore, the branching is implemented at this point to best capture the inter-seasonal correlations.

9.2.7 Spillage

As described in Section 6.2.2, a new spillage variable has been introduced to the model. This variable
allows the model to spill water if necessary during operational hours. While spilling water is generally
not preferred, it may be necessary in certain hours due to high inflow or model restrictions. Since spillage
is typically undesirable, the amount of spillage serves as an indicator of the flexibility potential.
The leftmost graph of Figure 9.6 illustrates the total amount of spillage in Europe across all periods and
scenarios. The model spills a small amount of energy throughout the year, with a slight increase during
the spring, attributed to the high inflow and increased risk of flood during this season. However, the
most notable observation is the significant increase in spillage during the peak season compared to other
seasons. While this may seem unrealistic, it is primarily due to the reservoir limits imposed at the end
of the season. These limits lead to spillage if the reservoir level exceeds this prescribed level before the
peak seasons in some scenarios.
The rightmost graph of Figure 9.6 shows that almost all spillage in Europe occurs in Norway. This
observation suggests a potential mismatch between the reservoir levels and the discharging capacity in
Norway. The discharge capacity is too small to sufficiently empty the reservoir in scenarios with high
inflow and a low final reservoir level, resulting in large amounts of spillage during peak seasons.

9.7 Further Discussion and Model Shortcomings

The introduction of the node formulation has significantly altered the model's functionality to enhance
tactical hydropower planning. However, this transformation has also introduced potential weaknesses
that could undermine the credibility and realism of the model.

Uncertainty

One of the primary advantages of the original EMPIRE model was its robust representation of uncer-
tainty in the operational stage, with the capability to accommodate up to ten different stochastic scenar-
ios for each season in every investment period. However, with the introduction of the node formulation,
some of this uncertainty is reduced due to the revised structure of the model. Specifically, the uncertainty
in seasons before a branching is weakened compared to those following a branching. Ideally, the model
would feature numerous start nodes to effectively represent uncertainty. As evidenced in Table 9.27,
achieving this is infeasible due to prohibitively long runtimes. Consequently, the node formulation ne-
cessitates a trade-off between runtime and uncertainty representation, implying that uncertainty cannot
be captured as comprehensively as in the scenario formulation across all seasons without significantly
extending runtime to an impractical degree. Table 9.28 is shown to give a comparison to run times in
the original model.

Another significant weakness to acknowledge is the presence of perfect information between seasons in
the node formulation when a branching does not occur. This flaw introduces an overly optimistic bias
into the model across several aspects. For instance, the representation of VRES in the generation mix
may be exaggerated, as the model can anticipate potential production well in advance of its occurrence.
Similarly, the model's visibility into regulated hydro storage allows it to anticipate inflow and electrical
demand long before they manifest, enabling proactive planning. Uncertainty plays a critical role in
hydropower management, underscoring the importance of not underestimating it.

Pumping to Hydro Reservoirs without PHES Installed

In the node formulation, the regulated hydropower reservoir both with and without pumped hydro in-
stalled, is modeled as a single common reservoir for each country, as detailed in Section 6.2.1. This
approach introduces a limitation in realism by assuming that pumped hydro can be utilized in all hy-
dropower reservoirs in the model. In reality, only a certain proportion of hydropower systems in Europe
are equipped with PHES, so the model's assumption that all systems can be charged does not accurately

                                                                                                                   98
Chapter 9. Computational Study

reflect real-world conditions. To mitigate this, the model restricts the charging capacity of pumped hydro
to realistic values for different countries, ensuring that the total pumped hydro capacity is not exceeded.
Nevertheless, it is important to recognize this limitation, as it makes the model more generalized and
introduces potential sources of error.

Another possibility could be to model hydropower plants with PHES and hydropower plants without
PHES as two separate storage balances to avoid this issue. The reason why this is not done is due to a
lack of historical reservoir data on those subgroups individually.

Initial- and Final Storage Levels

As already briefly mentioned in Case 2, the method of setting the initial- and final storage levels may
pose a weakness in the model. The reasoning behind this approach has already been discussed in Section
6.2.1, but is quickly reiterated here. Since the regular seasons could be sampled from three different
months each the actual reservoir levels can vary greatly depending on which week is chosen. This
way of selecting the initial- and final reservoir levels has both advantages and weaknesses. One major
drawback is the potential for the model to either overestimate or underestimate the hydropower potential,
depending on the variability of the initial- and final reservoir levels. Overestimating the potential may
result in excessive spillage to manage the surplus water, while underestimating it may lead to increased
charging to reach the desired storage level. Consequently, the model most likely has more spillage and
charging than it realistically should. Additionally, the model can lose or gain water by having different
start- and end criteria in subsequent years. Case 2 demonstrated that these weaknesses did not have
significant consequences for the model's overarching components. However, it is crucial to remain
aware of this limitation, especially if a more detailed analysis is required.

Data Shortcomings

The implementation of the node formulation required data on inflow as well as maximum hydro reservoir
levels and filling levels for all countries in the model. The search for this data for all countries was
unsuccessful, meaning these data had to be approximated. Even though a thorough analysis has been
conducted to get realistic data, there is no way of checking their validity. This could reduce the realism
of the produced results.

A new scenario generation method, elaborated upon in Chapter 8, has been devised to enhance the rep-
resentation of seasons based on probabilities derived from historical hydro inflow data. These probabili-
ties are designed by historical inflow data specifically for Norway, thereby improving the representation
of Norway. While Norway holds significant importance in regulated hydropower production and thus
warrants thorough estimation, it is essential to recognize that other countries may not be adequately
accounted for in the scenario generation process. Therefore, while this approach enhances the repre-
sentation of Norway, caution should be exercised regarding the generalization of the scenarios to other
European countries.

No Increase in Inflow

                                                                                                                   99
Chapter 9. Computational Study

There are no connections between inflow and hydro storage investments in the model. In reality, expand-
ing reservoir capacity, such as by constructing new reservoirs, allows for a greater volume of inflow to
be stored. However, this relationship is not currently accounted for in the model because it is nonlinear
and it is therefore difficult to estimate precisely how much additional inflow would increase. Conse-
quently, investments in storage capacity do not lead to an increase in inflow, as the model relies solely
on historical data for inflow estimation. Despite this limitation, the impact on the model is believed to be
minimal. This is because investments in hydro storage capacity are capped at 10% above current levels.
This cap is based on the assumption that most of the hydropower potential is already developed. Given
the limited scope of investments, it is deemed reasonable to assume no direct connection between inflow
and capacity investment. One could also argue that because of climate change, it is realistic to increase
the inflow in the years to come as discussed in section 8.2. However, this effect is also neglected.
Central System Planner

In EMPIRE it is assumed there is a central system planner that optimizes investments and short-term
operations for Europe. While these solutions are optimal for Europe as a whole, they may not necessarily
align with the interests of individual countries. This can lead to conflicting priorities in investment
decisions and operational strategies.

For instance, investments in transmission may benefit Europe overall but could have adverse effects on
specific countries. As already discussed in Section 2.1.1, creating a connection between two countries
may cause price contagion on the electricity price, as exemplified by the HVDC connection between
Norway and Germany. This connection may not be optimal for Norway, even though it is for Europe as
a whole. Thus, what is optimal for Europe may not necessarily be optimal for individual nations.

Similarly, there may also be conflicting interests when it comes to the operational stage. Every country
wants to maximize their profits when producing electricity, however, this may lead to situations where
the optimal total generation mix is not reached. For example, generating electricity from VRES is
profitable, as these have virtually no operating/fuel cost, however, due to stability issues and security of
supply, not everybody can produce using just VRES.

Another notable weakness of EMPIRE lies in its failure to fully integrate political discussions and so-
cietal resistance into the modeling. For instance, when considering the expansion of wind onshore
projects, the model may overlook the significant political debates and local opposition that can hinder
such developments. Ignoring political discourse and public resistance can lead to unrealistic projections,
as it fails to capture the complexities of reality. Consequently, recommendations that are technically ef-
ficient but politically not possible may be provided by the model.

Concluding Remarks

10.1 Conclusion

In this master's thesis, modifications are made to the power system model EMPIRE with the objective of
improving its tactical hydropower planning. The motivation behind this is that hydropower is anticipated
to play a significant role in the future of the European power system because of its enormous potential
to provide clean and renewable power and flexibility in the form of hydro reservoirs. Consequently, the
aim of this master's thesis is to implement a node formulation to better capture the value of hydropower.
The change from the scenario formulation to the node formulation is done to facilitate inter-seasonal con-
nections efficiently, enabling the transfer of energy between seasons and planning hydropower schedul-
ing over a longer time horizon. Additionally, the possibility of branching the year into several scenarios
is added to increase the amount of uncertainty in the model. Even though the uncertainty of the model is
not tested, a key finding from Chapter 3 is that all the optimization models facilitating the connection of
storage levels across every period of the year are deterministic. This indicates that the node formulation
in EMPIRE more effectively represents uncertainty in hydropower management compared to the most
similar optimization models reviewed.
Through testing, it is demonstrated that inter-seasonal connections significantly improves the tactical
hydropower management capabilities of the EMPIRE model. By interconnecting the seasons, the model
can leverage the flexibility of hydropower, allowing the shift of hydro production from periods of low
demand and excess water to periods of high demand and limited inflow. This enhanced capability to
plan hydropower operations more effectively aligns with real-world hydropower dynamics.
To optimize the performance of the node formulation, several modeling and technical improvements
are implemented. These include a new storage balance that integrates all elements interacting with the
reservoir and a revised scenario generation process. Although these modifications have not undergone
direct testing, they are strongly believed to enhance the node formulation and, consequently, the mod-
eling results by making the model closer to a real-world energy system. Conversely, the modeling of

                                                            101
Chapter 10. Concluding Remarks
initial and final storage levels and the distribution of weekly inflow underwent careful testing. The cur-
rent approach, which uses different initial and final storage levels based on the scenario sample and an
evenly distributed inflow, is considered sufficient for the model to behave as intended.
As demonstrated in Section 9.6 the enhanced EMPIRE formulation shows several promising trends.
The European generation mix demonstrates a shift in 2060 compared to 2020 towards a predominantly
renewable energy mix comprising sources like wind, solar, and hydropower. Moreover, there is a notable
increase in transmission volume over the years between the countries in Europe, indicating that the
European power grid becomes more tightly connected. This facilitates a better utilization of the energy
potential of Europe as a whole.
While the enhanced EMPIRE formulation is proven to better represent tactical hydropower planning,
it is important to acknowledge that other aspects of the model might be negatively affected to varying
degrees. The most notable impact is the uncertainty representation. The node formulation represents
uncertainty less effectively, partly due to fewer season samples for some seasons and the assumption of
perfect information between certain seasons. Moreover, the effects on other parts of the model, such
as wind and solar, are not thoroughly investigated within the scope of this thesis. Despite these limi-
tations, the primary objective of this thesis--to enhance tactical hydropower planning is achieved. For
analyses specifically aimed at future hydropower planning, the node formulation offers a more realis-
tic representation than the previous scenario formulation. Thus, while further studies are necessary to
fully understand the implications for the entire energy system, this thesis successfully demonstrates a
significant advancement in the modeling of hydropower within the EMPIRE framework.

10.2 Future Work

Even though the node formulation has improved the tactical hydropower planning of the model, there
are still several possibilities for improvements.

Peak Seasons

The current implementation of the peak seasons has a challenge that should be addressed. They are
currently placed at the end of the year which means the final storage level constraint applies to the final
hour of the peak 2 season. As demonstrated in the results this affects the model and makes some of the
operational variables less realistic.

To address this challenge, two potential solutions emerge. These approaches aim to mitigate any impact
on initial and final reservoir levels by avoiding peak seasons at the beginning or end of the year. The
first solution involves integrating peak seasons within the other seasons. A natural placement would be
either after or within the winter period since that is where the peak seasons are most likely to occur, as
indicated in Section 8.5. Alternatively, the second solution proposes isolating peak seasons from regular
seasons entirely, meaning that they would not be included in the scenario tree. This disconnection would
ensure that peak seasons no longer coincide with or depend on regular seasonal patterns.

Stability Testing

As already discussed in Section 9.7, the node formulation has reduced the amount of uncertainty in
the model. To increase the amount one could include several branches or increase the number of start
nodes. However, this would come at the expense of run time. There is therefore a trade-off between the
number of start nodes, the number of branches and run time. The ideal combination of these three can be
explored through stability testing, which involves running the model several times using different input
configurations and assessing the robustness of the model's performance.

Scenario Generation

In this master's thesis, the primary focus has been on scenario generation driven by variations in hy-
drological inflow. While this approach effectively introduces uncertainty into hydropower modeling, it
overlooks other important factors such as solar and wind conditions. Exploring these aspects of scenario
generation falls beyond the scope of this thesis. Consequently, unintended correlations between differ-
ent scenario input datasets may have arisen. Addressing such issues could involve altering the clustering
method used for scenario generation. For example, instead of clustering based solely on hydrological in-
flow, it may be beneficial to consider clustering based on total load or total net load or develop a method
that takes both data from hydro inflow and other VRES sources into consideration.

Parameter Validation

While working on this master's thesis, parameter validation has not been a priority. The parameter values
already implemented in the model have been assumed to be realistic enough for this study. However,
in Chapter 9, some potential problems with the parameters have been discovered. For instance, all the

                                                                                                                 103
Chapter 10. Concluding Remarks
case studies indicate that the hydropower discharging variables in Norway are more constrained than
in the rest of Europe. This finding suggests there may be an issue with either the parameters regarding
initial capacity or the parameters that restrict capacity expansion. In Section 9.6, where the results are
compared with other forecasts, notable differences in the initial power mix between EMPIRE and the
compared forecast are observed. Especially the amount of non-renewables seems to be underestimated
in the EMPIRE model. Additionally, nuclear technology is phased out in the Standard case throughout
the time horizon. These results indicate potential issues with initial generation capacities, investment
parameters, or cost parameters. Therefore, validating the parameters could lead to even more realistic
results.
