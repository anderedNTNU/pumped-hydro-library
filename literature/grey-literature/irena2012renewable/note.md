# IRENA cost analysis of hydropower (IRENA, 2012)

IRENA working paper, part of a five-report renewable cost series (hydro, wind, biomass, CSP, PV) (Preface, PDF p. 3). Reviewers include Truls Holtedahl (Norconsult) and EDF, IHA, World Bank, NREL staff. All costs in real 2010 USD.
**Page convention:** printed page = PDF page − 6 (PDF 7 = p. 1); Key findings = p. i (PDF 5). 42 PDF pages, read in full.

## Research question / purpose
Provide policy-makers with comparable, transparent cost data for hydropower: equipment cost, total installed project cost and LCOE, by country/region and project type, from the perspective of an individual investor; excludes subsidies, system balancing costs, CO2 pricing and externalities (pp. 1–2).

## Setting
- **System:** global hydropower; run-of-river, reservoir and pumped storage; large/small hydro; refurbishment/upgrades.
- **Markets:** not modelled; notes that hydro's value from peak prices and ancillary services is outside LCOE (p. 27, fn 18: worth USD 0.01–0.05/kWh extra, sometimes more).
- **Horizon and resolution:** static cost data, 2010 USD.

## Method
- **Model type:** literature and database review; simple discounted-cash-flow LCOE, Eq. on p. 3 (LCOE = Σ(I_t+M_t+F_t)/(1+r)^t ÷ ΣE_t/(1+r)^t).
- **How uncertainty is represented:** cost ranges; discount-rate sensitivity (3/7/10 %) and lifetime (40/80 yr) (p. 31, Table 6.1).
- **Key assumptions:** 10 % cost of capital (p. 27, fn 17); no material cost reductions to 2020 (p. 26).

## Data / case
Hall et al. (2003) database of 2155 US projects (43 GW); Lako et al. (2003) 250 projects (202 GW); Black & Veatch (2012) 500 MW greenfield breakdown; IRENA/GIZ small-hydro survey; Ecofys et al. (2011) EU small hydro (pp. 18–30).

## Key findings
- (p. i, Table 1) Installed cost: large hydro USD 1050–7650/kW; small hydro 1300–8000/kW; **refurbishment/upgrade 500–1000/kW**. O&M 2–2.5 %/yr (large), 1–4 % (small), 1–6 % (upgrades). LCOE 0.02–0.19 (large), 0.02–0.27 (small), **0.01–0.05 USD/kWh (refurbishment/upgrade)** at 10 % WACC.
- (p. i, item 5; p. 4) With large reservoirs hydropower "can store energy over weeks, months, seasons or even years" and provide the full range of ancillary services.
- (p. 4) "Hydropower is the only large-scale and cost-efficient storage technology available today."
- (p. 8) Classification: run-of-river, reservoir (storage) and pumped storage schemes; design choice between high capacity / low capacity factor (peaking) and low capacity / high capacity factor (base load) "will depend on the costs and likely revenue streams from different configurations" (p. 8; also p. 10, 27).
- (p. 9) Run-of-river design trades turbine capacity against spill; "The value of the electricity produced will determine what the trade-off between capacity and spilled water will be".
- (p. 9) Pumped storage "are not energy sources but instead are storage devices"; with conventional storage hydro they are "the only large-scale, low-cost electricity storage options available today"; PHS ≈ 2.2 % of US capacity, 18 % Japan, 19 % Austria; PHS is generally more expensive than conventional storage hydro and good sites are hard to find.
- (p. 10, Fig. 2.3) Lifecycle cost comparison (2010 USD/kWh): PHS and CAES far cheaper than lead-acid and Li-ion batteries.
- (p. 10) Norway's technical long-term PHS potential **10–25 GW (35 TWh or more)**, could almost double the present 29 GW installed capacity (citing EURELECTRIC 2011).
- (p. 14, 16) Global PHS capacity 120–150 GW (central 136 GW end-2010, up from 98 GW in 2005); ~4 GW added in 2010; interest rising where PV/wind shares are high; OECD focus on modernisation and new PHS.
- (p. 7, 20) Component lifetimes 30–80 years; plants > 100 years with regular E&M upgrades; economic lifetime ≥ 40 years, 80 years as upper bound (large hydro).
- (p. 17–18, 23) Civil works dominate large-hydro cost (≈ three-quarters site-specific); E&M cost strongly correlated with capacity; greenfield typically USD 1000–3500/kW; Black & Veatch 500 MW example: USD 3500/kW ± 35 % (reservoir 26 %, tunnel 14 %, powerhouse/shafts + equipment ~30 %) (p. 22, Fig. 4.5).
- (p. 20–21) Refurbishment: life extension (like-for-like, ≈ 2.5 % capacity gain, ≈ 60 % of greenfield E&M cost) vs upgrades (+10–30 % capacity, up to 90 % of greenfield E&M cost).
- (p. 24–25) O&M 1–4 % of capex/yr (IEA 2.2 % large hydro); Ecofys: USD 45/kW/yr large, 52/kW/yr small.
- (p. 31, Table 6.1) LCOE rises 85–90 % when the discount rate goes from 3 % to 10 %; insensitive to 40 vs 80 years lifetime.

## Strengths
- Compact, widely cited source of hydro cost ranges and refurbishment cost logic; explicit methodology and system boundaries.
- Clear statements on hydro/PHS as the only mature large-scale storage (2012).

## Weaknesses / limitations
- 2012 data (2010 USD); much of it secondary (IPCC 2011, IEA, Black & Veatch, Hall 2003).
- PHS costs are not separately quantified (only Fig. 2.3 lifecycle-cost bars); no cost split between pump-turbine capacity and reservoir/energy capacity.
- LCOE ignores the storage and flexibility value that is the core of our thesis; it treats PHS only qualitatively.
- Two-column layout with interleaved text in the extraction; some table values (Table 6.1) are not machine-readable in `source.txt` — read the PDF.
