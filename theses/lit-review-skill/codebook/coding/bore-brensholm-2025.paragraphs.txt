### 4.2 Stochaciticy  (5 paragraphs)

[P04] section 4.2, pos 1/5
Capturing the stochasticity associated with hydropower planning is highly relevant in an operations research setting. While deterministic models often provide useful insights to decision-making, Belsnes et al. (2016) showcased in their study that adding stochasticity enhances profit margins by allocating production to expensive hours and reducing the risk of spillage. This was demonstrated in a case study by evaluating the strategies obtained from the stochastic and the deterministic models over a new set of scenarios. Consequently, the stochastic model provided more robust solution strategies to the decision-maker while improving the degree of realism.

[P05] section 4.2, pos 2/5
An important element of uncertainty in hydropower planning is inflow. This is frequently recognized in literature review across all types of planning horizons (Abgottspon & Andersson, 2013; Belsnes et al., 2016; Helseth et al., 2016, 2017). Inflow to a given reservoir is determined to a large extent by the temperature conditions in the area where the hydropower plant is located. Hence, temperature can also be treated as a stochastic variable in a hydropower planning model, as seen in the SOVN model (Warland & Mo, 2016). Temperature is also closely linked to electricity consumption patterns, influencing the demand curve used to determine the market-clearing price (Idsø et al., 2024). This relationship is particularly relevant in long-term models, where prices are derived through a market-clearing process, as seen in the EMPS model (SINTEF, 2025).

[P06] section 4.2, pos 3/5
Building on this, it provides a rationale for modelling prices as stochastic variables in short- to mid-term hydropower optimization models. Schä"er et al. (2022) implement stochastic market prices in their study, in addition to a generic stochastic environmental variable, which implicitly represents temperature by indicating whether a low inflow period has ended. Aasgård (2020) and Faria and Fleten (2011), on the other hand, do not include any explicit environmental variables and instead focus on market prices. Various configurations of modelling stochastic market prices have also been observed in several of the papers mentioned above. Ideally, a model should take all prices across the existing markets into account, as they are subject to uncertainty (Belsnes et al., 2016). However, treating all prices as independent stochastic variables would possibly yield the curse of dimensionality when generating scenarios for the problem.

[P07] section 4.2, pos 4/5
Hence, the literature appears to converge on the importance of incorporating stochasticity into models across different planning horizons, as this improves the realism of the results. The most prominent stochastic variables to include are prices and inflows.

[P08] section 4.2, pos 5/5
28 Uncertain prices are particularly relevant in models where actors operate as price takers, while uncertain inflow is important to consider irrespective of how the price is determined. Regardless, researchers often limit the number of stochastic variables to avoid the curse of dimensionality.

### 4.3 Hydraulic modelling  (5 paragraphs)

[P09] section 4.3, pos 1/5
Section 3.1.5 showed the non-linear relationship between generated power and net head. To achieve sufficient precision of a hydropower system, accurate hydraulic modelling is often essential, as pinpointed in a meeting with Professor Arild Helseth at the NTNU Department of Electric Energy (A. Helseth, personal commmunication, 07th of November 2025). When incorporating the HPF into a model, it becomes crucial to select a method that both captures the underlying physical relationships and aligns with the problem requirements. This is often a trade-o" between computational complexity and the desired level of model detail.

[P10] section 4.3, pos 2/5
Several techniques for modelling the HPF exist, which depend on the time horizon, temporal resolution, system size, the available data, and the goals of the optimization model (Kong et al., 2020). The study points out piecewise linear approximation as a common technique to account for the non-linear HPF. The breakpoints, defined as pairs of water discharge and power output, are typically specified in advance and represented either as a two-dimensional table for fixed-head conditions or as a three-dimensional matrix when head variations are considered (Kong et al., 2020). A closely related approach described in Chazarra et al. (2016) is discretizing a family of non-linear curves and using binary auxiliary variables to indicate which section of the efficiency curve is activated for a given time step. As such, this approach can be characterized as a mixed integer linear program (MILP). The drawback of a MILP is that it often can become computationally burdensome when the size of the problem increases (Kong et al., 2020). It is important to consider this in relation to the purpose of the hydropower planning model.

[P11] section 4.3, pos 3/5
In contrast to the MILP approach, Gjelsvik and Haugstad (2005) and Belsnes et al. (2016) present a less computationally demanding approach to the HPF modelling. Gjelsvik and Haugstad (2005) present ProdRisk, a mid-term hydropower scheduling model developed by Sintef Energy Research. It ignores the variable head in the HPF and introduces correction coefficients in the form of head sensitivities instead. This is done through two separate simulations, in which the last simulation aims to correct the initial simulation through the inclusion of head sensitivities. Similarly,

[P12] section 4.3, pos 4/5
29 Belsnes et al. (2016) use an iterative method called successive linear programming, where the efficiency related to the net head is updated based on the efficiency used in the previous time step. To avoid flip-flopping solutions and ensure convergence, the efficiencies are linearized using a first-order approximation of the coupling between efficiency and a marginal increase in reservoir level. In addition, a penalty is added to the objective function for deviating from the previous efficiencies in the iterative process. The study concludes that implementing a head-of-water optimization improves the model solution, especially when the reservoir is half-full due to its flexibility.

[P13] section 4.3, pos 5/5
In the continuation of this, it is recognized that modelling hydraulic behavior can be decisive for model performance, but some approaches might lead to increased computational complexity. Thus, this trade-o" needs to be tailored to fit the purpose of the model.

### 4.4 Multimarket dynamics and participation  (8 paragraphs)

[P14] section 4.4, pos 1/8
Norwegian hydropower producers operate across energy and reserve markets, leveraging the storage capability to maximize revenues (SINTEF Energi, 2025). Modelling multiple markets can therefore provide more accurate income estimates and a more realistic representation of hydropower's true value than single-market approaches. Consequently, reviewing how the literature addresses multi-market participation across modelling frameworks is essential to capturing the full value-creation potential that this work aims to explore.

[P15] section 4.4, pos 2/8
It is evident that the literature provides studies with different configurations of market participation. Aasgård (2020) investigates a multi-market bidding problem considering the DA-market and mFRR-market using a price-taking approach, without the inclusion of reserve activation. Her model co-optimizes the revenue gained from participating in both markets, and a case study from NO2 shows positive gains from such coordinated bidding. Chazarra et al. (2016) report similar results when modelling coordinated bidding in DA-markets and the secondary reserve markets (aFRR) in a Spanish 7-day case study, as this yields significantly higher income compared to DA-participation only. Although the number of connected reservoirs and power plants may vary between the studies, they both show the benefits of multi-market optimization. A potential limitation with the latter model, however, is that it assumes that an expected fixed percentage of the contracted reserves is always activated. While this exogenous parameter is determined through statistical analysis, this is a simplified behaviour of the market, potentially yielding too optimistic results. On the contrary, the model mitigates risk in terms of water shortage and spillage, thereby reducing the overall risk associated with the strategy suggested by the model.

[P16] section 4.4, pos 3/8
Building on the modelling of activation presented in Chazarra et al. (2016), Farahmand et al. (2020) emphasizes the value of detailed reserve market activation modelling, taking the perspective of the system operator. In particular, the study demonstrates that reserve provision decisions that ignore activation lead to systematically underestimated costs. To a hydropower producer, this manifests as lost water value, adjusted reservoir management, slightly reduced future flexibility, and increased ramping. The latter can lead to additional wear and tear on the hydropower unit.

[P17] section 4.4, pos 4/8
Similar to several of the above-mentioned studies, Faria and Fleten (2011) also present a co-optimization model across the DA market and the Elbas market (Intraday), from a price-taking perspective. [P19] section 4.4, pos 6/8

[P20] section 4.4, pos 7/8

However, in the literature review, it seems worth mentioning the work of Abgottspon and Andersson (2013). They showed that adding forward contracts as an ingredient in the operation of a price-making hydropower plant, as well as pumped storage, contributes to a 20% increase in profit. While the model tends to be realistic and tractable, the results might be coloured by linear dependencies between commodities and market prices, and the assumption that forward contract prices are known.

### 4.5 Relevant solution methods  (9 paragraphs)

[P22] section 4.5, pos 1/9
Within hydropower planning, a range of solution methods is used to handle shortand mid-term operational problems, and long-term strategic decision-making. The choice of method shapes how flexibility, uncertainty, and the value creation of the hydro resources are represented. Reviewing these methods in the literature is therefore important for understanding how different approaches can be utilized, and for positioning our own work.

[P23] section 4.5, pos 2/9
A common solution method used across all hydropower planning horizons is Stochastic (Dual) Dynamic Programming (SDP/SDDP) as seen in various studies (Helseth et al., 2016, 2017, 2022; Pérez-Díaz et al., 2020). Helseth et al. (2016) use the SDDP framework for profit maximization of a hydropower producer selling electricity and capacity in separate markets. The problem is decomposed into weekly subproblems for a given sample of scenarios, and solved through a forward iteration where information is passed from one state to another. Then, at the end of the planning horizon, a backward iteration is performed, and cuts are added to the problem based on sensitivities from the state variables on the objective function. Similarly, Helseth et al. (2017), a SDP-model is used to assess the operational profitability of a hydropower producer participating in the energy and capacity market. The complete planning period is one year, but the problem is decomposed into weekly MILP problems. The SDP decomposition allows for the representation of non-linear and non-convex relationships, and for the given week, the realizations of the stochastic variables are known.

[P24] section 4.5, pos 3/9
32 Péres-Diaz et al. (2020) also use the SDP-framework in their mid-term optimization model, which has a scheduling horizon of one year. However, they decompose the problem into daily decision problems as mixed-integer quadratic programming problems with known exogenous stochastic state variables. These variables were the daily average energy market price, the daily inflow, and the daily residual demand curve in the reserve market. While both SDDP and SDP are common solution methods for stochastic operational decision problems, SDDP might be superior to SDP in terms of computational cost. The SDDP approach increases linearly with the number of scenarios, while the SDP increases exponentially.

[P25] section 4.5, pos 4/9
A conceptually related solution method to SDP/SDDP is the two-stage Benders Decomposition method, identified in numerous studies (Jacobson et al., 2023; Sifuentes & Vargas, 2007; Warland & Mo, 2016). Warland and Mo (2016) show that the Benders decomposition is a suitable solution method to long-term hydro-thermal planning problems. Using the stochastic optimization model for Scandinavia with individual water values and grid restrictions, denoted as SOVN-model, a sequence of problems referred to as scenario fan problems (SFP) is simulated for each time step in the planning period. Each SFP problem is a two-stage stochastic LP problem solved by using Benders decomposition. The first stage represents a given week of the simulation (weather scenario and week), and the second stage is represented by independent scenarios, where the decisions from the first stage problem are taken as input and used to solve the rest of the planning period for each scenario. Their analysis shows promising results, as it yields a better utilization of hydro resources compared to EMPS. This is also a long-term planning model, but it is based on aggregated hydro description and heuristics.

[P26] section 4.5, pos 5/9
In Jacobson et al. (2023), a Benders Decomposition technique for a large-scale energy system planning problem is presented. The complete problem is formulated as a MILP model. Further, it is then decomposed into a master problem, which handles investment decisions, and operational subproblems, handling detailed operational constraints. A key innovation in this work is the decomposition of the operational problem by weeks, despite the planning horizon covering the full 52-week year. This is achieved by introducing budgeting variables that impose weekly limits, thereby allowing the weekly subproblems to be solved independently and in parallel, Jacobson et al. (2023).

[P27] section 4.5, pos 6/9
Sifuentes and Vargas (2007) shows that the Benders decomposition is also applicable when solving a short-term hydro scheduling problem (STHS) with a full AC power flow model. The master problem is a mixed-integer programming problem that minimizes fixed costs associated with hydropower generation and thermal units. Whereas in the subproblem, the Optimal Power Flow (OPF) problem is solved. Then, the Benders cuts are applied to the master problem with respect to the sensitivities of the dual variables obtained from the OPF solution. These studies show that, irrespective of the planning horizon, Benders decomposition could be a relevant solution method.

[P28] section 4.5, pos 7/9
Building on the short-term solution methods presented above, Belsnes et al., (2016) propose a stochastic successive linear programming approach for the STHS problem, explicitly modelling uncertainty via scenario trees. They highlight a limitation of scenario-tree-based multistage stochastic programming, namely the decay of uncertainty as one moves deeper into the tree. The effective uncertainty is reduced, which biases the solution towards determinism. To mitigate this, they discuss the use of rolling horizon schemes, recalculating scenario trees as new information becomes available. They also note that scenario-tree generation and fine temporal resolution raise computational tractability issues when the horizon is very long, or the discretisation is fine, akin to the limitations of SDP. However, the SSLP does not exploit structures in hydropower planning, which avoids the benefit of decomposing the large hydropower planning problem. Thus, this method is primarily suited for STHS problems.

[P29] section 4.5, pos 8/9
While the presented solution methods thus far have focused on single objective optimization, Ahmadi et al. (2015) choose a different approach to the hydropower scheduling problem. They present a deterministic multi-objective optimization (MOO) approach that simultaneously considers emission minimization and profit maximization. In their work, the Normal Boundary Intersection (NBI) method and Weighted-Sum (WS) method are applied to a MILP problem to generate non-inferior solutions and construct the Pareto-optimal frontier. Obtaining this frontier provides stakeholders with valuable insights into the trade-o" between profit and emissions. While considering emissions is beyond the intended scope of our study, MOO represents an important direction for future research, particularly when integrating sustainable energy sources such as wind and solar (Ahlfors & Amelin, 2021).

[P30] section 4.5, pos 9/9
The presented solution methods in this subsection show that there are several approaches to the hydropower planning problem, depending on the inclusion of stochasticity, nonlinearities, multistage, planning horizon, and objectives. SDDP is a promising solution method if all these characteristics are under consideration, while SDP is suitable as long as the planning horizon is short enough to avoid intractable scenario trees. This is also a shortcoming of the SSLP. In contrast, the Benders' decomposition method avoids the planning horizon issue. Simultaneously, the method discussed here is limited to two-stage problems. However, this limitation can be addressed by using a nested Benders approach. Nested Benders essentially extends the classical Benders decomposition method to a scenario tree structure, where the tree can be viewed as a set of nested two-stage problems (Murphy, 2013). If several conflicting objectives are under consideration, MOO techniques such as the NBI and WS can be used to construct Pareto frontiers. In total, these methods provide guidance in how various problems can be solved in a holistic and more realistic manner.

Ahmadi (2015) modelled a detailed market participation framework, but within a multiobjective deterministic context and without distinguishing upward and downward regulation.
