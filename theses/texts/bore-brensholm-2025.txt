Abstract

In an evolving power system, balancing services are expected to become an increas-
ingly important consideration. A growing share of intermittent generation technolo-
gies, increased interconnection capacities, and rising electricity demand are among
the key drivers behind this development. Furthermore, due to the inherent struc-
ture of power markets, flexible resources such as hydropower are well-positioned to
participate across multiple markets. These aspects provide a strong motivation for
investigating how flexible hydropower can be operated more e!ciently across mul-
tiple markets, not only to increase profitability but also to ensure system stability. As
many hydropower plants are ageing and are currently being considered for upgrades,
the question of whether additional investments in capacity upgrades of hydropower
plants can help address the challenges mentioned above.

The project analyses the various electricity and capacity markets with respect to
both their technical characteristics and economic incentives. This is followed by a
literature review revealing a research gap on flexible hydropower plants participating
in a detailed multi-market framework. Thus, the following research problem has been
identified:

Optimization of flexible hydropower operation by incorporating multi-
market characteristics and relevant modelling considerations to quantify
operational performance and revenues.

This research is addressed through a deterministic optimisation model with the ob-
jective of maximising operational revenues across energy and several capacity mar-
kets. A case study of a hydropower producer located in the NO2 price area is
conducted to assess whether multi-market participation can contribute to justifying
a potential capacity upgrade of the power plant.

From an operational perspective, the results indicate that participation across both
energy and reserve markets, without the consideration of activation, leads to less
aggressive plant operation. From an economic perspective, participation in capacity
markets results in increased operational revenues, as the water resource is utilized
more e"ectively. The results further indicate, with the underlying assumptions and
input data employed, that a capacity upgrade could be economically justified. With
further development, the findings also suggest that the proposed model may evolve
into a more robust decision-support tool for evaluating future investments.

1 Introduction

Despite su!cient reservoir levels in Norwegian hydropower reservoirs and relatively
moderate outdoor temperatures on the 12th of December 2024, power prices surged
to 13.37 NOK (Andersen, 2024; NVE, 2025a). This price level is almost twenty times

                 kW h

higher than the annual mean in 2024, illustrating the significant price fluctuations
currently occurring in the market (SSB, 2025a).

In the power system, electricity must be produced and consumed simultaneously to
maintain the system frequency. A range of electricity markets has therefore been de-
veloped to ensure operational feasibility. While long-term financial instruments aim
to provide predictability, the day-ahead (DA) market provides operational guidance
one day before physical delivery. The market-clearing price (MCP) is determined by
the intersection of the supply and demand curves, established through bids placed
according to the merit-order principle. Furthermore, the intraday (ID) market func-
tions as a continuous bilateral trading arena, allowing participants to adjust their
scheduled positions. Closer to real time, several reserve markets are activated to
counteract imbalances and maintain a stable frequency.

Theoretically, the MCP equals the bid of the marginal unit in the merit order required
to satisfy demand. In the European market, this marginal unit is usually a gas-
fired power plant (Blume-Werry et al., 2021). Although hydropower typically has
very low marginal production costs, this is not reflected in its supply bids. On the
one hand, reservoir-based hydropower can defer production to future periods when
electricity is scarcer, creating an opportunity cost associated with alternative uses of
stored water. Furthermore, water in the reservoir functions as a societal safeguard
against costly blackouts, an aspect that must be incorporated into bidding strategies.
This opportunity cost is commonly referred to as the water value, which forms the
basis for the hydropower marginal cost bidding. Moreover, one unit of hydropower
generation can e"ectively displace the marginal gas-fired unit that would otherwise
set the price in the continent, given available transfer capacity. Consequently, the
severe constraints on European gas supply following Russia's invasion of Ukraine
triggered an energy crisis, ultimately driving power prices to notably high levels.

Achieving the target of net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050 requires substan-
tial innovation and regulatory measures within the energy sector. Instruments such
as the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) and rapid cost reductions in wind and
solar technologies have made variable renewable energy sources (VRES) an increas-
ingly important part of the energy mix, as illustrated in Figure 1 and Figure 2a. At

Norway's power system relies heavily on hydropower, which accounts for approxim-
ately 90% of total electricity production. As a weather-dependent resource, hydro-
power is vulnerable to dry periods that can lead to low reservoir levels, meaning
the country cannot always be fully self-su!cient in electricity supply. To mitigate
this risk, Norway has over time invested in several interconnectors to neighbouring
European countries, enabling more e!cient utilisation of electricity across regions
(RME, 2025). Increased integration with the European power market, however, also
exposes Norway to greater uncertainty in continental power prices.

Interconnecting the European grid reflects broader political and technological changes
in the European energy market, necessitating increased flexibility. Mentens et. al.
(2025) emphasize that such flexibility is essential for e"ective grid management and
a reliable power supply. They distinguish three overarching mechanisms: i) spa-
tial adjustment of demand and supply through interconnectors, ii) demand-response
systems facilitated by price signals, and iii) temporal adjustment of supply and de-
mand through storage solutions. Temporal flexibility may be provided by batteries,
prosumers, or upgrades that enhance the flexibility of hydropower plants. However,
uncertainty in battery deployment and future load development favours reservoir-
based hydropower as a stabilizing force in the market (Statnett, 2025b). Warland
and Mo (2016) argue that flexible, fast-responding hydropower plants capable of
producing during peak-price periods will benefit most in an increasingly uncertain
system. This increases the likelihood of operation at capacity bounds. Consequently,
physically accurate modelling of hydropower capacity becomes essential for delivering
the flexibility increasingly required by the evolving energy system.

The growth of intermittent generation, increased interconnection, gas shortages, and
new policy measures all underline the need to study non-conventional hydropower
planning. Belsnes (2016) suggests that hydropower producers may need to allocate a
larger share of their capacity toward the intraday and reserve markets. To optimise
future production, hydropower producers must therefore schedule watercourses in
ways that enable participation in multiple markets simultaneously.

The profitability of providing flexibility in a multi-market setting has already triggered
numerous investment plans among Norwegian hydropower producers. Concurrently,
simple analyses show that increased hydropower flexibility can significantly reduce
power price variability (Messel Larsen, 2024). A clear example is Eviny, a major
Norwegian power producer, which plans to double the installed capacity of three hy-
dropower plants to capture additional market value. Although profit is the primary
motive, such investments are also expected to alleviate price pressure (Messel Larsen,

and frequency stability, can be economically optimized is therefore a highly relevant

1.1 Purpose of the project

This project seeks to establish an understanding of the electricity markets available
to Norwegian power producers, develop a fundamental comprehension of how hydro-
power scheduling is modelled within an optimization framework subject to physical
constraints, and identify research gaps in the academic literature on hydropower
planning. The findings from the literature review indicate that multi-market op-
timisation models have received limited attention, which has led to the following
research questions:

     · What are the operational impacts of multi-market participation on the hydro
        power plant's cost-optimal capacity utilization and ramping hours?

     · What is the added value of including several capacity markets in a multi-market
        optimisation model?

     · How costly can capacity upgrades be while remaining economically viable, given
        the increased earning potential?

The main purpose of this project is therefore to investigate how a deterministic multi-
market model allocates the capacity of a single hydropower plant across the available
markets, and to assess the added value this may provide to the producer. The results
represent an optimistic estimate by construction, but demonstrate that the increased
revenue potential may justify the investment costs reported in industry benchmarks.
Participation in capacity markets causes the production level to fluctuate more fre-
quently, but typically with smaller amplitudes, and the reservoir is emptied less often
due to an overall lower production level. Factors such as the available reserve ca-
pacity and the relative price interaction between individual reserve markets and the
energy markets are decisive for determining which reserve products contribute most
to increasing revenues in an optimization setting. The work presented in this project
can serve as a base for future research on more realistic modelling of multi-market
participation.

2.1 The Norwegian power market and system

The Norwegian power market was liberalized on the 1st of January 1991, when the
Energy Act was o!cially implemented (Moen & Johansen, 1992). This meant that
the supply and demand of electrical energy were subject to the principles of mar-
ket clearing to increase overall economic e!ciency. A clear distinction was thereby
established between the competitive supply and demand of electric power through
market mechanisms and the monopolistic principle of governing electricity distribu-

                                                     6
Project Report     TIØ4500

tion through the power grid.

The Norwegian power grid can be divided into three main levels: the transmission
level, the regional level, and the distribution level. The Norwegian power grid is
regulated by The Norwegian Regulathory Authorty (RME), and one of the main
responsibilities of RME is to ensure e!cient energy distribution management in
Norway by facilitating sustainable grid development and improving the utilization of
existing power grid. Statnett, the Norwegian Transmission System Operator (TSO),
operates and maintains the transmission grid and is responsible for ensuring that the
power system remains balanced (Statnett, 2025a). The various reserve markets are
described under Section 2.4.

2.2 Day-Ahead market

The Day-Ahead (DA) market is the wholesale market where supply and demand
for electricity are determined. Here, producers and consumers submit bids on both
volume and price, and then the market is cleared for the coming 24 hours. At 10:00
CET, the available transmission capacities on interconnectors are published, and
by 12:00 CET, every market participant must have submitted their bids. Then,
the common European coupling process called Single Day-Ahead Coupling uses the
Euphemia algorithm to clear the market every hour for the coming 24 hours (Nord
Pool, 2023a).

From the 30th of September 2025, the European power system changed its time
resolution from hourly to sub-hourly (15 min) (Statnett, 2022). The transition to
quarterly time resolution is an important measure to meet the climate targets set
by European authorities. More precisely, the measure aims to smooth out structural
imbalances in the power system caused by a disaligned production profile compared
to the load change.

2.3 Intraday market

To better align supply and demand between the DA market and real-time operations,
the intraday (ID) market provides an additional platform for securing the necessary
system balance. Once the TSO has calculated the flows based on the results from
the DA auction, the available capacities for the ID market are published. The ID

market functions as a bilateral trading platform where participants can trade up to
one hour before delivery (Nord Pool, 2023b). The market platform for the ID market
is called SIDC (Nord Pool, 2023c).

2.4 Reserve markets

As the delivery period approaches, further market imbalances may arise. Therefore, a
range of support services exists to ensure the simultaneous balance between electricity
production and consumption, which, in turn, maintains the frequency at 50 Hz in
the Norwegian power system. The markets helping maintain this balance are known
as the reserve markets and are operated by Statnett. The system imbalances can
come in terms of both under- and over-frequency. If the system frequency falls below
50 Hz, balance can be restored by increasing power output to the grid or by reducing
power consumption. Conversely, if the frequency rises above 50 Hz, balancing can
be achieved by decreasing power output or by increasing the amount of power drawn
from the grid.

To achieve a smooth balancing, there is a range of di"erent support services, which
can essentially be divided into four main categories: FFR, FCR, aFRR, and mFRR
(Statnett, 2023). The set of reserve products all serve di"erent purposes in maintain-
ing a balanced frequency. In Figure 4, it can be observed how an under-frequency is
handled through an increase in power from the various ancillary services.

2.4.1 FFR - Fast Frequency Reserves

FFR is the fastest responding reserve and must be activated within one second. The
reserve was introduced quite recently, and exists to ensure that the frequency does not
fall below 49 Hz, and is activated once the frequency falls below a certain threshold
set between 49.5 and 49.7 Hz. The system operator, i.e., Statnett, is responsible for
the procurement of FFR capacity. To limit the possibility of over-dimensioning the
procurement of reserve capacity, FFR is acquired in two main products: A seasonal
profile with limited capacity on weekends and nighttime (FFR Profil), and a more
flexible profile delivered based on request by the system operator and short-term
forecasts (FFR Flex). For the seasonal procurement, the minimum bid capacity is
1MW, but for FFR Flex, the minimum bid capacity is 5 MW (Statnett, 2025d).

2.4.2 FCR - Frequency Containment Reserves

FCR is the primary reserve and can be split into two main reserves, FCR-N and FCR-
D. FCR-N is activated within the normal frequency band, 49.9 Hz ­ 50.1 Hz, and is
intended to reduce frequency deviations under normal conditions. Thus, providers of
FCR-N must be able to supply both upwards and downwards regulation, reflecting
the symmetric nature of the product. With larger imbalances related to faults in
production units, consumption units, or transmission components, the FCR-D is
activated. FCR-D does not have the same symmetric requirement as FCR-N, and
for now, downregulation in FCR-D is absent (Statnett, 2025c).

To ensure su!cient capacity from primary frequency reserves, a marketplace has
been established to determine the natural intersection between supply and demand
of reserve capacity. This market consists of two sub-markets, known as D1 and
D2. The minimum bid size in both markets is 0.1 MW, and the maximum response
time is 30 seconds. To be eligible for participation in the FCR market, each market
provider must be approved by Statnett, act as the balancing responsible for that
specific object, and hold an approved prequalification for the specific object (Statnett,
2025c).

The D2 market is executed before the DA market and cleared two days ahead with
hourly resolution per bidding zone. Bidders are allowed to submit bids between
00:00 and 17:30 two days ahead of the operational day. Awarded bids are notified
by Statnett at 18:30 (Statnett, 2025h).

Further, the D1 market is conducted after the DA market to cover residual require-
ments, including exchange requests from neighbouring TSOs. The D1 market is
cleared daily for the next operational day with hourly resolution per bidding zone.
Bidders are allowed to submit bids between 00:00 and 18:00 one day ahead of the
operational day. Awarded bids are notified by Statnett by 19:00 this day, which
then reveals the price (Statnett, 2025h). The separation into D2 and D1 markets
improves predictability because the D2 market runs before the DA auction, while
the D1 market runs afterward to cover remaining needs and exchange requests from
other TSOs.

2.4.3 aFRR - automatic Frequency Restoration Reserve

Secondary reserves are referred to as aFRR, and they are procured for both up and
down regulation. aFRR is activated to restore the frequency to 50.00 Hz. This, in
turn, releases the activated primary reserve, making it available for any new faults
and imbalances that may occur. The maximum time for full activation is currently
set at 5 minutes after the TSO sends a signal. However, Statnett targets a faster
response, ideally within 2 minutes or less. The minimum allowed bid size is 1 MW.
Bids can be submitted starting from 7 days before the operational day, and must
be submitted for each daily auction. Suppliers to this capacity market must submit
their bids by 07:30 CET the day before the operational day, but can be modified or
withdrawn up until the deadline. The need for aFRR is dimensioned per el-spot area,
and Statnett, being the system operator, clears the market before every operating
day. (Statnett, 2024a). The resulting market prices are published at 09 a.m. on the
operating day.

2.4.4 mFRR - manual Frequency Restoration Reserve

The mFRR, known as the tetriary reserve, releases the activated secondary reserve,
aFRR. For mFRR, there exsists two markets: a capacity market and an activation
market. In the capacity market, participants are paid for guaranteeing that their
reserved capacity can be utilized, with separate markets for both down- and up-
regulation. The activation market works by providers submitting bids with prices
for regulating production and consumption. The bids are sorted into a common list,
and when balancing is needed, the cheapest resources will be activated first (Statnett,
2024b). For mFRR, each bidding zone must maintain its own balance, but can receive

The mFRR is the slowest and most energy-intensive reserve. Each bid must be at

2.5 Future considerations and research in the power market

The need for electricity and better ancillary services is expected to increase moving
further towards 2050 (DNV, 2024). The reserve markets must therefore undergo
several structural changes to handle this development. It is still early to determine
how these developments and implementations will a"ect the markets, and they are
therefore only discussed briefly in this subsection.

Firstly, the Nordic TSOs are planning to transition to an activation market where
the aFRR demand will be calculated per bidding zone. Then, the cheapest bids will
be selected for activation through a common optimization, which takes into account
transmission constraints between zones. This transition is a part of the Nordic
balancing model (NBM), and will take place when Statnett connects to the European
Platform for the International Coordination of Automated Frequency Restoration
and Stable System Operation, commonly known as PICASSO (Statnett, 2025e).
Statnett is expected to join PICASSO in late 2027/early 2028.

Secondly, for the mFRR activation market, the Nordic TSOs will join the Manually
Activated Reserve Initiative (MARI) platform. MARI is a European platform for
the exchange of mFRR. As with PICASSO, this is also a part of the transition to
the Nordic balancing model (Statnett, 2025g). Statnett is expected to join MARI
somewhere around the turn of the year 2026 and 2027. Modelling the activation
markets is not considered in this project, but rather mentioned as possible future
work.

An additional structural change of importance is the transition to a 15-minute time
resolution. In the DA market, this change is scheduled for 30th September 2025,
while the ID market adopted 15-minute intervals in Norway on 18th March 2025.
Since this project relies on hourly data, a finer time resolution could reveal dynamics
not captured here. Nevertheless, the long-term implications of this transition remain
an interesting topic for future consideration.

Lastly, it is also relevant to consider how reserve market prices have been a"ected by
the transition to flow-based market coupling (FBMC). A preliminary examination of
this development may indicate its potential influence on the future power market. On
the 29th October 2024, FBMC was introduced in the Nordic region. FBMC optimizes
the allocation of grid capacity within and across European countries by accounting
for the actual physical power flows in the grid rather than relying solely on predefined
bilateral limits. Under FBMC, the allocation of cross-border interconnection capacity

3 Hydropower

The power output of a reservoir-based hydropower plant depends on the interaction
of technical components, physical processes, and economic considerations. Determ-
ining an optimal production level while accounting for temporal coupling, non-linear
dynamics, and uncertainty is di!cult without advanced optimization methods. This
section outlines the key elements relevant to the modelling process, beginning with
the physical and technical characteristics of hydropower systems and then addressing
the associated economic considerations.

3.1 Hydropower from a physical and technical perspective

Over time, the weather-dependent hydrological cycle has shaped the landscape into
interconnected systems of oceans, rivers, lakes, and glaciers collectively referred to
as water courses (Bonsor, 2001; LVK, n.d.). Hydropower utilizes the topographical
di"erences within these water courses to convert the kinetic energy of water into
electrical energy. In Norway, approximately 2700 of the 4000 water courses have
been developed for this purpose, leading hydropower to account for about 90% of
the national power mix (LVK, n.d. WWF Norge, n.d.). Many of these systems are
reservoir-based, where key elements include the reservoir, inflow, penstock, generat-
ing unit, and hydraulic couplings. A schematic of such a power plant is shown in
Figure 9 to support the following elaboration.

Figure 9: Schematic illustration of a hydropower plant (Kasper, n.d.).

3.1.1 Storage using the reservoir

The reservoir enables water to be stored during periods of high inflow and released
during dry periods (LVK, n.d.). It also allows the plant to exploit altitude di"erences,
commonly referred to as the hydraulic head. The water level must remain between
the Highest Regulated Water Level (HRWL) and the Lowest Regulated Water Level
(LRWL) to satisfy ecological and safety requirements. These limits determine the
amount of water that can be stored in a given time step and thereby the available
potential energy. In modelling, the reservoir level is typically determined by a balance
equation relating inflow, discharge, and storage.

3.1.2 Filling up the reservoir with inflow

Seasonal precipitation patterns and temperature variations largely determine the in-
flow to the reservoir. The inflow originates from the parts of the water course located
at equal or higher elevation. Producers are primarily interested in the utilizable in-
flow, defined as the portion of water that can be used for production within a given
period (NVE, 2024a). Estimating utilizable inflow requires information on snow
storage, the unsaturated zone, and drainage sections (NVE, 2015). These factors
contribute to determining the feasible production region. Including the avoidance of
curtailment, an involuntary reduction in a generator's output caused by grid restric-
tions, and spillage of excess water influences it even further (Gandhi et al., 2022;
IFC, 2015). In modelling, inflow may be represented as a deterministic parameter or
as a stochastic variable.

3.1.3 Discharging water through the penstock

To convert potential energy into kinetic energy, water stored in the reservoir is re-
leased into a penstock under hydrostatic pressure, directing the flow toward the tur-
bine. Penstocks are typically tunnels or pipelines constructed within the mountains
(LVK, n.d.). The cross-sectional dimensions and frictional properties of the penstock
determine the instantaneous flow capacity and thereby influence the volume of water
that can reach the turbine at any moment. In modelling, the penstock can impose a
physical capacity constraint on the rate of discharge.

                19
Project Report                                               TIØ4500

3.1.4 Converting to electrical energy using the generating unit

The generating unit converts the kinetic energy of flowing water into electrical energy,
and consists of a turbine and a generator. Water entering the turbine causes the rotor
to spin, inducing an electromagnetic field in the generator and producing electricity
(EDP, 2025). The e!ciency of the generating unit, defined as the ratio of electrical
output to mechanical input, a"ects the e"ective available capacity and must be
accounted for in the modelling framework.

3.1.5 Using the hydropower production function to determine the gen-
          erated power

In hydropower modelling, it is essential to represent the relationship between the
physical elements described above in a realistic manner. From a flexibility perspect-
ive, this is particularly important, as these relationships influence the calculated
capacity for participation in reserve markets. However, the underlying physics are
far from linear, but instead governed by a complex set of interdependent processes.
To capture these dynamics, researchers typically employ a hydropower production
function (HPF), which describes how the available water resource is converted into
electrical energy through a non-linear relationship (Diniz & Maceira, 2008).

                P = g · 103 · T (q, h) · G(P ) · q · h [MW]  (1)

The HPF in (1) expresses the electrical power output as the product of the gravita-
tional acceleration g, the mass density of water absorbed in the factor 103 for unit
conversion, the turbine e!ciency T , the generator e!ciency G, the discharge q, and
the net head h. The net head depends not only on the geometric altitude di"erence
between the intake and outflow, but also on the reservoir level at each point in time.
Because reservoir geometry is often irregular, the relationship between reservoir level
and net head is generally non-linear.

Moreover, the turbine e!ciency varies with both discharge and head, while the gen-
erator e!ciency depends on the produced power. These functional dependencies
introduce nonlinearity directly into the HPF, making it computationally demanding
to include in large-scale optimization models. To maintain tractability, particularly
in mid- and long-term scheduling models, it is therefore common to apply simplifying
approximations of the HPF.

3.2 Key elements from an economic perspective

The physical characteristics of hydropower provide the basis for understanding the
economic decisions faced by producers. Producers must define planning horizons,
choose pricing strategies, and identify relevant markets. They must also consider
cost drivers, particularly when evaluating investments. The following sections discuss
these elements in more detail.

3.2.1 Water value

Section 1 introduced the term water value and explained how it is used to price an
otherwise seemingly inexpensive resource by reflecting its alternative use through
storage. To understand this concept more thoroughly, it is necessary to consider
how seasonal variations a"ect the reservoir filling level. In spring, electricity prices
are typically low and snowmelt generates substantial inflow, causing reservoir levels
to rise as production is reduced. During autumn and winter, by contrast, inflow de-
clines while electricity demand and prices increase, leading to higher discharge rates.
Although this seasonal pattern is well known, future power prices and hydrological
conditions remain uncertain. The core operational challenge is therefore to determine
the optimal amount of water to release at each time step to ensure long-term energy
security and short-term profitability. By applying statistical analysis and advanced
modelling frameworks, producers can estimate the water value, which represents the
expected marginal value of having an additional unit of water in the reservoir (A.
Helseth, personal communication, April 2024). Thus, the water value reflects the
opportunity cost of using one additional unit of water for production rather than
storing it for future periods.

3.2.2 Planning horizons

Hydropower planning is relevant to consider at an operational, tactical, and stra-
tegic level. Depending on the planning horizon, each level has its own framework,
where short-term models deal with the operational concerns, mid-term models are
assigned to a more tactical level, and long-term models are dedicated to strategic con-
siderations. Altogether, these models are denoted as Long-, Mid-, and Short-Term
Hydro Scheduling optimization models (LMSTHS). It should be noted that although
some long-term models may incorporate price-making behaviour, the price-taker as-

Project Report  TIØ4500

sumption is more common across several planning horizons. Under this assumption,
electricity prices are treated as exogenous inputs to the model rather than being
influenced by the producer's decisions.

Ahlfors and Amelin (2021) describe the di"erent planning horizons and find that
the literature is not unanimous in its definitions of these horizons in terms of time.
However, the article describes the overarching characteristic of each planning horizon,
and a visual representation of the di"erent levels of planning horizons is depicted in
Figure 10.

Long-term planning
Hydropower planning is inherently stochastic due to uncertainty in future reservoir
inflow, which is particularly important to account for in long-term models (Larsen
et al., 2015). Many large reservoirs require several years to refill once they have been
drawn down, meaning that short-sighted operations can jeopardize future energy
security. Consequently, long-term planning and the establishment of water values
are essential to ensure that current operational decisions reflect both present market
conditions and the long-term availability of water resources.

According to Thaeer Hammid et al. (2020), long-term planning typically covers
horizons of one to five years. Its primary purpose is to secure energy availability,
and its outputs commonly include water values or weekly discharge plans, which
often serve as direct inputs to mid-term models. Because hydropower scheduling
involves substantial uncertainty, long-term planning is frequently based on Stochastic
Dynamic Programming (SDP) or Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP)

                                                    22
Project Report      TIØ4500

(Warland & Mo, 2016).

Although ensuring energy availability is a central motivation for long-term planning,
the mathematical objective of such models can vary from profit to social welfare
maximization. Whether both revenues and costs are incorporated in this objective
may also vary. When revenues are excluded, long-term models fail to directly capture
the potential upside associated with participation in di"erent electricity markets.

Long-term models usually represent hydropower systems at aggregated levels to re-
duce computational complexity, but this also introduces simplifications that may
lead to non-optimal operational decisions (Warland & Mo, 2016). Common simpli-
fications include neglecting detailed hydraulic characteristics or using coarse time
resolutions (Ahlfors & Amelin, 2021). In Nordic studies, weekly time steps are com-
mon, as illustrated by Helseth et al. (2022), whereas monthly time steps have been
observed in a Brazilian system study (Scarcelli et al., 2017). To mitigate the limita-
tions introduced by aggregation, many frameworks employ disaggregation procedures
to validate or refine long-term schedules. These procedures help address non-linear
and state-dependent constraints that would otherwise need to be simplified or omit-
ted due to problem size and computational burden (Warland & Mo, 2016).

Mid-term planning
This planning horizon typically covers horizons from several weeks to a few months.
Compared to long-term planning, the shorter time horizon allows hydropower sys-
tems to be represented with a lower degree of aggregation, enabling a more detailed
description of hydraulic connections, reservoir dynamics, and operational constraints.
However, mid-term planning still involves substantial uncertainty, particularly re-
lated to inflow variability and future electricity prices, and therefore relies on many
of the same modelling techniques as long-term scheduling. In this context, recent
research highlights the role of weekly planning as a promising extension which can
o"er valuable insights for the development of low-carbon power systems (Ahlfors &
Amelin, 2021).

The purpose of mid-term planning is to allocate water resources across seasons in
a way that balances operational profitability and long-term water availability. Typ-
ical criteria include maintaining adequate reservoir levels and deriving more detailed
water values than those produced in long-term models (A. Helseth, personal com-
munication, April 2024). Because the mid-term horizon is closer to real market
operations, market participation becomes a central element of the analysis, includ-
ing participation in day-ahead markets, capacity markets, and financial markets. As

                23
Project Report      TIØ4500

a result, mid-term models are particularly relevant for business case development
and investment assessment.

Short-term planning
Lastly, short-term planning concerns the immediate future. As in mid-term planning,
the input parameters in short-term models depend on the outcomes of the longer
planning horizons. The result is an operational production schedule, typically with
an hourly time resolution, specifying the discharge levels for each hydropower plant.
Because of the short time horizon, uncertainty plays a less prominent role. Therefore,
the modelling focus shifts toward achieving a realistic representation of plant beha-
viour, including e!ciency curves, hydraulic characteristics, and water travel time.
Such detailed modelling has become increasingly important as a growing number of
hydropower producers participate in both energy and capacity markets (Kong et al.,
2020).

Both the objectives and the solution methods vary in short-term hydropower schedul-
ing. Depending on the modelling context, objectives can include revenue maxim-
ization, cost minimization, or minimization of spilled energy (Kong et al., 2020).
Although short-term models are commonly solved deterministically, accounting for
uncertainty can improve their performance (Belsnes et al., 2016). Heuristic methods
are also used in the literature to address the STHS problem, as they are able to
provide good solutions within short computation times (Kong et al., 2020).

3.2.3 Market participation

Reservoir-based hydropower, with its significant storage capacity, is capable of provid-
ing considerable operational flexibility. In addition to the day-ahead (DA) and intra-
day (ID) markets, hydropower producers can participate in several reserve markets,
as highlighted in Section 2. According to the Nordic TSOs (2019), a large share
of Norwegian hydropower capacity qualifies for participation in the FCR market.
Furthermore, because hydropower can deliver the required response times, it is also
eligible for the aFRR and mFRR markets, which have longer activation times com-
pared to the FCR products.

3.2.4 Hydropower investment analysis

Many of the large hydropower plants in Norway were built between the 1950s and
1980s, and many are now approaching or exceeding their technical lifetimes. Be-
cause political regulations limit further development of many of the remaining wa-
tercourses, reinvestments have become the primary strategy for hydropower produ-
cers (WWF Norge, n.d.). This strategy is further reinforced by the growing need for
system flexibility, which increases the importance of upgrading and reconstructing
existing hydropower plants (Statkraft, n.d.). Such reinvestment measures fall into
the two categories, rearmament and expansion (NVE, 2025b).

Rearmament
Rearmament focuses on improving existing parts of the power plant while keeping
the discharge and head at the same level. This can include replacing the impeller,
replacing the generator, or reducing the friction loss in the penstock. These measures
typically fall under the same concession and are therefore exempt from new licensing
procedures (NVE, 2020).

Expansions
Expansion measures can enable higher discharge capacity and/or a greater head.
This may include enlarging the cross-section of the penstock, installing pumped-
hydro storage, which e"ectively reduces the LRWL, or extending tunnels to increase
the available head. "Driva 1 TWh" is a concrete example of a planned capacity
expansion in a Norwegian hydropower plant (TrønderEnergi, 2025). Unlike rearma-
ment, these measures require new licensing procedures (NVE, 2020).

Main cost-drivers
There is limited publicly available information about the costs associated with rearm-
ament and expansion that can be directly applied in an analysis. According to a re-
port published by the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) (2012), the
estimated cost ranges between 500 and 1 000 USD/kW of increased capacity. When
adjusted for inflation and converted using an exchange rate of 0.85 EUR/USD, this
corresponds to approximately 625 000­1 250 000 EUR/MW. This is, however, a very
broad range, as the cost is heavily dependent on which investment is considered.
According to IRENA (2012), the cost structure of a hydropower refurbishment or
expansion project is largely governed by two main drivers: civil works and electro-
mechanical equipment.

· Civil works costs includes all structural and construction-related activities
  such as dam or reservoir construction, tunnel construction, powerhouse modi-

entire planning period and discounted back to the present value. If the net present
value is positive, the project should be considered for implementation.

4.2 Stochaciticy

Capturing the stochasticity associated with hydropower planning is highly relevant
in an operations research setting. While deterministic models often provide useful
insights to decision-making, Belsnes et al. (2016) showcased in their study that
adding stochasticity enhances profit margins by allocating production to expensive
hours and reducing the risk of spillage. This was demonstrated in a case study by
evaluating the strategies obtained from the stochastic and the deterministic models
over a new set of scenarios. Consequently, the stochastic model provided more robust
solution strategies to the decision-maker while improving the degree of realism.

An important element of uncertainty in hydropower planning is inflow. This is fre-
quently recognized in literature review across all types of planning horizons (Abgott-
spon & Andersson, 2013; Belsnes et al., 2016; Helseth et al., 2016, 2017). Inflow to a
given reservoir is determined to a large extent by the temperature conditions in the
area where the hydropower plant is located. Hence, temperature can also be treated
as a stochastic variable in a hydropower planning model, as seen in the SOVN model
(Warland & Mo, 2016). Temperature is also closely linked to electricity consumption
patterns, influencing the demand curve used to determine the market-clearing price
(Idsø et al., 2024). This relationship is particularly relevant in long-term models,
where prices are derived through a market-clearing process, as seen in the EMPS
model (SINTEF, 2025).

Building on this, it provides a rationale for modelling prices as stochastic variables
in short- to mid-term hydropower optimization models. Scha¨"er et al. (2022) imple-
ment stochastic market prices in their study, in addition to a generic stochastic en-
vironmental variable, which implicitly represents temperature by indicating whether
a low inflow period has ended. Aasg°ard (2020) and Faria and Fleten (2011), on the
other hand, do not include any explicit environmental variables and instead focus
on market prices. Various configurations of modelling stochastic market prices have
also been observed in several of the papers mentioned above. Ideally, a model should
take all prices across the existing markets into account, as they are subject to uncer-
tainty (Belsnes et al., 2016). However, treating all prices as independent stochastic
variables would possibly yield the curse of dimensionality when generating scenarios
for the problem.

Hence, the literature appears to converge on the importance of incorporating stochasti-
city into models across di"erent planning horizons, as this improves the realism of the
results. The most prominent stochastic variables to include are prices and inflows.

                28
Project Report  TIØ4500

Uncertain prices are particularly relevant in models where actors operate as price
takers, while uncertain inflow is important to consider irrespective of how the price
is determined. Regardless, researchers often limit the number of stochastic variables
to avoid the curse of dimensionality.

4.3 Hydraulic modelling

Section 3.1.5 showed the non-linear relationship between generated power and net
head. To achieve su!cient precision of a hydropower system, accurate hydraulic
modelling is often essential, as pinpointed in a meeting with Professor Arild Helseth
at the NTNU Department of Electric Energy (A. Helseth, personal commmunica-
tion, 07th of November 2025). When incorporating the HPF into a model, it becomes
crucial to select a method that both captures the underlying physical relationships
and aligns with the problem requirements. This is often a trade-o" between compu-
tational complexity and the desired level of model detail.

Several techniques for modelling the HPF exist, which depend on the time horizon,
temporal resolution, system size, the available data, and the goals of the optimization
model (Kong et al., 2020). The study points out piecewise linear approximation as
a common technique to account for the non-linear HPF. The breakpoints, defined
as pairs of water discharge and power output, are typically specified in advance
and represented either as a two-dimensional table for fixed-head conditions or as a
three-dimensional matrix when head variations are considered (Kong et al., 2020). A
closely related approach described in Chazarra et al. (2016) is discretizing a family
of non-linear curves and using binary auxiliary variables to indicate which section
of the e!ciency curve is activated for a given time step. As such, this approach
can be characterized as a mixed integer linear program (MILP). The drawback of a
MILP is that it often can become computationally burdensome when the size of the
problem increases (Kong et al., 2020). It is important to consider this in relation to
the purpose of the hydropower planning model.

In contrast to the MILP approach, Gjelsvik and Haugstad (2005) and Belsnes et al.
(2016) present a less computationally demanding approach to the HPF modelling.
Gjelsvik and Haugstad (2005) present ProdRisk, a mid-term hydropower schedul-
ing model developed by Sintef Energy Research. It ignores the variable head in the
HPF and introduces correction coe!cients in the form of head sensitivities instead.
This is done through two separate simulations, in which the last simulation aims to
correct the initial simulation through the inclusion of head sensitivities. Similarly,

                                                    29
Project Report  TIØ4500

Belsnes et al. (2016) use an iterative method called successive linear programming,
where the e!ciency related to the net head is updated based on the e!ciency used in
the previous time step. To avoid flip-flopping solutions and ensure convergence, the
e!ciencies are linearized using a first-order approximation of the coupling between
e!ciency and a marginal increase in reservoir level. In addition, a penalty is added to
the objective function for deviating from the previous e!ciencies in the iterative pro-
cess. The study concludes that implementing a head-of-water optimization improves
the model solution, especially when the reservoir is half-full due to its flexibility.

In the continuation of this, it is recognized that modelling hydraulic behavior can
be decisive for model performance, but some approaches might lead to increased
computational complexity. Thus, this trade-o" needs to be tailored to fit the purpose
of the model.

4.4 Multimarket dynamics and participation

Norwegian hydropower producers operate across energy and reserve markets, lever-
aging the storage capability to maximize revenues (SINTEF Energi, 2025). Mod-
elling multiple markets can therefore provide more accurate income estimates and
a more realistic representation of hydropower's true value than single-market ap-
proaches. Consequently, reviewing how the literature addresses multi-market parti-
cipation across modelling frameworks is essential to capturing the full value-creation
potential that this work aims to explore.

It is evident that the literature provides studies with di"erent configurations of mar-
ket participation. Aasg°ard (2020) investigates a multi-market bidding problem con-
sidering the DA-market and mFRR-market using a price-taking approach, without
the inclusion of reserve activation. Her model co-optimizes the revenue gained from
participating in both markets, and a case study from NO2 shows positive gains from
such coordinated bidding. Chazarra et al. (2016) report similar results when model-
ling coordinated bidding in DA-markets and the secondary reserve markets (aFRR)
in a Spanish 7-day case study, as this yields significantly higher income compared
to DA-participation only. Although the number of connected reservoirs and power
plants may vary between the studies, they both show the benefits of multi-market op-
timization. A potential limitation with the latter model, however, is that it assumes
that an expected fixed percentage of the contracted reserves is always activated.
While this exogenous parameter is determined through statistical analysis, this is a
simplified behaviour of the market, potentially yielding too optimistic results. On the

                                                    30
Project Report      TIØ4500

contrary, the model mitigates risk in terms of water shortage and spillage, thereby
reducing the overall risk associated with the strategy suggested by the model.

Building on the modelling of activation presented in Chazarra et al. (2016), Farah-
mand et al. (2020) emphasizes the value of detailed reserve market activation model-
ling, taking the perspective of the system operator. In particular, the study demon-
strates that reserve provision decisions that ignore activation lead to systematic-
ally underestimated costs. To a hydropower producer, this manifests as lost water
value, adjusted reservoir management, slightly reduced future flexibility, and in-
creased ramping. The latter can lead to additional wear and tear on the hydropower
unit.

Similar to several of the above-mentioned studies, Faria and Fleten (2011) also
present a co-optimization model across the DA market and the Elbas market (Intra-
day), from a price-taking perspective. The model captures both block and hourly
bids in a 24-hour schedule in a rolling horizon fashion over multiple consecutive
days. In contrast to Aasg°ard (2020), this model is implemented on a case study
with a hydropower plant located in NO1, and the results indicate that including
ID-participation yields negligible increased profit margins. While these studies seem
to disagree on the benefit of modelling multi-market participation, it is important to
emphasize that they model di"erent markets and bidding zones. Thus, the results
might not be directly comparable, but they highlight interesting aspects of multi-
market interaction.

While the previous studies have considered the value of adding one extra market
into the optimization model, Ahmadi et al. (2015) choose a broader approach to
multi-market participation by including the DA, the bilateral long-term contracts,
and spinning and non-spinning reserve markets. The focus in their study, however, is
multi-objective optimization where minimizing environmental impact from thermal
power plants serves as the second objective. Thus, the study lacks a discussion on
the added value of modelling several power markets. The direction of the balancing
market is also a dimension that is omitted in this study.

Long-term forward contracts and price-making hydropower producers fall outside of
the scope of this project. However, in the literature review, it seems worth men-
tioning the work of Abgottspon and Andersson (2013). They showed that adding
forward contracts as an ingredient in the operation of a price-making hydropower
plant, as well as pumped storage, contributes to a 20% increase in profit. While
the model tends to be realistic and tractable, the results might be coloured by lin-

ear dependencies between commodities and market prices, and the assumption that
forward contract prices are known.

Connecting these articles reveals that the additional economic value of a joint mar-
ket formulation across eligible markets for a hydropower producer is largely absent
from the literature, as also noted by Belsnes (2016). Such a formulation could spe-
cifically integrate the various capacity markets with participation in the DA and ID
markets. It would be particularly interesting to examine whether the synergies from
co-optimization yield more accurate estimates of the potential operational revenues

4.5 Relevant solution methods

Within hydropower planning, a range of solution methods is used to handle short-
and mid-term operational problems, and long-term strategic decision-making. The
choice of method shapes how flexibility, uncertainty, and the value creation of the
hydro resources are represented. Reviewing these methods in the literature is there-
fore important for understanding how di"erent approaches can be utilized, and for
positioning our own work.

SDP/SDDP
A common solution method used across all hydropower planning horizons is Stochastic
(Dual) Dynamic Programming (SDP/SDDP) as seen in various studies (Helseth et
al., 2016, 2017, 2022; P´erez-D´iaz et al., 2020). Helseth et al. (2016) use the SDDP
framework for profit maximization of a hydropower producer selling electricity and
capacity in separate markets. The problem is decomposed into weekly subproblems
for a given sample of scenarios, and solved through a forward iteration where inform-
ation is passed from one state to another. Then, at the end of the planning horizon,
a backward iteration is performed, and cuts are added to the problem based on sens-
itivities from the state variables on the objective function. Similarly, Helseth et al.
(2017), a SDP-model is used to assess the operational profitability of a hydropower
producer participating in the energy and capacity market. The complete planning
period is one year, but the problem is decomposed into weekly MILP problems. The
SDP decomposition allows for the representation of non-linear and non-convex re-
lationships, and for the given week, the realizations of the stochastic variables are
known.

                32
Project Report      TIØ4500

P´eres-Diaz et al. (2020) also use the SDP-framework in their mid-term optimization
model, which has a scheduling horizon of one year. However, they decompose the
problem into daily decision problems as mixed-integer quadratic programming prob-
lems with known exogenous stochastic state variables. These variables were the daily
average energy market price, the daily inflow, and the daily residual demand curve
in the reserve market. While both SDDP and SDP are common solution methods for
stochastic operational decision problems, SDDP might be superior to SDP in terms
of computational cost. The SDDP approach increases linearly with the number of
scenarios, while the SDP increases exponentially.

Two-stage Benders decomposition
A conceptually related solution method to SDP/SDDP is the two-stage Benders De-
composition method, identified in numerous studies (Jacobson et al., 2023; Sifuentes
& Vargas, 2007; Warland & Mo, 2016). Warland and Mo (2016) show that the
Benders decomposition is a suitable solution method to long-term hydro-thermal
planning problems. Using the stochastic optimization model for Scandinavia with
individual water values and grid restrictions, denoted as SOVN-model, a sequence
of problems referred to as scenario fan problems (SFP) is simulated for each time
step in the planning period. Each SFP problem is a two-stage stochastic LP problem
solved by using Benders decomposition. The first stage represents a given week of
the simulation (weather scenario and week), and the second stage is represented by
independent scenarios, where the decisions from the first stage problem are taken
as input and used to solve the rest of the planning period for each scenario. Their
analysis shows promising results, as it yields a better utilization of hydro resources
compared to EMPS. This is also a long-term planning model, but it is based on
aggregated hydro description and heuristics.

In Jacobson et al. (2023), a Benders Decomposition technique for a large-scale energy
system planning problem is presented. The complete problem is formulated as a
MILP model. Further, it is then decomposed into a master problem, which handles
investment decisions, and operational subproblems, handling detailed operational
constraints. A key innovation in this work is the decomposition of the operational
problem by weeks, despite the planning horizon covering the full 52-week year. This
is achieved by introducing budgeting variables that impose weekly limits, thereby
allowing the weekly subproblems to be solved independently and in parallel, Jacobson
et al. (2023).

Sifuentes and Vargas (2007) shows that the Benders decomposition is also applicable
when solving a short-term hydro scheduling problem (STHS) with a full AC power

                33
Project Report      TIØ4500

flow model. The master problem is a mixed-integer programming problem that min-
imizes fixed costs associated with hydropower generation and thermal units. Whereas
in the subproblem, the Optimal Power Flow (OPF) problem is solved. Then, the
Benders cuts are applied to the master problem with respect to the sensitivities of
the dual variables obtained from the OPF solution. These studies show that, irre-
spective of the planning horizon, Benders decomposition could be a relevant solution
method.

Stochastic Successive Linear Programming
Building on the short-term solution methods presented above, Belsnes et al., (2016)
propose a stochastic successive linear programming approach for the STHS prob-
lem, explicitly modelling uncertainty via scenario trees. They highlight a limitation
of scenario-tree-based multistage stochastic programming, namely the decay of un-
certainty as one moves deeper into the tree. The e"ective uncertainty is reduced,
which biases the solution towards determinism. To mitigate this, they discuss the
use of rolling horizon schemes, recalculating scenario trees as new information be-
comes available. They also note that scenario-tree generation and fine temporal
resolution raise computational tractability issues when the horizon is very long, or
the discretisation is fine, akin to the limitations of SDP. However, the SSLP does not
exploit structures in hydropower planning, which avoids the benefit of decomposing
the large hydropower planning problem. Thus, this method is primarily suited for
STHS problems.

Multi Objective Programming
While the presented solution methods thus far have focused on single objective optim-
ization, Ahmadi et al. (2015) choose a di"erent approach to the hydropower schedul-
ing problem. They present a deterministic multi-objective optimization (MOO) ap-
proach that simultaneously considers emission minimization and profit maximization.
In their work, the Normal Boundary Intersection (NBI) method and Weighted-Sum
(WS) method are applied to a MILP problem to generate non-inferior solutions and
construct the Pareto-optimal frontier. Obtaining this frontier provides stakehold-
ers with valuable insights into the trade-o" between profit and emissions. While
considering emissions is beyond the intended scope of our study, MOO represents
an important direction for future research, particularly when integrating sustainable
energy sources such as wind and solar (Ahlfors & Amelin, 2021).

The presented solution methods in this subsection show that there are several ap-
proaches to the hydropower planning problem, depending on the inclusion of stochasti-
city, nonlinearities, multistage, planning horizon, and objectives. SDDP is a prom-

                34
Project Report  TIØ4500

ising solution method if all these characteristics are under consideration, while SDP is
suitable as long as the planning horizon is short enough to avoid intractable scenario
trees. This is also a shortcoming of the SSLP. In contrast, the Benders' decomposi-
tion method avoids the planning horizon issue. Simultaneously, the method discussed
here is limited to two-stage problems. However, this limitation can be addressed by
using a nested Benders approach. Nested Benders essentially extends the classical
Benders decomposition method to a scenario tree structure, where the tree can be
viewed as a set of nested two-stage problems (Murphy, 2013). If several conflicting
objectives are under consideration, MOO techniques such as the NBI and WS can
be used to construct Pareto frontiers. In total, these methods provide guidance in
how various problems can be solved in a holistic and more realistic manner.

5 Problem description

This section presents a detailed description of the multi-market participation problem
for a single hydro power plant, with emphasis on key limitations and decision-making
processes involved. Assumptions that have been made when developing the model
and their implications for the results are highlighted in Table 5.

The operator of a reservoir-based hydropower plant must coordinate production de-
cisions across operational, tactical, and strategic horizons. The present discussion
focuses on the mid-term operational setting, in which the plant is scheduled on an
hourly basis across a full year. In this context, the operator receives inputs in the
form of expected water inflow to the reservoir and market prices in the day-ahead
(DA), intraday (ID), and capacity markets (aFRR and FCR). These price signals
and hydrological conditions evolve over the planning horizon and constitute the in-
formational basis for subsequent operational decisions.

In reality, prices and inflows are uncertain. However, assuming a fairly certain estim-
ate exists, the operator must determine the hourly allocation of energy and capacity
across the available electricity markets. This entails selecting the production level
from the reservoir and distributing available power capacity between energy and
capacity markets. The central decision variables, therefore, include the amount of
energy produced and sold in the DA and ID markets, as well as the upward and
downward committed capacities in the aFRR and FCR markets. It is assumed that
the operator can submit bids for every hour simultaneously and does not have to
consider the chronological sequence of the market participation as described in Sec-
tion 2.

These decisions are governed by a set of physical, hydrological, and market-related
constraints. First, generation is bounded between minimum and maximum plant ca-
pacity, and the e!ciency of the turbine depends on the level of production. Second,
the reservoir state must remain within prescribed upper and lower limits for oper-
ational, environmental, and safety reasons. Water balance dynamics link decisions
intertemporally. While water release for electricity generation reduces the reservoir
level, inflow replenishes it, meaning that the level at any hour directly influences
the feasible operating region in subsequent hours. Third, participation in capacity
markets is contingent upon the scheduled production level. The ability to provide
upward reserve capacity is constrained by the margin between the plant's current pro-
duction and its maximum generation capacity. On the contrary, downward reserve
capacity is bounded by the di"erence between current production and the minimum

                                                    38
Project Report      TIØ4500

stable operating level. In both cases, reserve capacity may be activated. While the
activation dynamics are kept outside the model, the possibility of activation can be
reflected by introducing additional margins to the minimum and maximum stable
operating levels of the reservoir to ensure su!cient energy security. Additionally, the
size of the plant limits the share of the reserve market that the plant can capture.
Lastly, as the plant operates as a price taker, there is a tradeable volume limitation
in the ID market based on a set percentage.

Within this framework, the operator's objective is to maximize total revenue over
the planning period by optimally allocating energy and reserve capacity across the
markets while respecting physical and regulatory constraints. The result identifies
the optimal production schedule and market commitments for each hour. This spe-
cifies the reservoir state, discharge, and energy and capacity allocations that jointly
represent the economically best operating strategy under the given conditions.

8 Validation

The purpose of the validation is to assess whether the model's decisions align with its
stated objectives and constraints. This preliminary step strengthens the credibility
of the subsequent computational study.

For the validation, we restrict the planning horizon to two weeks in January. This
shorter horizon allows for a clearer visual interpretation of the parameters and vari-
ables in the optimal solution. However, it also requires minor adjustments to the
model. In the full-year simulation, the end-of-horizon reservoir level is required
to match the initial level. This constraint is feasible for the annual model while
still satisfying the minimum production requirement. In contrast, applying the
same constraint to the January subset results in infeasibility. Intuitively, this is
expected, as inflow levels in the beginning of January, previously illustrated in Fig-
ure 13. Therefore, for the validation exercise, the end-reservoir constraint is relaxed
to ST = 0.75 · S0, which is close to the highest feasible terminal storage level. Nat-
urally, the chosen end-reservoir level influences the resulting production plan.

The validation consists of three steps. First, we assess whether the water value, rep-
resented by the dual value of the reservoir balance constraint, is reasonable. Second,
we analyse the model's behaviour in the energy markets, comparing its decisions
to corresponding price patterns. Finally, we examine whether the energy-market
decisions appear consistent with those made in the capacity markets.

8.1 Water value

As discussed in Section 1, the water value represents the marginal income associated
with storing one additional unit of water in the reservoir at the beginning of the
planning horizon. In a deterministic model, this value reflects perfect foresight re-
garding how the additional unit can be used optimally. Running the model over the
two-week validation horizon yields a constant water value of 127.271 EUR/MWh, as
shown in Figure 16.

9.1.1 Connecting water values and the reservoir level

Before proceeding with the computational study, the base instance is used to il-

level.

Figure 20: Water value and reservoir level for the full-year base instance.

Examining the water value in Figure 20 and comparing it with Figure 16, it can be
observed that the former exhibits several discontinuities, rather than a single con-
stant value as in the latter. When considering these discontinuities in the context
of Figure 20, it becomes apparent that they occur whenever the reservoir is fully
depleted. Because the model has perfect foresight regarding prices and inflows, the
production schedule is optimized to maximize certain revenues. Consequently, the
reservoir is emptied whenever this constitutes the optimal strategy. Between such
depletion events, there is a marginal hour in which allocating one additional unit of
water to the market would be optimal. As the model is fully deterministic, it is there-
fore reasonable that the water value evolves as a strictly decreasing function. Each
depletion point represents a change in the opportunity cost of stored water, which
induces a new marginal valuation segment. In the remainder of the computational
study, this insight can be recalled whenever water values are presented.

next hour t+1. From Figure 22, it can be observed that the base instance exhibits

the energy markets. When capacity markets are introduced, the overall magnitude

increases. Since lower average production levels enable greater bidirectional flexibil-

9.4 Modelling revenues with capacity expansions

The computational study thus far has demonstrated that participation in multiple
markets alters the operational pattern and generates additional value for the hydro-
power producer. A central strategic question is whether this additional value can
justify investments in expanding production capacity. Consequently, the remainder
of the computational study examines how di"erent levels of capacity expansion in-
fluence the expected cash flows associated with multi-market participation. In the
model, this is implemented by adjusting the maximum generation capacity and the
maximum capacity that can be o"ered in the respective capacity markets.

                              69
Project Report  TIØ4500

Figure 27: Revenues obtained from base case and the various capacity expansion
cases.

From Figure 27, it can be observed that the revenue e"ect of an additional x MW
of capacity expansion exhibits a weakly concave pattern. This suggests that the
marginal benefit of additional capacity upgrades declines as the installed capacity
increases. With a given capacity upgrade, the maximum level of market participation
in the reserve markets scales proportionally with the increase in installed capacity.
However, as the volume limits in the intraday market are fixed, this market does
not provide an income source that can be proportionally leveraged. The diminishing
revenues are in accordance with diminishing marginal utility presented in economic
theory (Lin & Peng, 2019). Moreover, the figure indicates that the underlying revenue
composition remains largely unchanged across the scenarios. Since the power plant
acts as a price taker, the additional capacity primarily enhances its ability to exploit
high-price hours across the various markets.

10 Concluding remarks

This project has examined electricity markets, recent price developments, and key
modelling considerations relevant for hydropower systems. A central finding from
the literature review is that existing studies largely lack analyses of multi-market
participation within a mid-term planning context. To address this gap, the project
has focused on a deterministic mid-term hydropower planning problem that expli-
citly allows participation across multiple energy and capacity markets. The purpose
has been to analyse how the model allocates capacity across markets, how this al-
location influences the operational behaviour of a hydropower plant, the economic
value generated by such multi-market participation, and how these e"ects should be
interpreted in relation to investment decisions in increased capacity.

Operationally, the results indicate that participation in capacity markets leads to a
lower utilisation of turbine capacity, as large parts of the available capacity is reserved
for reserve market participation. At the same time, the model exhibits a higher num-
ber of ramping hours, although with smaller amplitude variations. These findings
imply that the hydropower plant engaged in multi-market participation tend to oper-
ate less aggressively in a market situation without considering activation. This can,
for several hours, reflect a strategic positioning for both upward and downward reg-
ulation. Moreover, as the plant undergoes fewer transitions between near-minimum
and near-maximum production levels, the mechanical wear is expected to be reduced.

Economically, the results show that participation in capacity markets can lead to
fewer reservoir drawdowns and that the model places a relatively high value on down-
regulation, as this may generate double revenue streams. At the same time, there
is a volume bias towards up-regulation. While energy markets remain the dominant
source of revenue, the tradable volumes and price patterns in aFRR favour participa-
tion in this market rather than FCR-N, despite FCR-N exhibiting the highest average
prices. These findings highlight the key drivers influencing the revenue potential of
capacity market participation. Further, it illustrates how the reservoir constraints
may favour up-regulation, while the input data incentives down-regulation as en-
ergy and down-regulating prices exceeding up-regulation prices alone. This reveals
important trade-o"s inherent in multi-market optimisation.

From the investment perspective, the analysis indicates diminishing marginal bene-
fits from increasing installed capacity. Nevertheless, the additional value generated
through capacity market participation can justify costly investments in increased
capacity, also under higher discount rates. The implications of these results sug-

                                                    73
Project Report      TIØ4500

gest that capacity upgrade investments can be economically viable, but uncertain
future prices and investment costs necessitate conservative assumptions to support
well-informed and robust investment decisions.

Overall, this work provides insights into both the technical and economic character-
istics of electricity markets, as well as key modelling considerations relevant to hy-
dropower applications. The findings establish a strong foundation for further model
development, with the potential to support more robust decision-making for hydro-
power producers. Future work can include more detailed representations of stepwise
dynamics of the power markets, explicit modelling of reserve activation, incorpora-
tion of uncertainty, refined cost modelling, and the application of advanced solution
methodologies as the problem formulation increases in complexity.

11 Future Research

There are several promising directions for future research, such as model extensions
and the application of more advanced solution techniques as the problem becomes
increasingly complex.

Possible model expansions

Stochasticity
A natural first step would be to incorporate stochasticity in energy and capacity
market prices. Assuming perfect foresight of future market conditions is optimistic
and can lead the model to operate the hydropower plant closer to its physical and
operational limits. Additionally, having perfect knowledge of future inflows makes
the model unrealistically confident in its water management decisions. Lastly, traded
volumes in the ID market can also be regarded as stochastic. Given that the model
limits trading to the realised hourly traded volumes, this constraint may also be
treated as a stochastic parameter.

Building on the lack of modelling uncertainty, the model currently exploits arbit-
rage opportunities arising from di"erences between DA and ID prices. With greater
uncertainty in ID and DA prices, the model would likely refrain from selling large
quantities of energy in the DA market and subsequently buying them back in the
ID market, as this strategy would become riskier under uncertain price conditions.
Changes in the water values are also expected to change relative to the baseline
water value trajectory, which exhibits a stepwise pattern over the planning horizon.
Finally, reservoir levels would likely appear more stable, as the model would be less
inclined to operate the power plant aggressively early in the planning horizon to
maximize revenues at specific high-price hours.

When incorporating stochasticity, modelling the decision hierarchy becomes a natural
point of focus. The timing of information availability and bid submission deadlines
provides the basis for constructing multi-stage formulations. Under such a frame-
work, the problem could be further developed, allowing the stepwise requirements of
the power market to be represented more realistically. To the best of the author's
knowledge, such an extension would provide new and valuable insights, particularly
in a multi-market context.

Cost and degradation
Another interesting aspect concerns the influence of generator start-up and shut-
down costs, detailed O&M expenses, taxes, and degradation e"ects. Since the current
model only accounts for operational revenues, incorporating these components into
the optimization model would possibly lead to a more realistic representation of
actual operational decisions. However, the inclusion of start-up/shut-down costs is
likely to increase computational complexity. Introducing start-up and shut-down
costs could necessitate the use of binary variables to indicate whether the generator
is started or shut down in each hour, thereby adding complexity to the optimization
problem, which must be represented over the entire planning period of one year.

In contrast, taxes, O&M costs and degradation e"ects could be incorporated without
necessarily increasing computational complexity, although they would require more
detailed data to produce a realistic estimate of profitability. Additionally, it is worth
emphasizing that these components, particularly taxes, are important considerations
for power producers and may enhance the study's relevance to the industry.

Reserve activation
Regarding more detailed modelling of the reserve markets, there also exists a clear
potential to incorporate reserve activation in a manner that is both suitable and
computationally e!cient. The approach proposed by Chazarra et al. (2016) can be
regarded as a simpler, yet suitable modelling alternative.

Transition to 15-minute time resolution
Lastly, another potential direction for future research is to move from an hourly to a
15-minute time resolution. However, the limited availability of high-resolution data
may pose a challenge. Moreover, such a refinement would increase computational
complexity, as the planning horizon would e"ectively quadruple and each decision
variable would need to be represented at the quarter-hour level throughout the entire
planning period.

Possible solution methods

As the model becomes more complex, it is relevant to adapt solution techniques to
ensure that the problem can still be solved within a reasonable time. Based on the
literature review, there appears to be potential for incorporating a stochastic dy-
namic programming framework into the presented optimization problem. This may
be achieved by decomposing the problem formulation into subproblems, where the

                                                    76
Project Report      TIØ4500

stochastic parameters are treated deterministically within each respective timeframe.

Helseth et al. (2017) provide valuable insights into this modelling framework and can
serve as inspiration for future work. Incorporating this type of framework alongside
a more detailed representation of the various reserve markets would, to the best of
the authors' knowledge, o"er new and valuable insights for hydropower producers.
Consequently, the study is relevant to the industry at a time when capacity upgrades
for participation across markets are an increasingly important topic.
