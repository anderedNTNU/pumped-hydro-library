Abstract

While the rapid increase in the adoption of Electric Vehicles (EVs) brings forth many
environmental and economic benefits, it also presents new challenges for the power systems. To
make the EVs a part of the solution and not a part of the problem, a flexible EV parking lot is
investigated. The operation of the EV parking lot is optimized to reduce the cost of operation
and peak consumption for the connected office building, while also offering grid-services
through an incentive-based demand response program (IBDRP). Data from an existing office
building and EV parking lot is used together with day-ahead spot price data.

To handle the short-term planning in the operation of the office building and connected EV
parking lot, a Receding Horizon (RH) framework is implemented. The RH framework can
handle the IBDRP activation signals which are given with varying notice time before the the
parking lot shuts off the charging. The decomposition technique Stochastic Dual Dynamic
Programming (SDDP) is used to provide future cost curves to the RH planning. The future
cost curves are implemented to strategically account for the long-term implications of raising
the monthly peak demand due to the demand charge cost. The combined model is tested on
varying notice times, varying degrees of EV charging reduction in the IBDRP activation, and
an implementation of Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) technology which can provide power back to the
office building and/or power grid.

The model performance is evaluated under various notice times (0, 2, and 12 hours). With a
12 hour notice, the system effectively manages the unexpected signals with only a 0.57% cost
increase compared to the Base Case. However, it struggles with shorter notice times. The 2
hour notice time leads to a 35% peak cost increase, and the 0 hour notice time results in only
49% of throttled energy being reallocated. Implementing V2G functionality further constrains
the system, with the 12 hour notice time being the only successful scenario, showing a 3.2%
total cost increase at maximum power delivery to the grid. Additionally, an existing IBDRP
offering economic incentives through demand charge cost reduction is tested. The 12 hour
notice time scenario achieves a 1.8% reduction in operational costs, while the shorter notice
times fail to meet charging demands.

The main findings indicate that an IBDRP can be of great value to the office building owner
and the DSO. With sufficient notice time, the office building can meet the DSOs throttling
demand, and potentially reduce operational costs while also covering the EV owners charging
needs.

Sammendrag

Selv om den raske økningen i bruken av elektriske biler (EVs) gir mange miljømessige og
økonomiske fordeler, medfører det også nye utfordringer for kraftsystemet. For å gjøre EVs til
en del av løsningen og ikke en del av problemet, undersøkes en fleksibel EV-parkeringsplass.
Driften av EV parkeringsplassen optimaliseres for å redusere driftskostnadene og toppforbruket
til det tilkoblede kontorbygget, samtidig som det tilbys nettjenester gjennom et incentivbasert
etterspørsels-responsprogram (IBDRP). Data fra et eksisterende kontorbygg og
EV-parkeringsplass brukes sammen med prisdata fra day-ahead markedet.

For å håndtere kortsiktig planlegging i driften av kontorbygget og den tilkoblede
EV-parkeringsplassen, implementeres et Receding Horizon (RH)-rammeverk. RH-rammeverket
kan håndtere IBDRP-aktiveringssignaler som er gitt med variende varslingstider før
parkeringsplassen slår av ladingen. Dekomponeringsmetoden Stochastic Dual Dynamic
Programming (SDDP) brukes for å gi fremtidige kostnadskurver til RH-planleggingen. De
fremtidige kostnadskurvene implementeres for å strategisk hensynta de langsiktige
konsekvensene av å øke månedlig toppeffekt på grunn av effekttariffen. Den kombinerte
modellen testes for varierende varslingstider, varierende grader av EV-struping ved
IBDRP-aktivering, og en implementering av Vehicle-to-grid-teknologi (V2G) som kan levere
strøm tilbake til kontorbygget og/eller nettet.

Modellen evalueres ved bruk av ulike varslingstider (0, 2 og 12 timer). Med 12 timers varsling
håndterer systemet effektivt de uventede signalene med en 0,57% kostnadsøkning
sammenlignet med modellen uten struping. Derimot så sliter modellen med de kortere
varslingstidene. 2 timers varsling fører til en 35% økning i effekt-tariffkostnader, og 0 timers
varsling resulterer i at bare 49% av den reduserte energien blir omfordelt. Implementering av
V2G-funksjonalitet begrenser systemet ytterligere, med 12 timers varsling som det eneste
vellykkede scenariet, som viser til en 3,2% total kostnadsøkning ved maksimal effektlevering til
nettet. I tillegg testes et eksisterende IBDRP som tilbyr økonomiske incentiver gjennom
reduksjon av effekt-tariffkostnadene. 12 timers varsling gir en 1,8% reduksjon i driftskostnader,
mens kortere varslingstider ikke klarer å møte ladebehovene.

Hovedfunnene indikerer at et IBDRP kan være av stor verdi for eieren av kontorbygningen
samt for DSOen. Med tilstrekkelig varslingsperiode kan kontorbygningen møte DSOens krav
om reduksjon av forbruk, og potensielt redusere driftskostnadene samtidig som de dekker
behovene for EV lading.

Introduction

1.1 Motivation

The global transition towards sustainable energy and transportation has seen a rapid increase
in the adoption of Electric Vehicles (EV). While this shift brings forth many environmental
and economic benefits, it also presents new challenges for the power systems. As EVs have a
high energy consumption when charging, several studies have predicted the consequences of
the rapid integration of EVs. One of these studies [2] analyses how the increase in EVs has
strained local distributional grids in Norway. The study found that EVs have increased the
total energy electricity use in Norway by 0.42% in 2020. Statnett also states that the electric
energy use for transportation will be 20 TWh by 2030 in Norway [3]. From this, it is evident
that the transportation sector is going to make a substantial impact on the grid.

One of the main challenges of the rapid increase of EVs is the increased peak loads in the grid.
A solution to mitigate peak consumption for the Distribution System Operators (DSOs) is the
integration of capacity-based grid tariffs. For instance, the monthly demand charge puts a cost
on on the highest peak single-hour import in the month, which has been present at commercial
user-level before, but not at household-level [4]. From this, the EV charging operators are
motivated to optimize their energy use over a monthly horizon in combination with the
day-to-day operation.

Office buildings and other commercial premises experience changes in their demand profiles
due to the integration of EV charging facilities, affecting peak import accordingly [5]. DSOs
can use Incentive-Based Demand Response Programs (IBDRP) as an incentive to reduce grid
problems. An example is Glitre Nett which offers a Demand Charge Cost reduction (DCCR)
as an incentive [6]. This reduction is based on the notice time before activation, where lower
notice times will result in a higher reduction. By strategically utilizing the EV Parking Lot
(EVPL) as a flexible resource, grid services can be provided to the DSOs as demand response
(DR) to reduce operational costs for service provider.

                                                             1
1.2 Objective/Contributions

This thesis aims to evaluate the costs and potential benefits of a flexible EVPL connected to
an office building participating in an IBDRP. These programs offer incentives for modifying
consumption patterns. This study focuses on optimizing EV charging and minimizing the
associated costs, and examines how participation in the IBDRP affects operational
performance and costs. Additionally, the thesis explores Vehicle-to-Grid (V2G) technology to
analyze how the technology affects operational performance, with and without
IBDR-participation. The IBDRP allows participation with varying notice times before
activation, impacting operational decisions due to the uncertain activation notification. To the
author’s knowledge, limited work has looked into IBDR programs with different notice times.
In this thesis this is addressed by integrating notice time into scheduling decisions within the
Receding Horizon (RH)-framework to react to the flexibility needs.

The main contributions of this work are the following:

    • A RH-framework is developed to operate an office building with a flexible EVPL, subject
       to hourly spot prices and monthly demand charge.

    • An SDDP-framework is applied to account for long-term impacts of raising the peak due
       to the monthly capacity-based Demand Charge Cost (DCC)

    • The RH-framework is investigated for IBDR-participation, where load reduction is
       requested by the local DSO. With a variation in notice times before activation and
       different quantities of throttled power, the EVPL scheduling is analyzed to provide
       insights on consequence of IBDR for the office building.

    • This work investigates the operational impact with V2G available, where the EVPL
       scheduling is analyzed to provide insights on the consequence of a throttling activation
       which also requires supplying energy back to the grid.

    • Analyze the consequence of an IBDRP when economic incentives are provided which
       reduces the DCC based on the notice time of the IBDR-signal.

1.2.1 Limitations

The model does not consider any dynamic limitations in power flow that might occur in a
real-life system. No investment costs are considered, only the operational costs associated with
energy consumption. The modeling of the EVPL is based on historical data on an aggregated
level, which limits the data available on the individual EVs.

2.1 Electricity grid

An electricity grid is defined as a network where electricity is delivered from producers to
consumers. The main elements in an electricity grid are power stations, electrical substations,
transformers, transmission systems, and distribution systems [8]. In this section, the
transmission- and distribution system is further explained.

2.1.1 Transmission System

The Transmission System Operator (TSO) is the administrative body that is responsible for
building, managing, and maintaining the transmission grid [9]. The transmission grid connects
producers with consumers in a nationwide system together with interconnections between
other countries [10]. The transmission grid carries a high voltage, usually 300 to 420 kV, with
certain parts of Norway having lines carrying 132 kV. Due to the cost of establishing and
maintaining such a large infrastructure, a TSO is often a monopoly and therefore subject to
strict regulations. In day-to-day operations, a TSO is responsible for maintaining a continuous

                                                             4
balance between electricity supply from power stations and the demand from consumers, and
also ensuring the provision of reserves that will allow for sudden contingencies [11]. The only
TSO in Norway is Statnett, which is a state-owned company under the oil- and energy
department.

2.1.2 Distribution System

The Distribution System Operator (DSO) is a key entity in the energy sector responsible for
managing and operating the distribution system, which delivers electricity from the
high-voltage transmission system to end-users. DSOs are responsible for the real-time
operation of the distribution grid, ensuring the balance between electricity supply and demand.
DSOs perform maintenance on the grid, including power lines, transformers, and substations.
The service provided ensures a reliable power supply to end-users, by working to make the grid
resilient against disruptions. In Norway, the distribution grid is divided into two levels [10]:
The regional distribution grid, serves as the intermediary, connecting the transmission grid
to the distribution grid. This grid can also include higher voltage production and consumption
radials. The regional typically operates at a voltage of 33 to 132 kV. The local distribution
grid supplied electricity to smaller end-users. This grid operates at voltages up to 22 kV and is
split into high-voltage and low-voltage segments. The threshold between these segments is 1
kV, with the low voltage distribution to standard consumers normally carrying 400 V or 230 V.

2.1.3 Challenges in the Norwegian Power System

Increased renewable power production and the electrification of the transport sector are
necessary to meet global emission goals but also bring forth many challenges for the grid
operators. New larger renewable power production such as solar and wind is connected to the
grid through converters [12]. This can worsen the grid stability, especially in Norway where the
grid is built around hydropower-generation which is connected to the grid through synchronous
generators. The synchronous generators are synchronized to the grid frequency while
strengthening the frequency stability of the system. There is also an increase in distributed
energy production, mostly through solar, which can cause local grid-stability issues [13]. The
distributed energy production is more often located in weaker parts of the grid further out in
the system, which is more prone to grid issues. Problems which were pointed out due to local
solar production are voltage quality issues and thermal loads in cables and transformers.

The TSO in Norway, Statnett, is already experiencing situations where they do not have
enough grid-stability reserves [12]. Grid-stability reserves are flexibility services that are
provided and activated when there is a need to stabilize the grid or frequency. With increased
variable renewable energy production, the need for reserves is increasing. Not having enough
reserves can lead to market constraints, and in worst-case a forced disconnection of consumers
or producers. The increasing need for reserves leads to economic possibilities for both existing
and new market operators. Statnett states that consumption and energy-storage has to

contribute more than what we are used to. This encompasses flexible use of electricity for
heating in buildings and industrial applications, charging of vehicles and the production of green
hydrogen [12]".

2.2 The Norwegian Pricing Model

2.2.1 The power market

In Norway, all power trading is done through the European power exchange Nord Pool [14].
Nord Pool was founded in 1993 as Statnett Marked AS, as the Norwegian government
deregulated the market for power trading. Throughout the years, Nord Pool has expanded
further out in Europe, and now provides services in most of northern Europe. Nord Pool is an
exchange built up from two main markets, namely the financial market and the physical
market. Based on the scope of this thesis, only the physical is further discussed.
As presented in Figure 2.2.1, the physical market includes the day-ahead and intra-day market
[14], while the TSO runs the Capacity market [15].

                                  Figure 2.2.1: The Nord Pool Market [16]

2.2.2 The Day-Ahead Market

In the day-ahead market customers can sell or buy energy for the next 24 hours in a closed
auction. Orders are matched to maximize social welfare while taking network constraints
provided by transmission system operators into consideration [17]. This ensures that the power
system is balanced before delivery [18]. Available capacities on interconnectors and in the grid
are published at 10:00 CET (Central European Time). After this, the buyers and sellers have
until 12:00 CET to submit their final bids to Nord Pool auction for delivery hours the next
day. The market results are typically published before 13:00 CET and are referred to as spot
prices [17]. Between the clearing of the spot price market and the delivery hour, the market
participants can adjust their market position in the intra-day market [18].

                                                             6
2.2.3 Grid tariff

To cover the costs of grid operation, DSOs typically implement a grid tariff, which requires
end-users to pay for the transportation of energy. The grid tariff usually consists of a
volumetric cost based on the energy consumption, a fixed cost, and on commercial buildings
there is a capacity-based cost which in this thesis is called the demand charge. Residential
customers were introduced to the capacity-based grid tariff in 2022. The rates on the grid
tariff and the contents are set by the respective DSOs. The Norwegian Energy Regulatory
Authority (RME) overlooks the income from the grid tariffs and checks that the DSOs income
does not surpass the regulated amount [4].

The grid tariff can differ between commercial customers and residential customers, as
presented by the DSO Lede in [19]. Commercial customers with a yearly consumption of less
than 100,000 kWh are usually on similar terms as residential customers. This includes a
monthly demand charge based on the average of the three peak hours in a month. A monthly
fixed cost is included which covers a fee to the energy fund, also called the ENOVA-fee. The
electricity fee is included on all power consumption, which is a volumetric cost that differs
between the summer and winter months. VAT on electricity is set at 25%.

For larger commercial customers, the grid tariff is structured similarly to the smaller
commercial customers, with a fixed cost, an electricity fee and VAT. The difference is that the
DCC usually only considers the single highest peak hour of the month, instead of averaging the
three highest hours. The demand charge can also be seasonal, being higher in the months
when the grid is more constrained. Lastly, a seasonal volumetric charge on reactive power
consumption can be included, usually in NOK/kVAR/month.

2.3 Demand Side Management

In this master’s thesis, the definition of DSM by Peter Warren is used; Demand-side
management (DSM) refers to technologies, actions and programmes on the demand-side of
energy metres that seek to manage or decrease energy consumption, in order to reduce total
energy system expenditures or contribute to the achievement of policy objectives such as
emissions reduction or balancing supply and demand. [20]

DSM is a method that enables the energy sector to influence and manage electricity
consumption patterns on the consumer side of the market. This is to encourage consumers to
reduce their demand during peak hours or shift their energy use to off-peak periods. Three of
the most common ways to for consumers to purposely alter their demand patterns are through
peak clipping, valley filling, and load shifting [21]. In Figure 2.3.1, different DSM techniques
are presented and visualized.

2.3.1 Demand response

A common initiative of DSM is Demand Response (DR). DR can be defined as the changes in
electricity usage by end-use customers from their normal consumption patterns in response to
changes in the price of electricity over time or grid reliability concerns [22] [23]. Demand
Response Programs (DRP) can be used to reduce energy costs, reduce the variation in
electricity prices, and make a more reliable power system. The DRPs performance can be
measured by peak load reduction and demand elasticity [24]. It can be particularly challenging
to create demand charge programs which fairly reflects the costs to the DSOs [25]. In Norway,
a DRP was implemented for all household end-users in 2022 when a demand charge was
implemented in the grid tariff, with the intention of making the customers aware of their peak
power usage [26].

DR programs are particularly relevant in the context of EVPL due to the amount of electricity
that can be consumed when multiple EVs are charging simultaneously. In this research, the
two most common categories of DRPs are discussed, namely price- and incentive-based
demand response.

2.3.1.1 Price-based demand response

Price-Based Demand Response (PBDR) is a strategy that encourages consumers to adjust
their electricity consumption patterns in response to price signals [27]. The purpose is to signal

                                                             8
the customers to change their consumption to better reflect the grid situation. The customers
are "punished" with higher electricity costs if the signal is ignored. A common way of
introducing PBDR is by providing accurate spot price data. Before the introduction of smart
meters in Norway, the spot price data was given as a monthly average. By 2019, all Norwegian
homes had a digital smart-meter installed [28]. The recent introduction of smart meters in
Norwegian households has made hourly power consumption data available for power and grid
companies, and spot price data is now provided in an hourly resolution. Hourly spot prices
make it possible for the end-users to adjust their demand more accurately to save costs and
help reduce peaks in the grid.

Another well-integrated PBDR program is the demand charge, which was already mentioned
in Section 2.2.3 as being a part of the Grid Tariff. Demand charge uses the consumers peak
power to charge the consumers with the intention of reducing power peak [29]. The demand
charge forces the short-term planning of electricity consumption to consider the monthly
implications of raising the peak, complicating the optimization of energy use. There is varying
practice when determining the basis for the demand charge. While some use the customer’s
maximum power consumption per month, others base it on the average power consumption
from multiple measurements over the same period. However, both approaches are considered
to be within the current regulatory framework [4].

2.3.1.2 Incentive-based demand response

Incentive-based Demand Response (IBDR) provides fixed or time-varying incentives to
participating customers for reducing their electricity usage [30]. IBDR works by sending signals
to the end-users, notifying them to reduce their electricity consumption in periods when
electricity demand is high or when there is a need to stabilize the grid. This might involve
adjusting the heating, delaying the use of certain appliances, or adjusting the EV charging.
IBDR being a voluntary agreement, consumer engagement and willingness to participate are
important factors for IBDR being a successful strategy.

IBDRPs have been utilized throughout energy grids which need higher reliability in peak
demand hours. A example is Tibbers smart charging [31], where the customers EV is
automatically charged when the electricity price is the lowest. Here, a 20% lower charging cost
is guaranteed, where Tibber in return can use the EV charging to help balance the grid. In
California, a demand response auction mechanic was introduced to the energy market in 2014,
which is a pay-as-bid pilot in which investors-owned utilities seek monthly demand response
system capacity [32]. Further, in Norway, a Disconnectable Tariff has been offered by grid
companies for some time. An example is Glitre Nett and their electricity tariff for large
businesses and commercial customers with adequate power consumption [33]. Here a
percentage-wise reduction in the DCC is offered for consumers, in which the size of the
reduction is based on how preemptive the signal for the request must be sent.

2.4 Electric Vehicle

There has been a rapid increase in the number of EVs in Norway in recent years. The
percentage of EVs in the Norwegian car park was 24% in July 2023 [34]. Further, globally
Norway has the most EVs per capita and the most public chargers per EV. In 2022, 58%
reported that they used a car to travel to and from work as the driver, and 5% reported that
they used a car to travel to work as a passenger [35]. With this in mind, the EVs spend a
significant amount of time parked at the office parking lot. All new buildings must facilitate
EV charging after a legal regulation in 2021 [36]. As EV charging can strain the local grids,
further explored in Section 3.1.2, having intelligent charging such as smart charging to
distribute the loads can be important in the future.

2.4.1 EV charging

In this section, the goal is to map the categories of chargers in the market, and how they can
facilitate EVs a flexible resource. An essential distinction among EV chargers lies in their
classification as either passive or smart chargers. A passive charger is defined as a charger that
supplies a constant power flow, without any consideration to the battery status [37]. A smart
charger can consider the battery status, resulting in a dynamic power flow. A smart charger
can also enable intelligent charging management when connected to the grid, using a
communication module [37]. Enabling communication with the grid, a smart charger can
charge an EV during off-peak hours. The integration of smart chargers can also contribute to
the efficiency, reliability, and sustainability of the electric grid [37][38].

2.4.1.1 Categorization of EV chargers and their function

In Norway, the charging standard is divided into four different modes, which the International
Electrotechnical Commission presents under the standard IEC 62196 [39].

Mode 1 chargers refer to regular or slow charging from standard power outlets [39], and are
defined as passive chargers. These chargers are the least safe chargers, as they are connected
directly to the grid with no built-in safety functions. In Norway, this includes charging on a
230V 1-phase socket and a 400V 3-phase socket with a charging current of up to 16A. Modern
EVs no longer use this type of charger, and the maximum power output of the Mode 1 charger
is 2,3 kW [40]. The use of this charger would only have a flexible potential by manually
disconnecting the charger.

Mode 2 chargers are also defined as passive chargers, charging from standard power outlets,
but the charging cable is semi-active [39]. The cable has built-in safety features that can
partially handle potential risks that may occur during charging. In Norway, this includes
charging on a 230V 1-phase socket and a 400V 3-phase socket with a charging current of up to
32A. The maximum power, similar to Mode 1, is also 2,3 kW [40]. Mode 2 chargers flexibility

                                                             10
potential is also restricted by the manual disconnection of the charger. However, a slow
charger like this will have a small effect on the capacity of the grid.

Mode 3 chargers are defined both as slow chargers and fast chargers [39]. Mode 3 chargers are
smart chargers, making them able to postpone the charging until the time of day when
electricity is cheapest. The control and safety functions are integrated into one charging point,
which is known as a charging station. Mode 3 chargers can also enable smart home solutions,
by increasing or decreasing the charging current depending on the overall power consumption
in the house, making the EV act like a flexible resource. The charging power for Mode 3 varies
from 7,4 - 22 kW, based on the electricity grid [40]. Flexibility can be provided to the DSO by
the Mode 3 charger, especially if several Mode 3 chargers are managed simultaneously to
reduce the charging load when grid demand is high.

Mode 4 is defined as direct current fast charging [39], and is also considered a smart charger.
The charger is integrated into the charging station, which has a rectifier that provides DC
directly to the battery. In 2019, the most common power-level for Mode 4 charging stations
was 50 kW, but this is constantly increasing as more EVs are being designed to have the
opportunity to be charged with higher power [40]. Mode 4 chargers can be a challenge to the
grid because of the high charging power. However, Mode 4 chargers can enable that EVs
participation in the balancing market. As the charging stations can draw high amounts of
power, a throttling signal from the DSO or aggregators can be of great value for grid stability.

2.4.1.2 Bi-directional charging

A bi-directional charger is a charger with a dual-direction power flow, which means that the
power can be transferred to and from the EV [41]. The vehicle-to-building (V2B) technology
makes it possible for the EV to extract energy from a building, or transfer its energy to a
building. Thus, V2B technologies can contribute to the development of smart buildings [42].
Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) refers to the bi-directional power flow between EV and the power grid
[43]. V2G technology can be used to feed power back into the power grid during peak hours,
resulting in grid balancing and peak shaving.

V2G-enabled chargers also enables smart charging, which means that they charge the EVs
during low-demand periods. The batteries in the EVs can store energy during times of high
production and low demand, and discharge during times of low production and high demand,
acting as a Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) [43]. Continuous charging and discharging
can affect an EV batteries lifespan [43]. Participating in an IBDRP can give economic
incentives and motivation to the EV owners and EV charging station owners to integrate the
V2G technology. Today, only a few EVs are capable of bi-directional charging [44], but more
EVs that are entering the market are integrating this feature [45]. Bi-directional charges can
cost more due to the additional components needed to manage the bi-directional power flow.
Since both V2G and V2B technologies employ bi-directional chargers, in this study, V2G from
here on encompasses both the bi-directional power exchange between the EVPL and the power
grid, as well as between the EVPL and building.

3.1 EV Charging and Flexibility

In this chapter of the literature review, the focus is on the consequences of EV integration on
the grid, charging strategies, and V2G technologies. This is done to find out how the literature
extracts flexibility out of EVs, and how flexible charging and inflexible charging can be a
problem for the grid.

3.1.1 Results of increasing EV diffusion

With a goal of all new consumer cars sold in Norway being zero emission from 2025 [46], either
hydrogen or electric, the diffusion of EVs and the impact on the energy system has to be
investigated. Henriksen et al. [2] studied the impact of increasing numbers of EVs in Norway.
They find that the diffusion of EVs in Norway has significantly strained local electricity grids,
and the increase in total electricity use because of EVs is calculated to be 0.42% of the total
electricity use in Norway as of 2020. With electric energy use for transportation in Norway
forecasted to be at 20 TWh by 2030 [3], transportation is going to make a substantial impact
on the grid.

                                                             12
A study done by P. Wangsness and A. Halse [47] investigated the impact of EV density on
local grid costs in Norway based on a historical dataset spanning from 2008 to 2017. It is
stated that the diffusion of EVs within a DSOs operational area is often associated with higher
local grid costs. It is also found that an increase in EV diffusion in areas where the DSO has a
higher density of customers results in a lower effect on the local grid cost.

In an article written by Affonso and Kezunovic [48] it was found that the installation of EV
chargers in a commercial building increased the daily energy consumption by 23%. This
increase in electricity use creates issues in neighborhoods, overwhelming the old grids.
Stringent regulations and safety concerns have led to a shift from simply plugging EVs into a
regular socket, to installing dedicated charging stations, contributing to a substantial market
for various EV chargers in Norway [2]. This shift aggravates the strain on local power capacity,
leading to congestion being more of a concern. This necessitates costly upgrades to low-voltage
networks as DSOs struggle with increasing demand for EV charging infrastructure. However,
newer and faster charging stations provide not only a significant increase in safety but also
enable EV charging which considers peak loads which in turn can help with grid congestion.

3.1.2 Grid problems due to EV integration

The intense integration of EVs has a huge energy requirement and has serious effects on the
energy sector [49], such as overloading, voltage instability, voltage drops, voltage unbalance,
energy losses, harmonics, frequency variation, and consequently reductions in supply quality
[50].

A study from Canada considered the integration of EVs into the distribution grid, where the
impacts of uncoordinated charging were evaluated on a real test feeder [51]. It was found that
for normal and fast charging scenarios, the uncoordinated charging led to unacceptable voltage
drops in remote buses, and the feeder line loading constraint was violated at 15% and 25% EV
penetration for fast charging and normal charging respectively. Another case study was done
on the power system on the Danish island of Bornholm [52], which analyzed the impacts of EV
penetration on the grid. For the uncontrolled study, the feasible integration of EVs was 10%,
while the controlled charging could handle the integration more effectively. Voltage drops are
found to be the limiting factor, and the peak demand in the network is increased by 31% for a
50% EV integration controlled charging scenario. From the literature, it is evident that
increased EV penetration with uncontrolled charging significantly increases peak demand and
strains the grid. Having more flexible EV charging is one way to combat this.

3.1.3 Flexibility from EV charging

Knezovic et al. [53] state in their research that DSOs have historically dealt with grid-security
challenges by making investments in grid reinforcement. However, an alternative solution,
enabled by the rollout of smart meters and high penetration of flexible loads, would be the
increased use of flexibility services. Flexible loads, with EVs at their forefront, can modulate

                                                             13
their consumption or even inject power back into the grid, depending on current grid conditions.

As the battery system in EVs can be modeled as a flexible load, looking into BESS literature
can also be relevant. In an article written by Y. Hu et al in 2022, they investigate the potential
utilization of BESS in some of the major European electricity markets [54]. It was found that
BESS can be a feasible solution in some parts of Europe, but has more limited potential in
Norway. They state that this is due to the Norwegian high penetration level of hydropower.
However, the study shows that the potentially profitable utilization increases every year in
Norway. The need for power increases and hydropower in Norway is already well developed,
making BESS a potential part of the solution for the future grid in Norway.

Winschermann et al. studied the significance of historical data in enhancing EV charging
strategies at office buildings [38]. Given the challenges posed by the high energy demands of
EVs on the electrical grid, smart charging was proposed as a potential solution. In the study,
historical data from an office parking lot, specifically focusing on arrival times, energy
requirements, and dwell times, was used to determine how past data mirrors real-time
operations. An algorithm was used to construct a power charging profile for EVs based on
user-specific parameters. Using a greedy charging approach in office settings can result in high
power peaks due to synchronized arrivals. Here, greedy charging entails charging without an
energy management system, which charges at the maximum available power from the plug-in.
Using historical data for efficient planning helped reduce the peaks by 50%. Complete
historical data which concerned arrival and departure times and energy requirements, was
found to be essential for efficient planning. In an article written by Affonso and Kezunovic
[48], smart EV charging in an EVPL for a commercial building is used to reduce the cost of
operation for the building owner while also reducing the strain on the local grid transformer.
Without the smart EV charger, the transformer was overloaded in the summer season, which
led to accelerated aging of the transformer.

Understanding the motivation for the end-users to participate in smart charging and the
flexible use of their EVs is important. The study by I. Henriksen et al. [2] presents the key
motivations for the adoption of smart chargers for EVs. Participants emphasized the safety
and faster charging speed of smart chargers compared to regular sockets. Many of the
participants were interested in smart home technology, which in turn made smart charging
very interesting. Additionally, the recognition of practical and economic benefits, such as
improved load management and potential incentives from the pilot project, played a significant
role in the decision-making process. The flexibility of smart charging, allowing users to delay
charging during periods of lower electricity demand, was also seen as advantageous. In
conclusion, the end-users adoption of smart chargers was influenced by a combination of safety
considerations, technological interest, practical and economic advantages, and the flexibility
offered in the charging process [2].

                                                             14
3.1.4 Vehicle to grid

When optimizing the flexible use of an EVPL, V2G is a technology that could help provide the
wanted flexibility. There are three main domains for V2G studies, namely the transmission
grid, distribution network, and consumer [41]. This literature study focuses on the consumer
domain.

A study conducted by Yee Ting Chai et al. [41] introduces a two-stage optimization method
for EVs in a V2G program at an office building. For the V2G program to work, the scheme
must address both the needs of EVs and building owners. For building owners, EVs need to be
reliably present to supply power. EV owners, on the other hand, should benefit financially
without V2G affecting their travel plans. To achieve this, the EV owners are allowed to reach
their desired State of Charge (SOC) at any time by raising the energy anxiety factor. The
proposed method uses day-ahead optimization based on expected loads and EV patterns,
aiming to cut electricity costs. Then, in the second stage, a real-time optimization adjusts to
actual EV behaviors. The simulations show promising results, although it is shown to be
difficult to consider the monthly peak demand when optimizing for shorter intervals.

Jingli Guo et al. [55] explores the impact of V2G services on EV battery degradation. The
study introduces EVs as a solution to environmental issues and highlights the potential of V2G
to support electricity grids. They also express concerns about the possible acceleration of
battery degradation due to frequent charging and discharging cycles associated with V2G.
Findings from different studies vary on whether V2G has acceptable or detrimental impacts on
battery degradation. Factors affecting battery degradation include battery chemistry,
charging/discharging regimes, types of V2G services, and service frequency [55]. Appropriate
V2G management, such as optimizing cycling during idle states, could minimize battery
degradation. It is concluded by emphasizing the need for accurate battery degradation models
and improvements in battery management for V2G deployment. Zhoa [56] states that
aggregators should compensate the EV owners for battery degradation when providing V2G
services to the power grid.

3.2 Demand Response

As mentioned in Section 2.3.1, DR is a method where the objective is to manage and modify
electricity consumption in response to external signals, such as changes in electricity prices or
grid reliability concerns [23]. The goal is to research how the two of the most common
classifications of DRPs, PBDRP and IBDRP, are implemented and used.

3.2.1 Demand charge

The demand charge is categorized as PBDR and is a part of the electricity bill for end-users. It
is based on the highest level or levels of electricity demand during a specific period, typically

                                                             15
measured in kilowatts (kW) [29]. In this section, the implementation of the DCC is
investigated together with the impact of considering the long-term impact of short-term
decisions in optimization models.

In the study done by Y.He et al. [57], the DCC in a charging station for an electric bus system
was analyzed. DCC is defined as a part of the total system cost, and the text illustrates how
both the total system cost and demand charge increase with an increase in the demand charge
rate. When including an energy storage system, the total cost for the system was reduced by
9.2%. In the study by K. Thorvalsen et al. [58], the implications of the new capacity-based
grid tariffs in Norway, which include a demand charge, are investigated. Stochastic Dynamic
Programming (SDP) is implemented to represent the long-term impacts of the demand charge.
The method was applied to a real Norwegian household, to strategically determine the
cost-optimal peak power and the expected total electricity cost. Two scenarios were
investigated: one where the future cost from the grid tariff, i.e. the demand charge, is
unknown and not considered, and another with perfect information about stochastic inputs.
The results revealed a 36.1% increase in cost when not considering the demand charge.

Operating an energy system with a monthly demand charge can pose challenges when trying
to identify the optimal peak-import level to aim for. A metaheuristic approach was conducted
in [59] to balance the short-term cost of operation for a household with the long-term impacts
of the demand charge, using a user-defined weighting constant. A high value for the weighting
constant indicated that the energy management system weighted the real-time price less. A
low weighting constant would indicate that the energy management system pays more
attention to the real-time price, resulting in a high DCC. When the the weighting constant was
optimal, the total cost was reduced by 64,76 % compared to when the system did not have a
management system.

3.2.2 Use of flexibility in IBDRPs

In this section the goal is to showcase how earlier studies used IBDRPs to provide the DSOs
with the needed flexibility. The studies have also looked into how and what the incentives
should be for participation. Latly, this section presents a study that investigates the
consequences of different notice times prior to an IBDR-signal.

Rana et al. studied how a residential community could provide ancillary services like voltage
support and loss minimization to a DSO [60], assuming that the ancillary service provider has
information on the status of the grid. It was found that when the objective was to minimize
the losses, the active losses were reduced by 4.2%. In the article written by Berg et al. [61], the
primary objective was to study how an energy community and a DSO can coordinate to
improve the voltage profile in the grid. A minimum compensation of 15 e per year to the
energy community for the voltage services was found, based on a yearly cost increase of 0.12%
because of increased electricity use and battery degradation. It is suggested that the energy
community and DSO should collaborate to make sure that the battery characteristics are
sufficient to improve the voltage profile in the grid.

                                                             16
Zhang et al. [62] studied smart EV charging at non-residential sites to reduce peak
consumption using a proposed water-filling algorithm with an IBDRP. The work found that an
80% DCCR of the theoretical maximum could be achieved with the IBDRP, leading to
significant savings to the retail venue. In the study conducted by Yee Ting Chai et al. [41],
where a V2G program at an office building was implemented, a detailed analysis of the costs
and benefits associated with V2G to reduce peak electricity import is included [41]. To
encourage EV participation in V2G, an IBDRP is implemented, which rewards the discharging

peak demand, and optimizing resource utilization when various stakeholders in the energy
system collaborate.
Study [63] investigated how different notice times in a DRP, with continuous updates of wind
forecasts, affected flexible consumers. The DRP used was demand bidding, which is defined as
a market based program. The study found that a long notice time is associated with higher
forecast deviations due to the uncertainty in long-term wind power predictions. A upside
however, is that the longer time period means the energy demand is more flexible, allowing
consumers to adjust their consumption schedules. Shorter notice time resulted in smaller
forecast deviations as the certainty of predictions in wind power increased. However, the
flexibility of energy demand decreased, which made it harder for consumers to reschedule their
consumption at short notice.

4.1 Deterministic Programming

The field of optimization belongs to the field of applied mathematics and encompasses the use of

[64]. An optimization problem is a mathematical approach to a physical problem, where the
problem is analyzed to control the output through the input variables. A deterministic
optimization problem will always give the same result given the same input. That is, there is
no randomness or uncertainty in the modeling.

4.1.2 The dual problem

Associated with every LP problem, there is a dual problem which is defined with exactly the
same input data as the original primal problem [64]. The dual values can provide insight into a
solution and help in sensitivity analyses to changing parameters.

4.2 Stochastic programming

Stochastic Programming (SP) is a methodology used to handle uncertain information in
optimization, and making decisions under uncertainty. Randomness is now introduced to the
problem for a more realistic approach.

4.2.1 Multistage stochastic programming

Multistage Stochastic Programming (MSSP) is an optimizing programming model that
includes multiple stages that for each stage presents new stochastic information. A common
example is that the stages represent time periods. If it in reality is practically impossible to
perfectly forecast uncertain state variables in the future, the problem is essentially stochastic
[65]. In each stage, the optimal solution is determined for a number of subproblems. The
decision made in this stage depends on the history up to the current stage. The set of possible
future sequences of outcomes in the problem are called scenarios [66]. The scenarios can be
ordered in scenario tree, as illustrated in Figure 4.2.1. Illustrated are the nodes n for each
realization, the time-stages t = [1, 2, 3] and 4 scenarios ω.

              19
          Figure 4.2.1: Illustration of a scenario tree with 3 stages, 4 scenarios, 7 nodes

4.2.2 Decomposition techniques

In MSSP, the problem, and the scenario trees, quickly become very large. State variables are
used to describe the state of the system at the current stage. These variables have the Markov
property, meaning only the current state matters, not the history leading up to the state [67].
A way of solving these large multistage problems is by using decomposition techniques. One of
these decomposition techniques is SDP.
As commonly done with other approaches, SDP can be used to minimize costs. For SDP, the
problem is decomposed into several one-stage subproblems, where the objective is to minimize
the sum of immediate and future operating costs [68]. The typical approach is to form a
backward recursion resulting in an optimal decision associated with each state at each stage
[66]. The SDP recursion requires the enumeration of all combinations of initial values [68]. As
a consequence, computational efforts increase exponentially with the number of state variables.
A problem that can arise for Dynamic Programs with multiple states, as seen in SDP, is that
the number of discrete states per stage grows exponentially with the number of state variables,
which is also known as the curse of dimensionality.
SDDP is an optimization method that can be applied to MSSP problems. No discretization is
necessary, and the curse of dimensionality is avoided. Unlike SDP, where the entire state space
is explored, SDDP explores the most interesting states based on sampling from the uncertainty
distribution [69]. A scenario tree for a three-stage problem is shown in Figure 4.2.2. An
important observation from the figure is that the number of scenario-states is the same for
stage 2 and stage 3. Here, the scenario tree does not fan out as in SDP, shown in Figure 4.2.1.

                                                             20
                            Figure 4.2.2: SDDP Scenario tree for three stages

The solution approach is based on the approximation of expected future cost functions by
piecewise linear functions, instead of using an interpolated table done in SDP [68]. These
approximate functions are obtained from the dual solutions of the optimization problem at
each stage and can be interpreted as Benders cuts in a stochastic, multistage decomposition
algorithm [65]. SDDP is a recursive algorithm, which employs a forward and backward pass.
The forward pass moves down the scenario three picking scenarios at random. The information
about the state variables is carried over from stage to stage. In the backward pass, all of the
possible scenarios are run and Benders cuts are created, representing the future cost of the
state variable. A drawback with SDDP is that non-convexities can not be easily treated in the
modeling [70]. The non-convexities can in some cases be handled by either making
approximations or combining SDDP with SDP.

4.3 Model Predictive Control Optimization

Model predictive control (MPC), also known as receding horizon control, moving horizon
control, or rolling horizon control, is a feedback control technique that solves an optimization
problem at each time step to determine the optimal control actions over a fixed time horizon
[71]. Normally MPC consists of three stages; a model to predict future outputs, calculate
control actions to optimize performance, and apply a horizon strategy to dynamically update
new information in the optimization process when new information becomes available [72].
MPC is a well used feedback control technique and has been used since 1980 [71]. MPC
algorithms often solve problems under uncertainty or large-scale optimization problems [73],
and are often used to solve MSSP problems [74]. MPC algorithms have among other things
become important in the operation of energy systems because the optimal operation must
account for the uncertainty of consumer’s behavior, weather conditions, and power generation
from renewable energy sources [75]. The reason why the feedback control technique goes by so
many names is due to how the update of new information is done. The control techniques are
commonly divided into receding horizon (RH) and rolling horizon (ROH) [76], presented in
Figure 4.3.1

                                                             21
         (a) Illustration of a rolling horizon framework (b) Illustration of a receding horizon framework

                       Figure 4.3.1: MPC control techniques, inspired from [73]

Figure 4.3.1a presents a ROH approach, consisting of three different horizons: the scheduling
horizon (SH), the prediction horizon (PH), and the control horizon (CH) [73]. SH represents
the total operational period and is decomposed into equal time intervals. The length of the PH
is defined by the accuracy of the prediction data for each subsequent time step, and the data
can be considered to have some certainty [71]. The CH has the same initial conditions as the
PH but covers a shorter duration, often with a time step equal to one time interval. The solved
decision variables in the CH then used as input in the PH, and are presented in the final solved
model.

Figure 4.3.1a showcases the dynamic updating of horizons for a RH-framework. In this
approach, the PH is equal to the SH. A long PH will allow more flexibility in optimizing future
control actions but can result in more uncertainty in the data. A short PH will on the other
hand lower the CHs ability to plan ahead effectively.

For both of the control techniques, when the optimization problem is solved for each time step
in the CH, the initial values are decided at the first time step, and the algorithm optimizes the
optimization problem with a forecast for the next steps. From the solved model, the optimized
decision variables will be given as initial values for the next time step. The algorithm will then
solve the new optimization problem with the new time horizon.

In this section, studies on EV charging modeling approaches and methodologies are presented
and compared. The goal is to find and compare the different factors, assumptions, and

                                                             22
4.4.1 Model approaches for optimizing EV charging

One of the challenges in modeling EVs is that each EV represents unique data because each
EV is dependent on the behavior of the EV owner. When modeling an EVPL, the ideal is to
have specific individual data on each EV for all time steps. A comparison is made on the
distinctions between an aggregated model and a non-aggregated model.

In the study done by J. Zhoa [56], an aggregated model for EV driving patterns is used to
simplify the optimization model. The decision to use an aggregated model is driven by
practical considerations, computational efficiency, contractual arrangements with EV owners,
and the need for effective representation and scheduling strategies in the electricity market,
especially when dealing with uncertainty and risk. In Zhoas study, the following is stated:

The computation burden can be very heavy if each individual EV is dispatched directly by the
system operators. Therefore an aggregated model is developed in the study to condense
individual driving pattern data into a simplified model, in analogy to a virtual battery with the
total capacity, charging, and discharging ability changing over time. In practice, the EV
aggregator makes a long-term contract with EV owners. [56].

It was found that when V2G is included, an aggregated model was often used for the EVPL
[77] [56]. However, in the study done by Y. Chai et al. [41], V2G was included, and a
non-aggregated model is used. To determine the charging and discharging schedule of the EV,
they predict day-ahead data of the EVs. The required data was arrival time, departure time,
and the desired SOC. EV behavior is defined as vehicle occupancy, the arrival- and departure
time. The EV owners might not be able to stay idle until the announced departure time, which
is a concern for the building owners who require the full commitment of the EV owners to
participate in the V2G event. In the study, EV owners who practice flexible working hours are
unable to leave the building earlier than the expected time slot at the desired SOC value. It is
stated that the coordination between building and EV owners is challenging due to the
stochastic nature of load and EV mobility, and therefore they require a full commitment of the
EV owners.

Through the previous studies it is found that using a non-aggregated model requires EV input
data to be provided in advance, or many assumptions have to be made. When planning
optimization for the EVPL, full commitment from the EV owners is necessary. Additionally,
models implementing V2G often use an aggregated approach to avoid the requirement of
detailed information on each EV.

4.4.2 Model predictive control and control techniques

The goal of this section is to review the feedback control methods used in other literature and
assess the possibilities of different control techniques. Other methods used for real-time and
feedback control are Minimum Variance, Smith Prediction, Generalized Minimum Variance,
and Pole Placement. With the right parameters, these methods can perform very well,

                                                             23
however, in journal [72], several weaknesses are pointed out. These methods face challenges
such as increased computational effort and sensitivity to errors, difficulty handling some types
of systems, high control effort, and lack of robustness against variable or unknown delays.
Modifications of Generalized Minimum Variance led to the development of MPC, and journal
[72] states that MPC is preferred because it offers better performance and control.

In Section 4.3, RH and ROH were presented as two control techniques for MPC. Article [76]
points out that when using RH optimization, the system can update the available information
and obtain a better response. A RH-framework was implemented in a study done by I.
Skoglund et al. [78] to schedule the charging and discharging of batteries in commercial
buildings while also considering the stochastic nature of the data and the monthly DCC. Using
the RH-framework to operate the battery, the total system cost was reduced up to 17.8%. In
[79], an RH approach was used on a deterministic multi objective optimization problem to find
the optimal weighting between diesel fuel cost and battery wear cost. The RH approach
resulted in a long run time, as an optimization procedure must be done for each time step.
However, they also found that the results obtained from RH were more effective and feasible
when compared with other conventional optimization methods, as RH offers a significant
advantage by accounting for the uncertainties and intermittent nature of renewable energy
resources.

In a study done by M. Tvedt [80], a ROH-framework was used to solve a linear optimization
problem to minimize the total cost of operation, using flexible assets. The study did not
consider the monthly demand charge which meant that using ROH was therefore suitable. The
study points out that grid tariff could be interesting to include in further work, however, this
would require changes in how the data is updated in the PH. Article [81] uses a
ROH-framework with an 8 hour PH to optimize EV charging. The study suggests
implementing dynamic horizons to optimize EV charging better.

In the article written by K. Baker et al., a stochastic RH approach is used to plan for energy
storage systems from an operational point of view [82]. By integrating a stochastic RH
approach, uncertainty can be added to the optimization. When using an MPC approach on a
stochastic optimization problem, the RH algorithm had to consider all time horizons and
scenarios simultaneously. This made the problem very complex and computationally heavy. In
their future work, they suggested that integrating scenario-based decomposition methods, like
Benders’ decomposition, could expand the scope to include more scenarios in the problem.

The studies show that a feedback control method can lead to cost savings and improved
system reliability, making it an important tool for analyzing and optimizing real-time energy
management strategies. Study [82] also states that a decomposition method can be of value in
combination with RH. A ROH approach is seen to struggle to consider the monthly demand
charge. Dynamic horizons is suggested to provide better real-time optimization of EV charging.

4.4.3 Stochastic dual dynamic programming

SDDP is commonly used in hydropower and hydropower modeling as it avoids the necessity of
discretizing the state values such as inflow and reservoir volume, making it possible to model
several reservoirs at once. The issue with non-convexity mentioned in 4.2.2 within hydro
generation modeling is explained and illustrated in [83]. The non-convexity is avoided in [84]
by modeling the before non-convex price state as a Markov chain using discrete spaces similar
to what is done in SDP.

SDDP is also used as a decomposition technique for applications outside of hydrothermal
models. In [85], an SDDP algorithm is used to model an integrated photovoltaic and energy
storage hybrid system in a household. The MSSP presented had 10 scenarios for each of the 96
stages, which would be computationally unfeasible if discretized. Therefore, SDDP is used to
avoid the well-known curse of dimensionality associated with stochastic problems. In [86] an
SDDP approach is developed to model distributed energy resources as an aggregator. The
reason for choosing SDDP instead of other decomposition methods is SDDPs ability to refine
solution quality in the areas where the solution is most likely to occur, together with SDDPs
ability to handle multiple state variables with lower computational effort.

In [87] SDDP is used mainly to consider both short-term costs and the price signals of
long-term costs of the energy use in a building. The future cost function created by SDDP
managed to represent the long-term future cost implication of the raising the peak, although
not with ideal accuracy.

4.4.4 Combining receding horizon and stochastic dual dynamic
          programming

As suggested by K. Baker et al. in Section 4.4.2 [82], there are incentives to integrate scenario
based decomposition methods with the RH framework. Using the long term planning from the
expected future value functions of the decomposition techniques could provide value to the
short-term optimization in RH.

An article written by A. Papavasiliou et al. [88] proposes an MSSP model for
transmission-constrained economic dispatch, emphasizing real-time optimization of storage
deployment with an uncertain multiarea renewable production. The authors state that much of
the existing literature analyzes storage values through two-stage day-ahead models or economic
dispatch models with limited look-ahead, which they find too optimistic. A gain of 1.1% is
found when using the stochastic real-time approach rather than a deterministic forecast.

SDDP together with an RH-framework was incorporated in a study done by F. Hafiz et al. [89]
to operate a battery system. The SDDP algorithm was used to solve an MSSP, that iteratively
constructed a segmented linear approximation of the future costs function. The RH was used
to update the forecast of the load and solar generation, which improves the optimal
decision-making process for the battery system. SDDP provided set points for the battery

                                                             25
SOC, and the RH-framework operated the battery in real-time to reduce the cost of operation.
Here, significant peak hour savings are found compared to a heuristic method.
The integration of RH control with SDDP entails a promising approach for optimizing energy
systems under uncertainty. By utilizing the long-term planning capabilities of SDDP with
uncertainty and the real-time strategy of RH, improvements in decision making and
operational efficiency can be achieved.

5.1 Model Description

This chapter introduces the model that was created to minimize the electricity costs for an
office building with an EVPL. The model setup and methodology are presented in detail,
covering the motivations and assumptions for the choices made.

5.1.1 Model overview

The modeling of the operation of the office building with a connected EVPL requires detailed
information on a short-term basis to make hourly operational decisions. The system is also
modeled over one month to consider the monthly grid tariff costs. The system is investigated
for participation in an IBDRP and is also subject to day-ahead spot prices, which require
dynamic updates of information. A version of a RH-framework is introduced which introduces
dynamic horizons that dynamically update the information available in the optimization
problem. The information available to the EVPL is on a daily basis, making it beneficial to
decompose the monthly problem into daily stages. The monthly peak is the only state variable
of the model, and information about the monthly peak is carried over between the daily stages.

As the demand charge is based on the peak import hour in a month, the long-term impact of
the peak level in the model needs to be considered for an optimal result. The RH-algorithm on
its own would struggle to optimize for the final peak value of the month, as detailed
information is only provided on a daily stage-wise basis. SDDP is therefore introduced to
create future cost curves containing cost information about the monthly peak. The
SDDP-framework uses the same decomposed monthly problem, but also introduces uncertainty
in the modeling of the office-building load for a more realistic approach.

5.3 Receding Horizon Algorithm and Framework

As mentioned previously, a RH approach with dynamic horizons is implemented in this study
to be able to account for the sudden IBDR activaion signals and also optimize the operation of
the system on a hourly basis. The framework for the approach is presented in this section,
explaining how the information is revealed to the optimization problem in the various horizons.

The load data for the office building is assumed to be known for the entire SH, and the spot
price is revealed only once daily. While a ROH approach could work for this, it would not be
able to account for the monthly demand charge. Alternatively, a RH approach with a PH
equal to one month could consider the monthly demand charge, but a constant PH can not
include information about the future daily spot prices. Therefore, this methodology presents a
combination of both approaches, referred to as RH, which uses future cost information from
SDDP to handle the long-term implications of the demand charge.

When implementing the RH algorithm, the first stage is to define the initial state of the
system. First, the variables in the model are initialized, including the initial variable P init.
The RH-framework considers three different horizons, where the SH considers the overall
operational period. The CH represents the current hour of operation that is to be determined

                                                             35
and moves forward for each step. Lastly, the PH represents the period within the optimization
problem containing detailed information about the data related to spot prices, office demand,
and EV demand.

            Figure 5.3.1: Receding horizon framework. Figure inspired from Paper I.

Figure 5.3.1 shows how the linear cuts derived from the SDDP-framework describe the
information beyond the PH. The linear cuts give the PH a simplified representation of the rest
of the SH, including the monthly demand charge. The methodology for this thesis presents a
new PH to the RH-framework at each time step. When the spot prices for the next day are
revealed, the PH is expanded, revealing information about the next day of operation. At the
same time, the linear cuts from the SDDP-framework are replaced so the PH can consider the
cuts from the next day of operation.
Within the RH framework, operational decisions are made at each time-step t in the CH, while
the PH is continuously adjusted based on the operating day, as illustrated in Figure 5.3.1. At
each step, the highest achieved peak ppeak so far in the optimization is stored in P peak,init
together with the accumulated EV charging demand for the current day PdEV,init and the
accumulated ENS for the current day EN Sdinit. The stored values are then used as input for
the next time-step t+1 within the RH framework. This process is repeated until the
RH-framework reaches the final time step of the SH. When integrating V2G into the model,
the accumulated V2G charging PdV 2G,ch,init and discharging PdV 2G,dch,init are also stored for the
current day, and provided as inputs for the next time-step. Figure 5.3.2 presents a block
diagram of the RH algorithm used in this thesis.

6.3.1 Stochastic office building data

The stochasticity of the model is based on the uncertain office-building load. Only the
SDDP-framework considers the stochastic data. Three scenarios are assumed for each decision
stage, low, medium, and high. The medium scenario is equal to the historical office load data,
presented in Section 6.2.1. High and low scenarios have a 30 % higher and lower office building
demand respectively. The motivation behind the stochastic scenarios is to have a more realistic
model with an uncertain future. The scenario percentages and probabilities for high and low
scenarios are chosen quite arbitrarily and are not based on any previous work. The transition
probabilities between each scenario are only based on which scenario is next. In other words,
the origin scenario that is being transitioned from does not affect the transition probability.
The probability of the medium scenario is 50%, high is 25%, and low is 25%. The cuts that are
exported to be used in the short-term planning in RH are the medium scenario cuts. In some
cases, deterministic cuts are used. These cuts are in practice generated by making the high
and low scenarios equal to the medium scenario.

6.3.2 Input data to the RH algorithm

At the first day of operation, P peak,init is initialized to a value of 0. Further, implementing the
IBDR-signals may result in the system not covering the daily aggregated EV demand. Because
of this, a slack variable has been integrated into the system, introducing a cost of 30,000 NOK
per Energy Not Served (ENS). This cost is not included in the total cost, as its purpose is only
to ensure that the system does not optimize for any ENS, but is forced to deliver the requested
charging if possible. However, in the economic analysis in this thesis, a penalty fee of 100
NOK/kWh for ENS is added to the total cost. The assumption for the size of this penalty fee is
to visualize the economic impacts of not being able to provide the mandatory charging. Using
a cost of 30,000 NOK/kWh would make it more difficult to compare the economic results.

6.3.2.1 Prediction horizons

The RH-framework uses the historical measured data for the office building load, which is
equivalent to only using the medium scenarios. Thus, the RH-framework uses deterministic
data which does not include any uncertainties. In the RH-framework, the length of the three
horizons has to be determined. The SH considers the overall operational period of one month.
The CH represents the hour of operation that is to be determined, resulting in a horizon of one
hour. Lastly, the PH has a horizon equal to the time remaining of the operating day, thus the
horizon will continuously decrease until the spot prices for the next day are revealed. The
RH-framework has a new PH for each time step, where every PH receives a simplified
representation of the rest of the SH from the linear cuts provided by the SDDP-framework. In

                                                             43
Norway, the spot prices for the next day are revealed at 1 PM [96], thus at 1 PM, the
RH-framework will receive the spot prices for the next day. When this happens, the PH is
expanded from 12 to 36 hours, revealing information about the next day of operation. When
the new information is revealed, the PH will also receive new linear cuts from the
SDDP-framework, which now presents a simplified representation of the cost of the remaining
SH. The dynamic updating of the RH horizons is presented in 6.3.1, where the blue future cost
curve is replaced with the red future cost curve considering the next day of operation.

Plotted in Figure 7.1.2a are the future cost curves created for September using deterministic

Comparing the different days plotted in Figure 7.1.2a, it is observed that the later it is in the
month, the more the curves are raised closer to the demand charge. This entails that the later
it is in the month, the higher the cost is for increasing the peak value and the more likely it is
for the model to not raise the peak. When plotting for the marginal future cost curves, it is
expected that later in the month and the closer the model is to realizing the DCC, the higher
the marginal cost should be for increasing the peak value. This is seen in all of the plots to a

varying degree. The later days have less potential to fully take advantage of the spot price
variations. The weighting of the DCC and the cost of spot price are always competing. At the
end of the month, the DCC has a bigger impact on the total cost than in the start of the
month, because of fewer opportunities to take advantage of spot price variations.

Further, a result of comparing the stochastic input data with the deterministic input data is
seen on the curves with more detailed and smaller variations for increasing peak values caused
by the stochastic load scenarios. These smaller bumps are cost levels which are because the
model has to optimize for higher and lower loads than the "normal" load in the stochastic
data. It can also be observed that the deterministic model generally has a higher marginal
future cost for lower peak values, which indicates that the model values a lower peak and less
flexibility in spot price variations. The stochastic model has to account for a possible increase
in the office-building load of 30% and therefore values raising the peak.

SDDPs quality of only optimizing for the interesting state variable values is easily observed in
the plots. In Figure 7.1.2b the marginal cost is seen to be constant at a lower value than the
DCC for peak values above 260 kWh/h. This is an inaccuracy caused by the model never
optimizing for peaks this high. The same phenomena can be seen for peak values lower than
215 kWh/h. Here, days 13 and later have a marginal cost higher than zero, which would not
have been the case in reality. These days are not optimized and tested on these low peak
values, and these values therefore lack detailed information on the cost of raising the peaks.
The increased detail and accuracy in the peaks of interest decrease the detail and accuracy in
the peaks outside of the researched range. Outer-lying peaks are represented poorly as they
are not tested at all of the possible peak values. This could cause problems if something out of
the ordinary (such as an IBDRP throttling signal) shifts the peak values outside of the
researched range. Using a decomposition method that discretizes the state variable range into
regular intervals such as SDP would increase the future-cost prediction accuracy for outer-lying
peaks, but would also lead to inaccuracies between the discrete states used.

Figure 7.1.1a which shows the deterministic case in March, is an interesting case as the
increase in marginal cost on a single peak value is very pronounced. The marginal cost is
unchanged at 0 NOK/kWh/h until approximately 210 kWh/h for days 3, 8, and 13, entailing it
is free to increase the peak until around 210 kWh/h. The later stages have a marginal cost
almost equal to the DCC from the beginning. It is evident from this that the peak value for
the month is "set" between days 13 and 18. A probable cause of this can be seen on day 16 in
Figure 6.2.2a, where the office load has a high peak. A similar peak can also be observed on
day 7, but since it is not the last peak, the marginal cost of increasing the peak to the
predicted peak on day 16 is zero.

7.3.6 Challenges with V2G

V2G is a growing technology in grid flexibility, although the impact is not positive in all cases.

7.4.4 The potential of 0 and 2 hour notice times

Until now, only an IBDR-signal with the worst-case scenario has been analyzed for C3, which
has led to the 0 and 2 hour notice times having ENS. The goal of this section is not to find the
optimal IBDRP when the notice time is 0 or 2 hours, but to analyze the potential of these
notice times without the worst-case scenario.

It is found that when the notice time is 0 hours, even an IBDR-signal that requires 1 kW EV
capacity reduction every weekday leads to ENS. However, there is only one day in the month
with ENS, as this is the only day which maximizes maximizes the EV charging capacity in the
hours after the activation. If this critical day does not receive an IBDR-signal, it can be
expected that a larger EV capacity reduction can be applied in the activation hour without
resulting in ENS. However, it is found that when the EV capacity is reduced by 5 kW, there is
ENS for days 7, 18, 28, and 31. There is shown to be limited flexibility in the system for a
notice time of 0 hours as it struggles to avoid ENS. At the same time, it is seen from Figure
7.4.4 that if an IBDR-signal is not given on day 7, the peak does not increase, resulting in a
changed planned EV charging. This entails that it is difficult to deduce which days of the
month which are critical for the operation to avoid ENS.

For notice time 2, the system does not lead to ENS if the IBDR-signal, given every weekday,
requires an EV capacity reduction of 104 kW or less during the activation hour. However, 0
ENS can not be guaranteed due to the potential changes in planned EV charging when the
IBDR-signal is not given every weekday. Without IBDR-signals, the monthly peak is 217.66
kWh/h due to the 50% reduction in DCC that the 2 hour notice time provides. If an
IBDR-signal is given on any day other than day 0, it is uncertain whether the planned EV
charging has the flexibility to shift 104 kW to the other hours. If only one day in the month is
seen to be critical for ENS, the system could risk entering the IBDRP and hope for no IBDR
activations. Further, it is found that when the reduced EV capacity is bigger than 104 kW,

                                                             70
ENS occurs on multiple days. The reduction in EV capacity equal to 105 kW results in 19 days
having the same amount of ENS, which also indicates that the planned EV charging is quite
similar across the month.

From the analysis, it can be discussed if an IBDR-signal with notice times of 0 hours and 2
hours can be of potential benefit for the office building. If the IBDR-signal is received only
once a month, it is possible that notice times of 0 and 2 hours could yield 0 ENS, however, this
depends on three things. First, which days the IBDR-signal is given. Second, the planned EV
charging for the given day. And last, the amount of reduced EV capacity in the activation hour.

7.5 General Discussion

There are many assumptions made in the modeling of the system. To discuss how they affect
the results, and if any assumptions should have been altered, a general discussion chapter is
provided.

In this thesis, an IBDR-signal has in most analyses been given on the first day of operation, or
every weekday throughout the entire month. An IBDR-signal every weekday, which in this
thesis is considered a worst-case scenario, is analyzed to see if the system can handle the
maximum possible strain.

If an IBDR-signal never occurs, participating in the program would provide direct cost savings
for the office building. However, this would only lead to a cost increase for the grid operators,
but at the same time, the DSO will have insurance on how they can resolve peak power
problems in the grid. With the grid becoming more constrained, peak power problems in the
grid can increase, which can also lead to situations where more IBDR-signals are sent.

Bi-directional chargers can have a higher price due to the additional components needed to
manage the two-way flow of electricity. This thesis found that the cost savings from V2G was
limited. It can therefore be discussed if the cost savings will cover the potential additional cost
of the bi-directional chargers and the degradation of the EV batteries. If another month had
been analyzed, where the spot prizes had even more variation throughout the day, it could
have been found that the V2G technology was more valuable for the system. However, a
positive with the V2G technology is that it enables the system to meet an IBDR-signal which
requires more power than the EVPL can throttle in the activation hour. The possible
economic benefits of providing extra power to the grid were not discussed and could have
helped the V2G technology become more profitable. It was also assumed that all the EVs that
used the EVPL were capable of bi-directional charging. Today this is an unlikely case,
however, the V2G technology is under development, and in the future, more cars could be
capable of bi-directional charging.

The energy not supplied variable in the model is quite an important factor when the costs were
discussed. In the model it was assumed that the EVPL demand should be met, no matter the
cost. It is seen that the ENS makes the 0 hour and 2 hour notice times difficult to operate.
Further, the cost of the energy demand that could not be met is dependent on the agreement
with the EVPL owner and the EV owners. An agreement could be possible that states if there
was to come an IBDR-signal, the EVPL can not be held accountable for the lost charging. Or
the EV owners can be asked to charge for a longer time if they want a full charge. It is

                                                             73
probably possible to come to an agreement where the cost of ENS is not as detrimental to the
total operational costs as the Cnot delivered = 100 NOK/kWh/h.

The modeling of the EVPL required the aggregation of the EV data. This was done due to the
lack of information available on the individual operation of the EVs. A detailed individual
modeling of the EVs could provide a more detailed insight into the operation of the EVPL.
The participation in the flexible charging and V2G technology could be optional for the EV
owners, reducing the flexibility potential. There could also be individual data available on the
charging times, charging power, arrival and departure times, battery SOC at arrival and
departure, battery charging profile, losses, and so forth. These input values would either
require empirical data or many assumptions, which could worsen the results in the same way
that aggregating the EVs does. Individual modeling of the EVs would increase the
computational effort of both the real-time modeling and the creation of the future cost curves.

SDDP is a modeling framework that can handle multiple state variables without the
computational efforts being too extreme. The model developed in this thesis only has one state
variable, indicating that other decomposition methods could have been used. SDP is another
decomposition method that discretizes the state variable, mentioned in Section 4.2.2 of the
Theory. SDP was used in [58] to represent the long-term implications of the demand charge.
The future cost curves presented in the study using SDP were more detailed in the outer-lying
areas of the possible values for the state variable. This is caused by the discretizing of the state
variable not weighting the "most interesting" values as in SDDP. This could implicate that the
SDP algorithm would be better suited when optimizing a system for handling unexpected
IBDR-signals. Throughout the results, the IBDR-signals are seen to raise the peak values
above what was seen in the BC. This is seen to sometimes bring the peak values outside of the
detailed range when estimating the future cost implications of the demand charge, which could
cause inaccuracies.

The hour of the day which is throttled was chosen to be hour 12. This choice is based on hour
12 being a high load hour for the EVPL without the IBDR-signal in March, in addition to
giving the system the potential of being proactive and reactive. With the spot price in March
being generally high in the morning, the model is seen to often allocate the EV charging to
later in the day when the spot price is lower, reducing the amount of energy that can be moved
reactively. If the activaiton hour was at hour 11, the cases with notice time of 0 hours and 2
hours would have more hours to choose from when reallocating of displaced energy from the
throttling. Potentially, this could have reduced the amount of ENS in when using a notice time
of 0 and 2 hours.

A more realistic implementation of the throttling activation hour is to factor in when the grid
has the highest power demand. This will vary, but as the spot price is set by the market
through demand and supply, the grid is expected to be the most stressed during high spot
price hours. If the activation hour is in the morning, the cases with a notice time of 0 hours
and 2 hours would have greater flexibility in the reallocating of energy. At the same time, an
activation hour early in the morning would entail a low amount of energy throttled. The
system would be forced to act mostly reactive and the signal would be easily handled. In

                                                             74
reality, there is no restriction that the activation can only be given one time during a day.
More throttling hours throughout the day would of course only entail a more constricted
system and less flexibility.

8 Chapter

Conclusion

This thesis investigated how an electric vehicle parking lot (EVPL) can use flexible resources
to reduce the cost of operation for an office building while providing grid stability services
through an incentive-based demand response program (IBDRP). In the thesis a model is
developed using the decomposition method Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP)
and the real-time operation framework of Receding Horizon (RH). The SDDP-framework
creates future cost curves which represent the future cost of increasing the peak demand. The
future cost curves are seen to be accurate in the normal operating interval for the peak values,
but can encounter inaccuracies in the outer-lying peak values. The RH-framework is
implemented so that the operation of the EVPL can be optimized while simultaneously
handling sudden IBDRP-signals.

The model is tested for the given system with IBDRP-signals using notice times of 0, 2 and 12
hours. In the worst-case scenario with maximum throttling at hour 12 every weekday, the 12
hour notice time is proven to handle the unexpected signal quite easily, with only a 0.57% cost
increase compared to BC. It was shown through the sensitivity analysis that the marginal cost
of providing grid services through an IBDR-signal was 0,047 NOK/kW at the highest, which
would be a cheap source of flexibility for the DSO. However, the model struggles with limited
flexibility for the shorter notice times, not being able to reallocate all of the displaced energy.

An implementation of Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) is investigated to see if the system can provide
additional flexibility services in the IBDRP. The notice times of 2 and 12 hours are seen to not
be sufficiently long, as V2G is seen to constrict the system even more. The 12 hour notice time
is successful at delivering the requested power to the grid, with a 3.2% total cost increase at
maximum power delivery, compared to BC. These results imply that it is essential to have a
sufficiently long notice time if the DSO is to request any power to be exported to the grid
through an IBDRP.

Lastly, the model is tried on an existing IBDRP which provides economic incentive through a
percentage demand charge cost reduction (DCCR). The DCCR percentage varies based on the

                                                             76
notice time, and only the operational costs are considered. The IBDRP with a 12 hour notice
time results in a 1.8% reduction in total operational costs, showing that the office building
could profit from participating in the IBDRP, even in a scenario with an IBDR-signal
activation every day.

8.1 Further Work

In this thesis, the real-time RH optimization framework used deterministic input data. It
would be interesting to include a scenario picker into the model similar as to what was done in
SDDP. A weighted forecast that predicts what hours is likely to have throttle activation could
also be an interesting addition to the model. Implementing a different decomposition method
than SDDP would also be interesting for comparison purposes.
When testing the model for different IBDRP-signals, only three notice times were considered,
which were part of an already existing program. It would be interesting to perform a
sensitivity analysis on which notice times the system can handle for different parameters. It
would also be interesting to analyze the system for different activation hours. Also, the system
investigated in this thesis was modeled as an aggregated load. It could be beneficial to try to
model the system as a non aggregated EVPL. Although this would require a lot of data on
each individual EV, the benefits could be a more realistic and detailed modeling.
Finally, it would have been interesting to investigate how the marginal cost curves for
flexibility can be used as information for the DSOs in an IBDRP, to reflect the actual costs of
the flexibility measures.
