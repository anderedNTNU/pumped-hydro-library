# How the reference reviews phrase things

From `theses/lit-review-skill/measures/PHRASING.md` (first sentences by paragraph role, 3-grams shared by three or more reviews, verb and sentence-opener counts). These are the *shapes* the theses use, not sentences to reuse; the point is that a draft should sound like them, which the profiler cannot measure.

## Register
- Plain academic English, present tense for what studies show. Sentences start most often with "The", "In", "This", "However", "While", "They", "As", "To", "These", "Although", "Consequently", "Further". "However" opens one sentence in about fifteen; "While …, …" is the usual way to concede before a contrast.
- The commonest content words are the field's own: model / modelling, study, planning, horizon, uncertainty, market, flexibility, hydropower, optimization, framework, participation, reserve, stochastic, deterministic. Say "the model", "the study", "the framework", not "the paper's approach" or "the authors' contribution".
- Reporting verbs, in order of use: use / used, find / found, present, consider, investigate, apply, show, develop, compare, highlight, implement, identify, propose, assess, address, report, analyse, demonstrate, argue, emphasize, note. "Find" and "show" carry findings; "present", "propose", "develop", "apply" carry methods; "highlight", "emphasize", "argue" carry the authors' claims.
- Studies are the subject of the sentence about half the time ("Helseth et al. (2016) found …"); the other half the claim comes first and the citation follows in parentheses.

## How a paragraph of each kind starts
**CLAIM (theme claim):** a gerund or abstract noun that names the issue, then why it matters. Shapes: "Capturing the uncertainty inherent in … is …"; "Stochastic inflow is one of the most important …"; "An important element of … is …"; "Several techniques exist for …, depending on …"; "In the context of …, the literature typically distinguishes …"; "Building on this, the literature provides a rationale for …"; "The findings of the reviewed studies must be interpreted within …". Never a study name first.

**STUDY:** the study is the subject, followed by a verb of doing and the object. Shapes: "AUTHOR (year) conducted a similar study using …"; "AUTHOR (year) further argued that …"; "AUTHOR (year) demonstrated that, unlike …, …"; "A study of … examines …"; "In AUTHOR (year), a … technique for … is presented." Findings follow as "They found that …", "Their results indicate that …", "The results also suggest that …". A limitation closes as "However, the study …", "Although …, the analysis was conducted under …".

**GROUP:** a quantifier and the shared thing. Shapes: "Several studies have focused on …"; "Various approaches exist for handling …"; "Research on … often differs between … and …"; "A common solution method used across … is …"; "Other parts of the literature have focused more on …"; "Participation in … has also been studied in combination with …". Then two to four studies with "… while …", "Similarly, …", "In contrast, …", and a closing "These studies collectively highlight …, yet none …".

**COMPARE:** the dimension first. Shapes: "A key statistical property in … is …"; "The level of detailed input data … also varies across studies"; "In contrast to AUTHOR (year), this model …"; "Unlike AUTHOR (year), …". The contrast is carried by "whereas", "while", "in contrast", "the former … the latter …".

**CRITIQUE (embedded or standalone):** concede, then limit. Shapes: "While the previous studies have considered …, AUTHOR (year) …"; "Additionally, while AUTHOR (year) emphasizes …, the study overlooks …"; "Although no comparison with … was performed, …"; "It is, however, important to note that …".

**SYNTH:** a signal word, then "the literature". Shapes: "Overall, the literature emphasizes …"; "Hence, the literature appears to converge on …"; "Together, these studies point at …"; "To recap, …"; "The presented … show that there are several approaches to …, depending on …".

**GAP / contribution:** Shapes: "… from an investment perspective remains relatively unexplored in the literature"; "To the author's best knowledge, there is a research gap concerning …"; "Connecting these articles reveals that … is largely absent from the literature, as also noted by AUTHOR (year)"; "Thus, Section X reveals an opportunity to …"; "The thesis answers the research questions by contributing …"; "An overview of the discussed literature is provided through Table N".

**OPEN:** "This section presents a related literature review of …"; "The literature reviewed thus far has highlighted …"; "The aim is to provide the reader with …"; "The remainder of the literature review points out …".

## Hedging and strength
- Findings from one case: "in their case", "the results indicate", "may", "appears to", "is likely to".
- What several studies agree on: "the literature emphasizes", "it is evident that", "is widely addressed", stated without hedge.
- Claims about the field: "typically", "often", "commonly", "generally" (each about once per thousand words).
- Strength words the theses do use: crucial, significant, important, valuable, comprehensive, relevant (each a few times per chapter). Never: pivotal, paramount, nuanced, robust (more than once), seamless, landscape.

## Sentence habits worth copying
- "X is relevant/important for Y, as Z" rather than "X plays a crucial role in Y".
- "AUTHOR (year) found a gain of … compared to …, while AUTHOR (year) reported …" to put two findings side by side in one sentence.
- A consequence sentence starts with "Consequently", "Therefore", "Thus", "Accordingly", "Hence", or "This yields / This introduces / This indicates".
- A pointer to the thesis: "important for the modelling assumptions adopted in this thesis", "which is particularly relevant for …", "these findings have proved to be helpful in …".

## Section openers: the only four shapes the closest theses use (all 29 section-first sentences checked, 2026-11-10)
1. **Relevance of the topic, with a pointer back to the background chapter.** "Capturing the uncertainty inherent in … , as discussed in Section 3.1, is highly relevant in …"; "Section 3.1.5 showed the non-linear relationship between …"; "Section 3.5 underscored that … are often necessary".
2. **What the literature does or distinguishes.** "In the context of …, the literature typically distinguishes between A and B"; "Research on … often differs between …"; "In the reviewed literature there are generally two ways of …"; "Most research regarding … focuses on …"; "Building on this, the literature provides a rationale for …".
3. **A general fact about the domain, sourced or self-evident.** "Stochastic inflow is one of the most important sources of uncertainty in …, as it …"; "Inflow to a given reservoir is largely determined by …"; "All power system models involve uncertainty"; "Norwegian hydropower producers operate across energy and reserve markets …".
4. **What the section does.** "This section presents a related literature review of …"; "This chapter explores … models with characteristics similar to …"; "The literature reviewed thus far has highlighted …".

Never: a mechanism statement about our own plant ("For a pumped-storage plant, prices decide both …"), a question, "How X matters …", or a study name (one exception in 29).
