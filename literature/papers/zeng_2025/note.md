# LDES as an enabler for renewable integration (Zeng et al., 2025)

_Energies article 466, pages 1–22 (PDF page = article page)._

## Research question / purpose
A review of the role of LDES technologies in renewable integration and carbon neutrality, covering four categories: mechanical, chemical, electrochemical and thermal storage; compares performance metrics, application scenarios and prospects (abstract, p. 1; p. 2).

## Setting
- **System:** generic/global; no case study.
- **Markets:** none discussed beyond "peak shaving" and supply–demand balancing (pp. 2–3).
- **Horizon and resolution:** not applicable.

## Method
- **Model type:** narrative technology review (called "systematic" in the abstract, but no search protocol is reported).
- **How uncertainty is represented:** not applicable.
- **Solution method:** not applicable. Publication counts 2015 vs 2024 per category (p. 2).
- **Key modelling choices and assumptions:** none.

## Data / case
- Publication counts (p. 2): mechanical storage 345 → 780 articles (2015 → 2024, +125 %); chemical 6717 → 16,619 (+147 %); electrochemical 7229 → 15,686 (~doubled); thermal 969 → 2539 (+162 %).
- IRENA: renewables must grow from ~25 % (2020) to 60 % (2030) of global electricity for net zero (p. 1).

## Key findings
- (p. 2) Storage is classified by duration into short-duration and LDES; **no universally accepted definition of LDES**, but it is "generally characterized by storage durations of at least several days, with some technologies capable of seasonal energy storage" (citing [1, 3, 4]: Shahzad et al. 2024, Shan et al. 2022, Sepulveda et al. 2021). The paper does not say whether "storage duration" means discharge duration at rated power or how long energy can be held.
- (p. 3) PHES principle: pump water from lower to upper reservoir in low-demand periods, release through turbines in high-demand periods; surge chamber and transformer roles (Fig. 2).
- (p. 4) PHES ≈ **96 % of global energy storage capacity and 99 % of storage volume** (citing Blakers et al. [7] = `blakers2021`); advantages: large capacity, long lifespan, high reliability, flood control in rainy regions [8]; limits: needs water and elevation difference, unsuited to arid/flat regions; ultra-low-head PHES not yet efficient/economic [11].
- (p. 4) Outlook: "power generation efficiency of approximately 90 %, meaning about 10 % of energy is lost in a full charge–discharge cycle" — this conflates one-way generation efficiency with round-trip efficiency (Table 4, p. 18 gives ~80 % for PHES; Blakers p. 7 gives ~80 % round trip). Future directions: novel sites (high-rise buildings, artificial lakes, abandoned mines), intelligent control, LCOS-based configuration selection.
- (pp. 4–6) CAES: up to 8 MPa; D-CAES 40–50 %, A-CAES 55–75 %, I-CAES ideal > 90 %; unlike PHES not dependent on specific topography (p. 5).
- (pp. 6–9) Hydrogen: storage for months or longer; gaseous storage ~40 kg/m³ at 70 MPa, 3–4 kWh/kg compression energy; liquid hydrogen at −253 °C, boil-off ~3 %/day (p. 7). Synthetic fuels (e-methane, e-methanol, e-ammonia): high cost and efficiency gap (pp. 8–9).
- (pp. 9–12) Li-ion: up to 265 Wh/kg, 97 % efficiency, self-discharge as low as 0.03 % (p. 10); redox flow batteries: energy and power scale independently, storage from several days to months, 65–80 % efficiency, > 15,000 cycles (p. 11).
- (pp. 12–17) Thermal: ATES 67.5–87 %, TTES/PTES 45–65 %, BTES 40–60 %, molten salt loss 5–8 %/month, seasonal gravel storage; latent and thermochemical storage.
- (p. 18, Table 4) Summary: **PHES — water, gravitational potential energy, storage duration "several hours to several days", efficiency ~80 %**; CAES several hours to several days, 55–75 %; hydrogen and synthetic fuels several months or longer; LIB several hours to several days; RFB several days; thermal mostly seasonal.
- (p. 18) PHES and CAES offer significant cost and scalability advantages for large-scale renewable integration.

## Strengths
- Wide technology coverage with one comparison table (Table 4, p. 18) of media, durations and efficiencies.
- Recent (2025) and open access.

## Weaknesses / limitations
- Descriptive; no cost data, no market or system analysis, no quantitative comparison of LDES value.
- Internal inconsistencies: LDES defined as "at least several days" (p. 2) while PHES, CAES and Li-ion are listed with "several hours to several days" (p. 18); 90 % generation efficiency equated with 10 % cycle loss (p. 4); equations (2) and (3) on p. 8 are identical (methanol), ammonia synthesis missing.
- "Storage duration" is not defined, so it mixes discharge duration and storage time.
- PHES facts are second-hand (e.g. 96 %/99 % from Blakers et al.).
