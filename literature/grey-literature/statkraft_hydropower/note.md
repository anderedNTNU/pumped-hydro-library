## Data / case
- Fact bullets (top of page): **88 % of all power production in Norway is hydropower (2024, NVE)**; 15 % of world production (IEA); Statkraft produced 55 TWh of hydropower (2024) with 14,245 MW installed (2024). Hydropower is "around one-sixth" of world power production.
- § What is hydropower – how does it work: water dammed in a reservoir can be stored until needed. **"Hydropower plants without reservoirs are often called run-of-river power plants. In these cases, production is controlled by how much water flows past at any given time."** Several plants can be placed one after another, so the water's energy is used several times (a cascade).
- The steps: rain and melted snow accumulate in reservoirs high in the mountains → the water is guided down to the plant and "gains high speed" → it hits a turbine on a shaft with a generator → mechanical energy → electricity → the grid → the water continues to the sea.
- § Why important: water is stored when demand is low and used when demand is high. Controllable plants "can start and stop production at short notice, either in seconds or in a more long-term perspective over days, weeks and seasons".
- "Norway's hydropower fleet is sometimes described as Europe's renewable battery. **About 50 per cent of the reservoir capacity in Europe is located in Norway**, and Statkraft owns around half of Norway's reservoirs."
- § How does hydropower affect the price of electricity: prices are set by supply and demand. Dry years and low reservoirs raise prices. Because hydropower is controllable, it can be stored for periods of high demand, which "contributes to more even electricity prices over time". Reservoirs also reduce flood risk.
- § Environment: dams, water-level variation, regulated waterfalls, and impacts on salmon, trout and freshwater mussels.

## Key findings
- Hydropower's flexibility spans timescales from seconds to seasons. Norway holds about half of Europe's reservoir capacity.
