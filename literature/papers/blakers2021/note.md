# A review of pumped hydro energy storage (Blakers et al., 2021)

_Page numbers: the article is paginated 1–18; the downloaded PDF has an extra IOP cover page, so PDF page n = journal page n − 1._

## Research question / purpose
A topical review of PHES "technology, cost, environmental impacts and opportunities" (abstract, p. 1), motivated by (i) the large deployment of variable wind and solar, and (ii) the vast potential for low-cost **off-river (closed-loop)** PHES that does not interfere with rivers (p. 1). Written from an Australian/ANU perspective (100 % renewables).

## Setting
- **System:** global overview of hydro and PHES; Australian National Electricity Market as case study (pp. 5–6, 14).
- **Markets:** qualitative: arbitrage (buy low, sell high, p. 7), ancillary services (rapid response, inertia, black start, pp. 7–8), revenue concentration in stress periods (p. 13).
- **Horizon and resolution:** not a model paper. Cites an hourly 100 %-renewable Australia study [18] for storage requirements (p. 14).

## Method
- **Model type:** narrative review plus simple engineering/cost calculations; no optimisation model.
- **How uncertainty is represented:** not applicable.
- **Solution method:** not applicable. GIS-based global site search (616,000 sites) from the authors' earlier work [15–17] (p. 10).
- **Key modelling choices and assumptions:** LCOS example assumes 5 % real discount rate, 60-year lifetime, 180 or 360 cycles/yr, purchase price $40/MWh (p. 13); all prices in USD (p. 2).

## Data / case
- Global hydro capacity 1310 GW (2019); hydro produced 4300 TWh in 2018 = 17 % of global electricity (p. 1). China, EU, Brazil, USA largest; per capita Iceland, Norway, Bhutan, Canada, Switzerland lead (p. 1, Figs. 1–2).
- Australia: solar + wind 99 % of new capacity; 9 % (2017) → 24 % (2020) of NEM electricity; Snowy 2.0 (2 GW, 350 GWh) under construction (pp. 5–6, 8).

## Key findings
- (p. 1, abstract; p. 1 intro) **PHES ≈ 96 % of global storage power capacity and 99 % of global storage energy volume** (citing the Sandia/DOE database [3]). Batteries compete for short-term storage (minutes to hours); PHES much cheaper for large-scale storage of "several hours to weeks" (abstract), "overnight or several days" (p. 2); PHES technical lifetime 50–100 years (p. 2).
- (p. 4) For low wind/solar penetration (below about one quarter), existing generators can balance; at higher penetration storage becomes important. (p. 5) Mixing PV and wind and strong interconnection reduce storage needs.
- (p. 6) A pump-turbine can work in reverse as a pump; often combined in one unit. **Power capacity** = max rate of energy production (MW/GW); **energy** = potential energy stored in the upper reservoir (GWh); "a reservoir with 10 GWh of storage could operate with power of 1 GW for 10 h." Typical heads 100–800 m; larger head gives cheaper per-unit storage because most component costs are largely independent of head.
- (p. 7) Power = head × flow × g × generation efficiency. **Generation efficiency about 90 %; round-trip efficiency about 80 %** (20 % lost per pumping/generation cycle). Example: 100 m³/s, 570 m head, 90 % → 500 MW. Large reservoirs offer **seasonal storage** (thousands of GWh).
- (p. 7) Conventional river-based PHES: lower reservoir on a river, upper smaller; most river water passes through; pumping when prices are low, generating when high: **arbitrage**.
- (pp. 7–8) Ancillary services: PHES goes from idle to full output in **20 s to a few minutes**, provides rotational inertia when spinning and black-start capability; batteries and PHES together can replace ancillary services of fossil/nuclear plants.
- (pp. 8–9) Off-river (closed-loop) PHES: pairs of artificial reservoirs several km apart; example 2 × 100 ha, 600 m head, 20 m average depth, 90 % usable, 80 % round trip → 18 GL, **24 GWh = 1 GW for 24 h** (p. 9). Minimal flood-mitigation cost, better heads, fewer environmental costs, construction 2–3 years feasible for 10 GWh (p. 9).
- (p. 10) Global atlas: **616,000** good greenfield off-river sites (60° N–56° S), sizes 2–150 GWh, cost classes A–E; combined potential **23 million GWh**. Australia needs ~500 GWh / 25 GW for 100 % renewables (= 20 GWh and 1 GW per million people).
- (pp. 11–12) **Capital cost = energy cost ($/GWh) + power cost ($/GW)**; conflating them via a fixed storage time (e.g. 6 h) is misleading when comparing batteries (≈1 h) and PHES (e.g. 20 h for a 1 GW off-river system) (p. 12). Energy cost mainly reservoirs; doubling head or water/rock ratio approximately halves $/GWh. Power cost mainly conveyance and powerhouse; scale economies in power rating and head (p. 12).
- (p. 12) O&M plus major refurbishments after 20 and 40 years ≈ **1 % of initial capital cost per year** ≈ 20 % of annualised capital cost (60 years, 5 %). Fig. 14 (p. 13): indicative capital cost of 1 GW off-river systems; for high-quality sites the 1 GW powerhouse becomes the dominant cost.
- (p. 13) LCOS: discount rates of 1 % or 12 % roughly halve or double LCOS relative to 5 %; doubling cycles per year roughly halves LCOS. **Arbitrage income "may be highly non-uniform"**, with a large share from very high prices in occasional stress periods; ancillary-service revenue also matters, including "network insurance (maintaining full upper reservoirs over long periods)".
- (p. 14) Australian 100 % renewables study [18]: storage ≈ 1 GW and 20 GWh per million people; rule of thumb **one day (24 h) of energy consumption** as storage for a large VRE-dominated grid; spilling occasional surplus is cheaper than overbuilding storage; typical spillage 5–25 % (p. 15).
- (p. 15) Land ≈ 12 ha/GWh (400 m head, 20 m depth); water ≈ 1 GL/GWh at 400 m head; evaporation net ~350 mm/yr in Australian prospective regions. (p. 15) "Pumped hydro provides storage for hours to weeks."
- (p. 16) Storage cost to support 100 % renewables in Australia ≈ $7/MWh (all PHES); with transmission and spillage ≈ $18/MWh balancing cost. Global need with one day of storage ≈ 500 TWh and 20 TW.
- (p. 17) Conclusion: PHES and batteries are complementary, best suited for longer and shorter storage periods respectively.

## Strengths
- Clear, citable definitions of power vs energy capacity, head, efficiency and round-trip efficiency.
- Separates energy cost and power cost of storage, which maps directly onto our configuration dimensions (reservoir capacity vs pump/turbine power).
- Quantitative rules of thumb (efficiencies, O&M share, LCOS sensitivities, land and water needs).

## Weaknesses / limitations
- Advocacy for off-river PHES from the group behind the atlas; Australian sunbelt perspective (daily solar cycle), less on Nordic seasonal inflow-driven reservoirs.
- No operational or market model; revenue discussion is qualitative.
- Minor errors: gravitational constant given as "9.8 m s⁻¹" and flow "in l s⁻¹" in the power formula (p. 7), units inconsistent with the 500 MW example.
- Many numbers are from the authors' own earlier work [4, 17, 18] or web sources.
