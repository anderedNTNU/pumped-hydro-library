Abstract

The increasing share of renewable power generation and the growing need for balan-
cing services are enhancing the value of flexible generation technologies in modern
power systems. Hydropower is particularly well positioned to provide such flexibility.
However, as the opportunities for developing new hydropower plants are limited, ad-
apting to future system needs increasingly requires the modernization and upgrading
of existing facilities.

To support such investment decisions, this thesis develops a medium-term stochastic
multi-market optimization framework for hydropower producers. The framework is
designed to evaluate both the operational and economic implications of multi-market
participation, as well as the economic value of hydropower capacity upgrades. Uncer-
tainty in inflow, energy prices, and capacity market prices is explicitly represented
through a stochastic optimization framework based on Stochastic Dual Dynamic
Programming (SDDP). Using SDDP, water management policies are generated and
subsequently applied in a simulation model to obtain results. The simulation results
are then used in an NPV analysis to determine the maximum feasible investment
cost for various capacity upgrade configurations.

The framework is applied to a case study of a hydropower plant located in the NO2
bidding zone. The results indicate that participation in capacity markets signific-
antly affects reservoir management and total revenues compared to participation in
energy markets alone. Additionally, capacity market participation can provide an
economic hedge against periods with low energy prices. Furthermore, policies using
different price levels in the capacity markets seem to have minor impact on simulated
revenues. Lastly, the results show that multi-market participation, particularly in
capacity markets, can strengthen the economic rationale for capacity upgrades when
the maximum feasible investment costs are evaluated across different discount rates.

Future research may focus on several aspects depending on whether the objective is
to further improve operational planning or investment analysis. Relevant extensions
include a more robust treatment of extreme inflow events, improved representations
of market conditions and future price developments, and the incorporation of reserve
market activation. Such developments would contribute to a more comprehensive
assessment of hydropower flexibility and capacity upgrades under uncertainty.

1 Introduction

Over the past decade, large-scale deployment of Variable Renewable Energy Sources
(VRES) and and the decommissioning of coal- and nuclear-based generation have
altered European power-system dynamics (Gorenstein Dedecca et al., 2025). Simul-
taneously, Norway has become more tightly interconnected with continental Europe
through expanded cross-border transmission, making the Nordic system more ex-
posed to the same developments (Tveten et al., 2025). These trends can alter the
revenue potential across the various electricity markets, as reduced synchronous in-
ertia and more volatile net-load patterns increase the value of flexible balancing
services (Statnett, 2025c). Reservoir-based hydropower possesses several character-
istics well suited to provide such services, particularly when flexibility is required
over extended periods. This is further supported by Sch¨affer, Helseth and Korp°as
(2022), who emphasizes the crucial role of hydropower to balance fluctuations as a
consequence of increased VRES. According to Chazarra et al. (2016) hydropower
units are more flexible than other dispatchable generation technologies such as nuc-
lear and coal thermal plants, as they can change their generation from minimum to
maximum capacity in just a few seconds, with low operational costs.

   Figure 1: Market changes in the European power system (Tveten et al., 2025)

Concurrently with the changing generation mix, European power markets have un-
dergone substantial reforms, as illustrated in Figure 1. Prices for balancing- and
capacity-related products have increased, contributing to higher system costs borne
by grid operators and market actors (Tveten et al., 2025). In addition, increased
energy price volatility creates greater opportunities to capitalize on the energy mar-
kets. This strengthens the case for capacity upgrades, as higher installed capacity
enhances the ability to both exploit periods of elevated price volatility and provide
larger volumes of flexibility to capacity markets.

In January 2026, Tussa Group approved a billion-NOK investment for a 90 MW capa-
city upgrade of an existing hydropower facility in western Norway (Vangsnes, 2026).
As Norwegian regulatory frameworks largely protect unaltered watercourses while
aging assets increasingly require reinvestment and rearmament (Ekspertutvalget for
vannkraftbeskatning, 2019), the Tussa II project exemplifies a broader strategic shift
among Norwegian hydropower producers. Rather than developing new greenfield
plants, capacity expansion and modernization of existing facilities are increasingly
pursued to meet the requirements of tomorrow's power system. Consequently, mod-
els that capture multi-market participation while quantifying the profitability of
expansion measures under realistic market conditions are increasingly important.

Motivated by these developments, this thesis investigates the effects of multi market
participation in capacity markets, Intraday Market (ID) market, and Day-Ahead
(DA) market within a stochastic optimization framework. The objective is to analyze
both the profitability and the operational consequences of coordinated multi-market
participation, while also evaluating how it influence the Net Present Value (NPV)
of capacity upgrade projects and operational flexibility in hydropower dominated
power system.

Building on the project report by Bore & Brensholm (2025), there is limited literature
linking multi-market participation for hydropower producers, within a stochastic
framework, to investment decisions. This paper therefore contributes to the field
in two primary ways. First, it develops a Medium Term Hydropower Scheduling
(MTHS) that includes participation in several energy and capacity markets. Second,
the framework is applied to evaluate how multi-market participation contributes to
increased profitability of a capacity upgrade of a hydropower plant. Based on this,
the study addresses four main research questions:

No. 1. What is the economic value of Capacity Market Participation (CMP), and
how does CMP affect scheduling, water values and spill?

No. 2. How is the revenues across water management strategies affected by the
price level in the capacity markets?

No. 3. How does different capacity upgrade configurations of a hydropower plant
influence revenues and scheduling across energy and capacity markets?

No. 4. To what extent can the additional value derived from multi-market parti-
cipation support a capacity upgrade of a hydropower plant under uncertain prices?

1.1 Purpose of the thesis

Building on the presented research questions, the purpose of this thesis is to investig-
ate how a hydropower producer participating simultaneously in multiple energy and
capacity markets allocates available capacity across the different markets. Particular
emphasis is placed on understanding how coordinated multi-market participation in-
fluences operational decisions, revenue streams, and the economic value of potential
capacity upgrades for hydropower producers.

1.2 Thesis structure

To address the objectives of this thesis, the remainder of the work is structured
as follows: Section 2 introduces the electricity markets, Section 3 outlines the key
technical and economic aspects of hydropower production, and Section 4 reviews
relevant literature and modeling approaches.

Section 5 presents the methodology used to address the research questions, including
data preparation, implementation of the SDDP algorithm, the simulation frame-
work, and the NPV analysis. Section 6 describes and formalizes the multi-market
participation problem through the mathematical model presented in Section 7. Sec-
tion 8 presents the study's input data, while Section 9 validates the framework and
discusses key concepts and challenges for the subsequent analysis.

The computational results are presented in Section 10, followed by concluding re-
marks in Section 11 and Section 12 outlining potential directions for future research.

2 The power markets

Electricity markets have a complex structure designed to ensure continuous balance
between power production and consumption. To provide a fundamental understand-
ing of key concepts, this section first presents an overview of the different power
markets and how they are interconnected. It then discusses key trends and expected
future developments that are likely to shape these markets in the years ahead.

2.1 Market overview

The Norwegian power market was liberalized in 1991 with the introduction of the
DA market, marking a shift toward market-based supply and demand in order to
improve economic efficiency. However, this deregulation did not change the core
objectives of electricity trading, which remained focused on socially rational use of
resources and reducing geographical price differences (Moen & Johansen, 1992).

Since then, the markets has evolved to accommodate changes in both the supply
and demand side. The ID market was introduced in 2009, enabling trading closer to
real-time operation (Nord Pool Spot, 2009). In addition, several balancing markets
have been developed to ensure system stability, and these continue to evolve to main-
tain robustness in the power system. These markets are described in the following
sections, starting with the DA market in Section 2.2, followed by the ID market in
Section 2.3, and finally the balancing markets in Section 2.4.

2.2 Day-ahead market

The DA (spot) market is the wholesale platform where electricity supply and demand
are balanced for the coming 24 hours. Market participants submit bids specifying
quantities and prices. Transmission capacities on interconnectors are published at
10:00 CET, and bids must be submitted by 12:00 CET. Market clearing is then
conducted through the European Single Day-Ahead Coupling process, where the
Euphemia algorithm determines hourly market outcomes based on aggregated supply
and demand bids (Nord Pool, 2023a). Using a merit-order framework, generation
units are dispatched according to ascending marginal costs, and the market clearing
price is established at the intersection of supply and demand.

2.3 Intraday market

To further align supply and demand closer to real time operation, the ID market al-
lows participants to adjust their net positions after the DA market has been cleared.
Following the DA auction, the Transmission System Operator (TSO) calculates net-
work flows and publishes the transmission capacities available for ID trading. The
market operates as a bilateral trading platform, with trading conducted through the
single intraday coupling (SIDC) platform, allowing position adjustments up to one
hour before delivery (Nord Pool, 2023b, 2023c).

2.4 Balancing markets

Moving further towards the time of delivery, additional market imbalances may arise.
To cope with this, a range of balancing services exist to ensure continuously align-
ment between energy production and consumption. In Norway, Statnett holds the
system responsibility for the balancing services as the TSO, in accordance with the
regulations set by Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Administration (NVE)
(NVE, 2024a). Imbalances in the power system can occur in the form of both over-
and under-frequency events. When the system frequency rises above 50 Hz, system
balance can be achieved by increasing consumption or reducing power output to the
grid. Conversely, if the frequency falls below 50 Hz, balance can be achieved by
increasing power output to the grid or reducing the electricity consumption drawn
from the grid.

3 Hydropower

This section presents key aspects of hydropower production. It first discusses the
stochastic nature of hydropower and the concept of water value in Section 3.1, fol-
lowed by taxation and fees, and technical considerations related to hydropower plants
in Section 3.2-3.3. It then introduces the different planning horizons used in hy-
dropower scheduling in Section 3.4. Finally, it addresses the increasing need for
reinvestments and upgrades of hydropower plants in Norway in Section 3.6.

3.1 Stochasticity

Hydropower scheduling is inherently stochastic from physical, market, and regulat-
ory perspectives. Uncertain inflows and market prices interact with reservoir storage,
creating intertemporal trade-offs in production decisions. In addition, hydropower
producers providing capacity to reserve markets face uncertainty regarding the actual
level of capacity activation. Consequently, bidding strategies across different power
markets must account for this uncertainty. Finally, regulatory frameworks may af-
fect both reservoir constraints and the revenues through reservoir regulation and
tax policies, respectively. While the physical and market perspectives reflect short-
term uncertainty, the regulatory perspectives represent long-term uncertainty, which
generally allows more time to adjust operational planning frameworks. Regardless,
short-term and long-term uncertainty are components that hydropower producers
must account for.

Water value

The value of stored water in reservoir-based hydropower is driven by uncertainty in
future inflows and electricity prices. As a result, stored water has an opportunity cost
reflecting its alternative future use. Since hydropower has low marginal production
costs, effectively treating water as a free resource, alternative approaches are needed
to economically value stored water. In addition, reservoir storage provides a buffer
against shortages and potential blackouts, which can also be reflected in hydropower
producers' bidding strategies. Thus, the water value represents the value of having
an additional unit of water in the reservoir (A. Helseth, personal communication,
April 2024).

Traditionally, methods for determining the water value have been developed for
energy-only markets. However, as emphasized by Helseth et al. (2016), the inclusion
of multiple markets that consider both energy and reserve capacity challenges the
conventional approaches to calculate water values. The relevance of this challenge is
further reinforced by the increasing demand for balancing services, as highlighted in
Statnett's System Development Plan (Statnett, 2025b).

3.3 Technical elements to include in hydropower scheduling

Several physical and technical concepts are fundamental to hydropower modeling,
including storage, inflow, production characteristics, and the conversion of water's
kinetic energy into electricity. This section introduces the concepts necessary to
understand the problem formulation and the model presented in Section 7, without
assuming that the reader has extensive knowledge of hydropower systems.

Reservoirs are replenished through natural inflows, with seasonal snowmelt often
representing a major source of water storage. In regions with substantial snowfall,
this seasonal variation plays a key role in determining water availability.

Stored water can be released through turbines during periods of high electricity prices
or system demand, enabling hydropower producers to optimize generation. If reser-
voir levels become excessive, surplus water may be spilled. As water flows through
the turbine, its kinetic energy is converted into mechanical energy, which is sub-
sequently transformed into electrical energy by a generator through electromagnetic
induction (EDP, 2025). The generated electricity can then be transmitted to the
power grid for distribution to end-consumers.

The relationship between reservoir discharge and power production is inherently non-
linear, posing challenges for mathematical modeling. To capture these dynamics,
hydropower systems are commonly described by a Hydropower Production Function
(HPF), which represents the conversion of water resources into electrical energy
(Diniz & Maceira, 2008).

                 P = g · 10-3 · T (q, h) · G(P ) · q · h [MW]  (1)

The HPF in Equation 1 expresses power output as a function of gravitational accel-
eration g, turbine efficiency T , generator efficiency G, discharge q, and net head h.
The net head depends on both the elevation difference between the intake and outlet
and the reservoir level.

Additional nonlinearities arise because turbine efficiency varies with discharge and

Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

head, while generator efficiency depends on power output. As a result, incorpor-
ating the HPF directly into large-scale optimization models can be computation-
ally demanding. Consequently, simplified approximations are commonly employed,
particularly in medium- and long-term scheduling models, to ensure computational
tractability.

3.4 Planning horizons in hydropower scheduling

Building on the fundamentals of hydropower production, hydropower scheduling is
typically divided into three main planning horizons: long-term, medium-term, and
short-term scheduling. Different scheduling models are applied across these horizons
depending on the operational objectives, the level of detail required, and the timescale
of the decisions being made, as depicted in Figure 8.

 Figure 8: Planning horizons for hydropower scheduling (Bore & Brensholm, 2025)

Long-term planning
Long-term planning primarily focuses on ensuring energy availability over an exten-
ded time horizon, typically ranging from one to five years (Hammid et al., 2020).
These models are generally less detailed and often rely on aggregated system repres-
entations to reduce computational complexity, for instance by neglecting nonlinear-
ities and using a coarser temporal resolution (Ahlfors & Amelin, 2021). The primary
objective of these models is to ensure sufficient energy availability, preventing short-
sighted operational decisions from negatively affecting the long-term value of stored
water resources. This is commonly addressed by providing output to medium-term
models with water values that reflect the long-term opportunity cost and strategic
value of stored water.

                                                    12
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

Medium-term planning
Medium-term models represent the operational and strategic decisions of power pro-
ducers over a planning horizon ranging from months to a few years (Sch¨affer et al.,
2022). Compared to long-term models, these models typically involve a lower degree
of aggregation and therefore provide a more detailed representation of the hydro-
power system.

As medium-term models move closer to real-time operation, a more detailed repres-
entation of energy, capacity, and financial markets becomes increasingly important.
The primary objective of these models is to allocate water resources efficiently across
time periods, ensuring sufficient energy availability while supporting more detailed
operational decisions and improving the estimation of water values. These water
values can serve as input to short term models (Scha¨ffer et al., 2022). In addition,
the outputs from such models can serve also as valuable decision-support tools for
evaluating potential investment opportunities, which constitutes an important aspect
of this thesis.

Short-term planning
Short-term models focus on the operational decisions made by power producers when
participating in various electricity markets. In these models, a more detailed repres-
entation of both the hydropower system and the market structure becomes essential.
This may involve accounting for nonlinearities in production, water travel times
within the river system, and the sequential structure of electricity markets. In con-
trast to long-term and medium-term planning models, uncertainty is generally of less
importance in short-term models, as more accurate information becomes available
closer to real-time operations.

3.5 Decomposition techniques

In large-scale hydropower scheduling, decomposition techniques constitute tools for
reformulating complex optimization problems into computationally manageable forms.
Both medium- and long-term planning problems may be characterized by high di-
mensionality, multiple stochastic variables, and long temporal horizons, leading to a
rapid explosion in the size of the underlying state and decision spaces. Consequently,
direct solution approaches can become computationally challenging, even for moder-
ately sized systems. This challenge is further amplified by the sequential structure of
power markets and the intertemporal value of stored water, which require operational
decisions to account for both current and future system conditions.

                                                    13
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

To address these challenges, Stochastic Dynamic Programming (SDP) and Stochastic
Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP) are widely adopted in hydropower scheduling.
These methods exploit the temporal structure of the problem by decomposing it
into a sequence of smaller, stage-wise subproblems linked through state variables,
typically reservoir levels. In long-term planning, it is particularly valuable for cap-
turing intertemporal trade-offs under uncertainty, while in medium-term scheduling
it enables tractable representation of operational decisions across finer temporal res-
olutions.

In this work, an SDDP algorithm has been developed to decompose the medium-
term hydropower scheduling problem. A detailed description of the methodological
choices, including state transition modeling, cut generation, and the structure of the
forward and backward passes, is provided in Section 5.

3.6 Demand for capacity upgrades

A large share of Norway's hydropower plants was constructed between the 1950s and
1980s and is now approaching the end of its technical lifetime, increasing the need
for refurbishment and upgrades. At the same time, the growing share of VRES is in-
troducing new requirements for the future power system. Consequently, hydropower
plants must provide not only stable energy production, but also higher power out-
put during critical periods and greater ramping flexibility to support system balance
(NVE, 2025b).

Building on this trend, Statkraft has announced plans to invest approximately 27
billion NOK by 2030 in the expansion, reinforcement, and upgrading of existing hy-
dropower facilities (Statkraft, 2025). Furthermore, NVE projects that capacity up-
grades will increase annual electricity production by around 2.5 TWh and installed
capacity by approximately 5 GW in Norway (NVE, 2025c). Under these assump-
tions, the additional production corresponds to roughly 500 full-load hours annually,
equivalent to a capacity factor of approximately 5.7%. This is substantially lower
than the 2022 capacity factor of 35.7% onshore wind power in Norway (Skagerak
Energi, 2024). The relatively low utilization of the added capacity suggests that
these investments are primarily intended to enhance operational flexibility rather
than maximizing energy production.

4.2 Stochasticity

Capturing the uncertainty inherent in hydropower planning, as discussed in Sec-
tion 3.1, is highly relevant in an operations research context. According to Belsnes

                                                    16
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

et al. (2016), incorporating stochasticity can improve profit margins, reduce spillage,
and yield more robust solutions than deterministic strategies. Furthermore, un-
certainty becomes increasingly important as the planning horizon expands, which
explains why it is more prominent in medium- and long-term models, whereas short-
term models often focus on accurately representing the physical system (Helseth
et al., 2022). As this subsection demonstrates, uncertainty may arise from inflow
estimates, price forecasts, and other climate-related variables.

4.2.1 Uncertain access to inflow

Stochastic inflow is one of the most important sources of uncertainty in hydro-
power planning, as it determines the long-term availability of hydropower production.
Pereira and Pinto (1991) recognized this in their pioneering work on SDDP, where
inflow uncertainty defines the operational flexibility of hydropower plants, particu-
larly in medium- and long-term planning models. Consequently, stochastic inflow
has been widely addressed in the literature for such planning horizons, as shown in
Table 4 . In contrast, short-term models often neglect inflow uncertainty, recognized
in the work of Aasgard (2020), Faria and Fleten (2009), and Boomsma et al. (2014).
For planning horizons of 24­72 hours, such simplifications may be more acceptable,
though Belsnes et al. (2016) demonstrated how neglecting inflow uncertainty may
still represent a limitation, even in short-term models.

4.2.2 Uncertain climate conditions

Inflow to a given reservoir is largely determined by the weather conditions in the
area where the hydropower plant is located. Consequently, weather-related variables
such as wind, solar radiation, and temperature can also be treated as stochastic
variables (Sch¨affer et al., 2019; Warland & Mo, 2016). While the former study
assessed the value of flexibility under different power system mixes, the latter sought
to improve long-term hydropower planning through the SOVN model. Sch¨affer et
al. (2022) also modeled temperature implicitly through an environmental variable
used to determine whether a low-inflow period had ended. This was found to have a
limited impact on production and profitability, but a significant effect on water values
passed on to short-term models. Temperature is also closely linked to electricity
consumption patterns and therefore influences the demand curve used to determine
market-clearing prices (Idsø et al., 2024). This relationship is particularly relevant

                                                    17
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

in long-term models, where prices are derived through a market-clearing process, as
in the EMPS model (SINTEF, 2025).

4.2.3 Unknown prices and available volumes

Building on this, the literature provides a rationale for modeling prices as stochastic
variables in short- to medium-term hydropower optimization models. Most stud-
ies in this field explicitly incorporate stochastic market prices under different market
compositions, with the exception of large-scale system models. These models primar-
ily focus on minimizing system costs, as seen in Pereira and Pinto (1991) through
fuel costs and non-delivered energy metrics. Nevertheless, the explicit treatment of
stochastic up- and down-regulation prices remains limited, representing an important
research gap (Chazarra et al., 2016). In principle, uncertainty should be represented
across all relevant market prices (Belsnes et al., 2016). However, treating all prices
as independent stochastic variables may lead to a substantial increase in scenario
dimensionality.

The volume of bids submitted across the markets described in Section 2, is central
to both price formation. A producer supplying a large share of market volume
may influence prices, operating as a price-maker, whereas smaller participants are
typically price-takers. Because the DA market accounts for most traded system
volumes, a price-taker assumption is generally adopted in the literature. For other
markets, however, this assumption is less clear. Faria and Fleten (2009) applied a
price-taker model in the ID market, and Hjelmeland et al. (Hjelmeland et al., 2019)
did the same for symmetric spinning reserves, while P`erez-D`iaz et al. (2020) modeled
aFRR participation with a price-maker approach. Although the latter found limited
revenue effects, they reported up to 30% lower spillage, which is particularly relevant
for reservoirs with flood-regulation functions.

Further, the volume of submitted bids affect the risk market participants face in
terms not being activated in the balancing markets (Boomsma et al., 2014). Chaz-
arra et al. (2016) highlighted reserve-usage uncertainty as an important direction
for future research, and Aasgard (2020) demonstrated ways to represent this un-
certainty, including threshold-based formulations. Taken together, these aspects are
important both for the modeling assumptions adopted in this thesis and for a realistic
representation of the market environment.

Overall, the literature emphasizes the importance of incorporating stochasticity across

                 18
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

planning horizons to improve model realism. The most relevant uncertain variables
are typically prices and inflows. Price uncertainty is especially important in price-
taking models, whereas inflow uncertainty remains central regardless of how the price
is determined. However, the number of stochastic variables is often limited to reduce
computational complexity and avoid the curse of dimensionality.

4.2.4 Representing uncertainty

In developing optimization tools for hydropower planning, it is not sufficient merely
to acknowledge uncertainty. It must also be represented using realistic data that
preserve key statistical properties. This is commonly addressed through statistical
modeling and the analysis of different correlation structures. Further, Boomsma
et al. (2014) emphasized the importance of transparency in the construction and
treatment of realistic input data, as such assumptions may significantly influence
model outcomes. These considerations must therefore also be carefully accounted
for during the data construction process.

A key statistical property in uncertainty modeling is the correlation structure across
market prices and between prices and inflow. Helseth et al. (2017) reported no
significant correlation between DA and reserve market prices, an assumption sub-
sequently adopted by P´erez-D´iaz et al. (2020). In addition, the latter study found
no significant correlation between market prices and inflow in the Spanish power sys-
tem. In contrast, Boomsma et al. (2014) found strong correlation between spot and
balancing prices, while Gjelsvik et al. (1999) showed that spot prices are correlated
with accumulated inflow the Norwegian power system. These differing conclusions
indicate that reported correlations depend strongly on data sources and on the meth-
ods used to construct the series. Accordingly, an observed lack of correlation should
not be interpreted as evidence of no underlying empirical relationship.

Uncertainty is typically represented through scenario-tree construction. Although
Belsnes et al. (2016) noted that scenario-tree quality assessment is a research field
in its own right, the studies reviewed in this thesis share several common steps.
First, historical or model-generated time series are collected. Second, statistical fit-
ting and clustering are applied, often using methods such as K-means (P´erez-D´iaz
et al., 2020; Scha¨ffer et al., 2022). Third, discretized levels or clustered nodes are
used to build Markov chains within the selected optimization framework (Helseth et
al., 2016; P´erez-D´iaz et al., 2020). This yields conditional probability distributions
for future states that depend only on the current state, which simplifies scenario-

                                                    19
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

tree construction (Aasg°ard, 2020; Gjelsvik et al., 1999). Across the literature, both
data length and tree granularity vary substantially. Studies has used approximately
between 3­70 years of data (Helseth et al., 2017; Hjelmeland et al., 2019) and typic-
ally 5­20 nodes/discretizations (P´erez-D´iaz et al., 2020; Sch¨affer et al., 2022). While
the former is largely constrained by data availability, the latter directly affects com-
putational effort. Variation in both dimensions influences how well the scenario tree
captures plausible future outcomes. These findings have proved to be helpful in the
development of the methodology used in this thesis.

The level of detailed input data used to represent stochastic time series also var-
ies across studies. Chazarra et al. (2016) adopted hourly prices to capture short-
term operational realism, whereas Helseth et al. (2016) and Scha¨ffer et al. (2022)
used weekly average prices in medium-term stochastic models. Although this differ-
ence is partly explained by differing planning horizons, weekly averaging can smooth
intra-week variability and thereby mask opportunities for more profitable operational
strategies. Scha¨ffer et al. (2022) explicitly acknowledged this limitation and justified
it by the study's objective, which was not detailed input representation. Accordingly,
data granularity should be chosen in line with the purpose and scope of the analysis.

4.3 Hydraulic modeling

Hjelmeland et al. (2019) argued that exploiting the flexibility of hydropower in
balancing markets requires modeling inherently non-convex operational characterist-
ics, including unit commitment decisions and the generation­discharge relationship.
Their results indicate that incorporating the non-convex generation function de-
scribed in Section 3.3 is important when co-optimizing energy and reserve capacity,
as otherwise reserve capacity sales may be overestimated.

Warland and Mo (2016) further argued that modeling non-convexities is important
across all planning horizons, and that the value of such models increases as the
share of renewable generation grows. Ignoring these characteristics may lead to
suboptimal resource utilization and, ultimately, poor investment decisions. It is,
however, important to note that many Norwegian hydropower plants operate under
high-head conditions, where variations in reservoir levels have only a limited impact
on the generation function. In other hydropower systems, stronger head dependencies
may result in more pronounced non-convexities (Hjelmeland et al., 2019).

Several techniques exist for modeling the HPF, depending on factors such as the

                                                    20
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

planning horizon, temporal resolution, system size, data availability, and the object-
ives of the optimization model (Kong et al., 2020). The literature review presented
in the specialization project highlighted approaches including piecewise linear ap-
proximations with binary auxiliary variables to identify active segments, as well as
efficiency correction techniques that avoid explicit modeling of variable head. The
review further emphasized that an accurate representation of hydraulic behavior can
be crucial for model performance, although more detailed formulations often increase
computational complexity. Consequently, the choice of HPF representation involves
a trade-off that should be tailored to the purpose of the model (Bore & Brensholm,
2025).

4.4 Multi-market participation

The literature reviewed thus far has highlighted aspects that proves to be important
for multi-market optimization frameworks. These are relevant not only for short-
term operational planning, but also for long-term investment decisions. Further,
this subsection examines which combinations of energy and reserve markets have
been considered in the literature and the extent to which they have been analyzed
from an investment perspective.

The findings of the reviewed studies must be interpreted within their respective con-
texts. Direct comparisons of numerical results may be misleading due to differences
in market design, terminology, and regulatory frameworks across regions. Further-
more, case-specific findings cannot necessarily be generalized to aggregate system
levels. Finally, the results are highly dependent on the underlying input assump-
tions and data used in the analyses (Aasg°ard, 2020).

4.4.1 Market compositions

In the context of multi-market participation, the literature typically distinguishes
between coordinated and sequential bidding problems. In the coordinated approach,
decisions across multiple markets are optimized simultaneously, whereas the sequen-
tial approach requires producers to optimize for each market in succession, inspired
by the market sequence described in Section 2.4.1 (Boomsma et al., 2014). Of these
two, the coordinated bidding problem is the most commonly studied in the literature.

Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

DA and ID
Studying multi-market participation primarily focuses on the effects of incorporating
additional markets into optimization frameworks. Hydropower producers participat-
ing in both the DA and ID markets is rarely considered and has previously been found
to yield limited additional value (Faria & Fleten, 2009). In contrast, the inclusion of
reserve markets has received increasing attention.

DA and aFRR
Several studies have examined participation in both the DA market and aFRR mar-
kets, although terminology differs across regions (Chazarra et al., 2016; Helseth et al.,
2016; P´erez-D´iaz et al., 2020). Helseth et al. (2016) reported a 1.1% profit increase
for a Norwegian hydropower system over a two-year period. Their study represents a
sequential bidding approach and found that reserve market participation shifted pro-
duction towards fewer full-load hours. In contrast, Chazarra et al. (2016) considered
coordinated participation in the Spanish DA and aFRR markets and reported profit
increases of nearly 25% compared to DA-only optimization. Similarly, P´erez-D´iaz
et al. (2020) found an added value of 4.47­4.60% over a one-year horizon, with an
additional 0.12% gain when modeling the producer as a price-maker rather than a
price-taker. They report that the increased value observed in Spain exceeds similar
results from studies in Norway and Switzerland.

DA and FCR-N
Participation in the DA market has also been studied in combination with symmetric
reserves. The symmetric characteristics of FCR-N makes it interpretable to symmet-
ric reserve markets applied in the literature, although terminology varies. Helseth et
al. (2017) assessed its operational value and reported an average profit increase of
6.02% over one year. Similar to Helseth et al. (2016), reserve market participation
shifted production towards fewer full-load hours. Brekke (2016) further extended this
market composition by including a hypothetical rotational energy market. Although
no comparison with a DA-only case was performed, revenues from the balancing
market accounted for less than 1% of total revenues.

Comparing the coordinated problem with sequential bidding problem
The sequential bidding problem is rarely studied in isolation, but several studies
have compared sequential and coordinated market participation (Aasg°ard, 2020;
Boomsma et al., 2014). Boomsma et al. (2014) investigated whether hesitating
bidding strategies could be explained by differences in price levels and risk expos-
ure between the DA market and a balancing market. They found that coordinated
bidding increased annual revenues by 17.33%, compared to 14% under a sequential

                 22
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

structure. Although coordinated bidding yielded the highest value, both approaches
significantly outperformed energy-only participation. The results also suggest that
withholding capacity from the DA market may be profitable if it can be sold later in
balancing markets. According to the authors, this finding has important implications
for market design, which should incentivize behavior that supports system operation
rather than strategic capacity withholding (Boomsma et al., 2014).

Aasgard (2020) conducted a similar study using mFRR as the balancing market. Al-
though the reported added values were approximately one order of magnitude lower,
the conclusions were similar. That is, coordinated bidding outperformed sequential
bidding, and both approaches generated higher revenues than DA-only participa-
tion. Furthermore, Aasgard found that the value of coordination increases with un-
certainty, as coordinated bidding becomes more beneficial when market participants
have limited information about future balancing conditions.

4.4.2 Investment perspective

Multi-market participation from an investment perspective remains relatively un-
explored in the literature. Brekke (2016) found that revenues from a hypothetical
rotational energy market were insufficient to justify investments in synchronous con-
denser mode of hydropower plants, although the analysis was conducted under lower
reserve market prices. Helseth et al. (2017) argued that the operational value of re-
serve market participation may support future capacity upgrades or new hydropower
developments. Given the long lifetime of such investments, failing to account for fu-
ture market opportunities may expose investors to significant risk. Nevertheless,
evaluating multi-market participation through investment analysis was not included
in their study. Hence, explicitly incorporating an investment perspective into the
framework introduces an additional dimension to explore.

4.5 Solution methods to stochastic hydropower planning

The literature review is clear on the importance of including stochasticity and non-
convex behavior related to hydro power planning. To account for this, dedicated
solution methods exists and have been researched for different purposes. The goal
of this subsection is to explore the solution methods Deterministic Equivalent (DE),
SDP and SDDP to Multi-Stage Stochastic Programming (MSSP) problems.

                                                    23
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

4.5.1 Solving the deterministic equivalent

Including stochasticity in optimization frameworks often leads to the curse of dimen-
sionality (Aasg°ard, 2020). One way to mitigate this challenge is to limit the planning
horizon to a few days or a week, allowing the use of DE as a solution method. Aas-
gard (2020) and Boomsma et al. (2014) employed three-stage formulations, whereas
Faria and Fleten (2009) and Chazarra et al. (2016) used two-stage models. Despite
this difference, all studies apply DE to analyze multi-market participation. Fur-
thermore, their solution methods rely, either directly or indirectly, on Mixed Integer
Linear programming (MILP) formulations to represent unit commitment decisions
and head dependencies. Overall, Chazarra et al. (2016) demonstrate that realistic
hydropower scheduling with multi-market participation can be achieved in a DE
-framework.

4.5.2 Stochastic Dynamic Programming (SDP)

Section 3.5 underscored that for MTHS problems, decomposition strategies like SDP
and SDDP are often necessary. These frameworks is based on estimating a future
value of stored water, either by approximation (SDDP) or by Bellmans equation
(SDP) (Helseth et al., 2017). The SDP solution method is often applied to smaller
hydropower systems to enable accurate representation of non-convex relationships,
which the SDDP framework does not easily facilitate, and can be applied to a multi-
market setting (Helseth et al., 2016, 2017; P´erez-D´iaz et al., 2020; Sch¨affer et al.,
2022). Often, these non-convexities are represented using binary variables, yielding
a MILP problem in the stages (Helseth et al., 2017; Scha¨ffer et al., 2022). To handle
the non-linear head dependency, P`erez D`iaz et al. (2020) demonstrated that the
stage problems can be formulated as Mixed Integer Quatratic Programming (MIQP).
Neglecting the binary constraints might lead to an overestimation of the ability of
the hydropower system spinning reserves (P´erez-D´iaz et al., 2020).

4.5.3 Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP)

While SDP captures the sequentiality and intertemporality of stored water naturally,
its practical application is limited by the curse of dimensionality (Pereira & Pinto,
1991). Consequently, SDDP has become the state-of-the-art approach for MTHS in
hydro-dominated systems (Helseth et al., 2016; Sch¨affer et al., 2022). Pereira and

                                                    24
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

Pinto (1991) demonstrated that, unlike SDP, SDDP does not require state-space
discretization. Rather than evaluating the entire state space to determine the future
value function, the algorithm iteratively approximates it using cutting planes. This
significantly reduces the computational burden and avoids the combinatorial growth
associated with increasing the number of state variables. By applying SDDP to a
large-scale medium-term hydrothermal scheduling problem, Pereira and Pinto (1991)
demonstrated rapid convergence and established the methodological foundation for
subsequent research on stochastic hydropower scheduling.

Improving the SDDP framework
Several studies have focused on methodological extensions of the SDDP framework.
To incorporate risk preferences, Shapiro et al. (2013) introduced a worst-case ex-
pectation approach and showed that risk-averse formulations are less sensitive to
demand shocks than risk-neutral models. Helseth et al. (2022) and Helseth and Mo
(2023) improved the linkage between aggregated and detailed hydropower represent-
ations through feasibility cuts, yielding more accurate water values at the expense
of increased computational effort. Similarly, Hjelmeland et al. (2019) proposed
strengthened Benders cuts and achieved improved policy quality, though with sub-
stantially higher computational costs. Integer optimality cuts have also been intro-
duced to account for non-convexities. Abgottspon and Andersson (2013) demon-
strated an example of a multi-horizon model with inter- and intrastage dynamics,
solved using SDDP. It showed that their framework can represent complex medium-
term hydropower planning problems with considerable modeling detail while remain-
ing computationally tractable. While these studies have improved the frameworks'
applicability, none of them primarily focused on participation across multiple elec-
tricity markets.

Applying the SDDP framework to a multi-market setting
Other parts of the literature have focused more on incorporating multi-market par-
ticipation into the SDDP framework. Using a hybrid SDP/SDDP approach to co-
optimize energy and capacity sales in Norway, Helseth et al. (2015) found that the
multi-market extension had only a minor impact on computational burden, although
simplifying assumptions may have led to an overestimation of achievable profits. Ex-
tending this framework, Helseth et al. (2016) incorporated stochastic reserve prices
and sequential market clearing. A similar solution method was chosen by P´erez-D´iaz
et al. (2020) in their previously mentioned case study. In addition to applying a
hybrid SDP/SDDP approach to a multi-market setting, Brekke (2016) also considers
the added value from a capacity upgrade perspective. While SDDP has been applied
to extended capacity martet participation (CMP), the economic implications for

                                                    25
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

structural investments such as capacity upgrades remain largely unexplored through
such an framework.

4.6 Our contribution to the literature

The literature review has provided the basis for understanding how stochastic hy-
dropower planning in a multi-market setting has been studied among researchers.
It underscores the importance of accounting for stochasticity, especially in planning
problems with longer planning horizons. Multi-market participation has proven itself
to be an increasingly relevant topic of research, as they affect how long-term water
values are found which may influence the operational profit from using short-term
models. As such, this might have implications to long-term investment decisions in
upgrading and modernizing existing systems (Helseth et al., 2017). To avoid overes-
timating the value of multi-market participation, the literature review has also shown
the relevance of adressing non-convexities such as head dependency.

Helseth et al. (2016) identified a research gap related to the inclusion of balancing
markets in hydropower scheduling models. Despite considerable research efforts since
then, a gap remains in evaluating comprehensive market participation from an in-
vestment perspective, particularly in the context of hydropower modernization and
capacity upgrades. To address this gap, this thesis investigates the research ques-
tions presented in Section 1. Specifically, it evaluates the economic value of CMP,
examines how revenues are affected by different capacity price levels, and assesses
how the resulting added value may support capacity upgrades of varying sizes.

The thesis answers the research questions by contributing to the literature with
a SDDP-framework allowing stochastic representation of inflow and power market
prices. The methodology places emphasis on including a comprehensive market com-
position, consisting of the DA-, ID-, aFRR Up-, aFRR Down-, FCR-N- and FCR-
D-market. Also, the methodology stresses the importance of considering the results
from an investment perspective, as many hydropower producers today faces complex
long-term strategic decisions. In this way, the authors fills an existing research gap of

5 Methodology

In this section, the methodological framework used to evaluate the monetary value
of a capacity upgrade in the context of capacity market participation is presented.
The framework consists of five main steps, as illustrated in Figure 9. These include
preprocessing and clustering input data, formulation of a Markov chain to represent
state transitions, application of the SDDP algorithm to generate water management
strategies, hereby denoted as policies, simulation of system operation, and finally
an NPV analysis to determine the maximum feasible investment cost of a capacity
upgrade project.

                       Figure 9: Overview of the modeling framework

5.1 Clustering of inflow and DA market prices

In hydropower-dominated systems, such as the Norwegian power system, spot prices
and inflow are often correlated, as mentioned in Section 4.2.4. For this reason, spot
prices and inflow are jointly clustered on a weekly basis into three clusters using
K-means clustering. K-means is an unsupervised learning algorithm that partitions
data into K clusters by assigning each observation to the nearest centroid and iter-
atively updating the centroids to minimize within-cluster variance. This results in
clusters of data points with similar characteristics (IBM, 2024b). The median time
series from each cluster are extracted and used as model input. For each week, the
resulting DA price­inflow combinations are high-low, medium-medium and low-high
respectively. This is depicted in Figure 10.

5.2 Markov chain and representing capacity market prices

Moving between consecutive weeks, there exist a need to represent how inflow and
spot prices are evolves throughout the planning horizon. A fixed scenario tree is
insufficient for this purpose, as it cannot capture how the current state of the system

                                                    28
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

influences likely future outcomes. This motivates the use of Markov chains, identified
Section 4, where transition probabilities between states are estimated from observed
transition frequencies in the clustered data (Fewster, 2014). Short-term uncertainty
is represented by conditioning each week's possible realizations on the outcome of
the preceding week. The Markov chain logic is applied to the inflow process, while
preserving its correlation with spot prices. The resulting Markov structure is also
illustrated in Figure 10.

Representing capacity market prices
Regarding capacity market prices, a different procedure was applied. Klæboe et al.
(2013) found no significant relationship between spot prices and balancing market
prices, a result later confirmed by Backe et al. (2023). This provided a rationale for
constructing the capacity market price series independently of DA price and inflow
dynamics. It is assumed that short-term capacity market price uncertainty is similar
across the various capacity markets. Accordingly, capacity market price series were
randomly assigned to each day-ahead price­inflow trajectory as part of the data
preprocessing.

For each assigned capacity market price scenario, three outcomes were considered,
corresponding to scaling factors of 1.25, 1.00, and 0.75 applied to the base prices.
This approach captures potential variations in capacity market price levels. For
visual clarity, see Figure 10, which illustrates how price variations are represented as
possible outcomes.

5.3 SDDP algortihm

The SDDP algorithm is based on approximating the expected value function of
stochastic dynamic programming using a set of linear inequalities. As described
in Section 4.5.3, it does not require discretizations of the state space, thereby avoid-
ing the combinatorial growth in the number of states. Additionally, it requires the
underlying optimization problem to remain convex. This condition is satisfied by
the stage-wise optimization problem presented in Section 7, further motivating the
use of SDDP. A brief discussion on the convexity requirement is provided in Ap-
pendix A. Lastly, the Markov chain representation of uncertainty facilitates the
application of the algorithm, as the transition probabilities provide a structured and
computationally tractable way of modeling stochastic processes across stages.

The SDDP algorithm iterates between a forward and a backward pass until conver-
gence. In the forward pass, sample paths are generated by simulating the system
sequentially from the first to the final stage, where paths are drawn stochastically
based on the Markov transition probabilities. At each stage, the current state is ob-
served, and operational decisions are made by solving the current stage subproblem
using the set of cuts accumulated up to that point.

In the backward pass, the algorithm proceeds in reverse order back to the initial
stage. At each stage, the subsequent subproblem is solved for all possible scenario
realisations conditional on the current state. The resulting dual variables, obtained
from the interstage linking constraints, together with the optimal objective value of
the subproblem, are used to construct Benders stage cuts. Given a maximization
problem, these cuts provide approximate upper bounds on the future value function.
The applied SDDP algorithm in this thesis is formulated in Algorithm 1.

Describing the applied SDDP algorithm
Algorithm 1 describes the mathematical foundation of the SDDP algorithm in this
thesis. After preparing the input data and assigning discrete capacity market price
levels for each week, the algorithm begins with a forward pass. This is initialized in
line 4, and continues until line 16. The purpose of the forward pass is to discover the
state space and obtain values for the interstage linking variable which is used further
to generate cuts. For each week in the planning horizon, the corresponding stage
model is solved sequentially along a sampled path drawn from the Markov chain
transition probabilities, where the ending reservoir level from the previous stage is
carried forward as the initial storage condition for the next stage. This procedure
continues throughout the entire planning period until the final week, where a terminal
constraint is imposed to ensure that the reservoir storage level equals the initial
storage level.

Next, a backward pass is performed, described in line 17-29. Starting from the
second-to-last stage and moving backwards to week 0, the algorithm solves the sub-
sequent stage models for all possible combinations of spot prices and inflow real-
izations, initialized with the value of the interstage linking variable (v) from the
forward pass. Based on these solutions, the corresponding dual variables from the
interstage linking constraint () and objective value (Q) are extracted and weighted
according to both the transition probabilities (Pw) to the next stage and the prob-
ability of being in the current state in stage n (µwcurr). These weighted values are
then used to construct a Benders cut, which is stored in a cut dictionary and added
to all state models associated with the current stage. The cut is mathematical for-
mulated in (12) in Section 7. Through repeated forward and backward passes, the
algorithm gradually improves the approximation of the future value function using
the generated cuts.

A convergence criterion is not applied in this thesis, but the algorithm is instead
run for a fixed total of 50 iterations. Applying a tight convergence criterion can
improve the precision of the future value function. However, this usually comes at
an increased computational burden. As such, determining the number of iterations or
the convergence criterion is a matter of considering precision against computational
cost. A convergence criterion was initially tested, but did not have major impact on
the water management strategy.

simulation model as constraints representing the future value of stored water. The
simulation model consists of forward passes partially similar to the one in Algorithm

with annual capacity market price series. The assignment of capacity price series to
DA price and inflow trajectories is based on a new random seed, distinct from that
used in the SDDP algorithm.

6 Problem description

This section presents a detailed description of the multi-market participation problem
for a single hydro power plant, with emphasis on key limitations and decision-making
processes involved.

The operator of a reservoir-based hydropower plant must coordinate production de-
cisions across operational, tactical, and strategic horizons. The present discussion
focuses on the medium-term operational setting, in which the plant is scheduled on an
hourly basis across a full year. In this context, the operator receives reservoir inflows
and prices from the DA, ID, aFRR, FCR-N, and FCR-D markets as inputs. These
price signals and hydrological conditions evolve over the planning horizon and con-
stitute the informational basis for subsequent operational decisions. The operator's
objective is to maximize total revenues by optimally allocating energy and reserve
capacity across the markets while respecting physical and regulatory constraints.

It is assumed that the operator has fairly certain estimates for prices and inflow
for one week at the time. Hence a weekly bidding strategy must be constructed at
an hourly resolution, without having explicit knowledge of the input for the com-
ing weeks. These uncertain conditions motivates the need for quantifying the value
of storing water across weeks. The bidding strategy entails selecting the produc-
tion level and water discharge from the reservoir and distributing available capacity
between energy and capacity markets. The central decision variables include the
amount of energy produced and sold in the DA and ID markets, as well as the up-
ward and downward committed capacities in the aFRR and FCR markets. It is
assumed that the operator can submit bids for every hour simultaneously and does
not have to consider the chronological sequence of the power market described in
Section 2.

The decisions are governed by a set of physical, hydrological, and market-related
constraints. First, generation is bounded between minimum and maximum levels,
and the efficiency of the turbine depends linearly on the level of production. Second,
the reservoir level must remain within a prescribed upper and lower limit for oper-
ational, environmental, and safety reasons. Water balance dynamics link decisions
intertemporarily. While water release for electricity generation and spillage reduces
the reservoir level, inflow replenishes it, meaning that the level at any hour directly
influences the feasible operating region in subsequent hours. Third, participation in
capacity markets is contingent upon the scheduled production level. The ability to
provide upward reserve capacity is constrained by the margin between the plant's

                                                    35
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

current production and its maximum generation capacity. On the contrary, down-
ward reserve capacity is bounded by the difference between current production and
the minimum stable operating level. In both cases, reserve capacity may be activ-
ated. The possibility of market activation can be reflected by introducing additional
margins to the minimum and maximum stable operating levels of the reservoir to
ensure sufficient energy to deliver these services. Additionally, the plant size limits
the share of capacity that can be offered in both the capacity markets, while trading
in the ID market is constrained by a volume cap defined as a percentage of the total
hourly traded volume.

7 Mathematical model

This section presents the mathematical model, formulated as a stochastic linear
programming model from the perspective of a price-taking hydropower producer.
The objective of the model is to maximize operational revenues across energy and
capacity markets within weekly decision stages. Within each stage, the model is
assumed to have perfect foresight of market prices and inflow conditions. In addition,
a simple linear PQ-curve is introduced to represent the relationship between the
active power delivered to the grid and the water discharged from the reservoir.

7.1 Model assumptions and simplifications

The framework is based on several model assumptions and simplifications to fit the
purpose of the thesis. In the following, these assumptions is further elaborated on.

7.1.1 Perfect weekly price and inflow foresight

Perfect foresight within a stage is assumed in the model to ensure an appropriate tem-
poral resolution while avoiding an excessive number of stages, which would otherwise
significantly increase computational complexity. Consequently, sequential decision-
making in the power market is not represented with full temporal granularity.

7.1.2 Power output independent of head variations

Variations in hydraulic head within the reservoir are not explicitly modeled in this
study. In reality, changes in reservoir storage affect the hydraulic head, which in-
fluences the efficiency and power output of the generating unit, as presented in
Equation 1. However, to reduce model complexity, the hydraulic head is assumed to
remain constant over the planning horizon. Consequently, the relationship between
reservoir discharge and power production is modeled as linear and independent of the
reservoir level. Although this simplification neglects efficiency losses at low storage
levels and gains at high storage levels, it is considered reasonable for the purpose
of this work, where such nonlinear effects are of secondary importance compared
to inflow variability, price stochasticity, and reservoir dynamics. The resulting lin-
ear approximation is illustrated in Figure 11, where the discharge segments of the

                                                    37
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

modeled power plant are compared with a single linear relationship between water
discharge and net energy production.

                 Figure 11: PQ-segment curve vs simple linear PQ-curve

7.1.3 Energy activation

The model only considers capacity markets, leaving energy activation outside the
explicit formulation. Instead, -variables are introduced to represent the maximum
energy volumes required for the power plant to deliver the committed system ser-
vices. This ensures that sufficient energy is available in the reservoir to provide
these services. However, excluding activation neglects a potential revenue stream
for the power plant. The net revenues obtained from this would likely be affected
by the ramping costs associated with activation. To explicitly capture activation,
binary variables would be necessary to determine if a capacity bid was activated or
not, which in turn would violate the convexity requirement of the underlying op-
timization problem in the SDDP-algorithm. Additionaly, computational complexity
would have increased significantly due to binary decisions for each hour and capacity
markets over the entire planning horizon. For this reason, energy activation is kept
outside the modeling framework, leaving the calculation of the revenue impact of
activation as an area of future research.

Master's Thesis                                                     TIØ4905

7.1.4 Price taker assumption

It is assumed that the power plant operates as a price taker in both the energy and ca-
pacity markets, implying that its bidding behavior does not influence market prices.
Consequently, all prices are treated as exogenous inputs to the optimization problem,
allowing the plant to adjust production and capacity provision in response to prevail-
ing market conditions without affecting price formation. Although this assumption
neglects strategic bidding behavior and market power effects, it is considered reason-
able for individual producers operating in large and competitive markets, given that
its power plant is sufficiently small relative to the overall market.

8.4 Capacity market prices

As outlined in Section 2.4, the capacity markets have undergone several structural
changes over time, which is reflected in the price data. To establish an adequate
input foundation for the model, various construction techniques were tested on the
historical data obtained from ENTSO-E and Statnett. The goal was to generate price
series from historical data while preserving its underlying variability. This resulted
in 44 synthetic capacity market price series based on historical data from 2022­
2025. The series were further scaled into high, medium, and low variants, yielding a
total of 44 × 3 annual price series. This subsection first describes briefly the tested
construction methods and then presents the resulting capacity market prices series.

DBSCAN
First, the density-based machine learning algorithm DBSCAN was tested to cluster
weekly data, extract median time series, and subsequently construct new capacity
market price series from this. Unlike K-means, DBSCAN forms clusters based on
density rather than a predefined number of clusters. The algorithm relies the neigh-
borhood radius and the minimum number of samples required within that radius
(Deng, 2020). However, due to the limited availability of data, the method under-
performed and was ultimately not applied.

                                                    47
Master's Thesis  TIØ4905

Time series modeling - ARIMA
Time-series modeling was also explored to assess whether the statistical properties of
the historical capacity market data could be exploited to generate more representative
price forecasts. In particular, an ARIMA model, which combines auto-regressive and
moving-average components for time-series forecasting, was tested (IBM, 2024a).
However, its predictive performance on the test data was unsatisfactory, and hence
the model was not considered suitable for further use.

Rolling standard deviation
Lastly, applying a rolling standard deviation technique was tested on the historical
data. First, 2025-equivalent price series were established by replacing periodical zero
prices with the most recent positive price, adjusted by a random value drawn from a
normal distribution N (0, w,y). w,y denotes the weekly standard deviation excluding
zero prices for the corresponding week and year. To ensure non-negative prices, the
procedure was repeated up to 1000 times. If no positive value was obtained, the price
was set equal to the lowest positive price in 2025. Further, the process was repeated
with the 2025-equivalent prices, now with a rolling 24-hour standard deviation.

The establishment of 2025-equivalent prices was necessary for the aFRR market,
as the data from 2022 and 2023 contained periods with systematically zero-valued
prices. This was not considered representative for current market conditions. In
the absence of such price patterns in the FCR price data, the step of establishing
2025-equivalent prices for these markets was omitted. To account for FCR-D only
being procured between May and September in the data, all hours outside this period
were set to zero. Otherwise, the methodology was similar across markets. Based on
simple visual analysis, presented below, this method remained the most suitable to
generate price series preserving the underlying variability of the historical data.

8.6 Limitations and implications with the input data

The presented input data relies on assumptions that was necessary to enable sufficient
data. In this context, it is important to justify the decisions made and reflect on
the potential implications of these choices. A summary of the assumptions, their
rationale and implications is provided at last in Table 10.

Firstly, a combination of historical and forward-looking data was used to represent
price and inflow uncertainty. While future DA price trajectories were simulated using
historical inflows in the fundamental market model described in Section 8.1, synthetic
capacity market prices were constructed based on historical data. Consequently, the
optimization model relies on price series derived from fundamentally different under-
lying conditions, not only for ID prices, but also capacity prices. This constitutes
a limitation preventing the framework from capturing potential inter-dependencies
between markets.

Secondly, capacity market prices are assumed to remain uncorrelated with DA prices.
This is supported by the absence of a strong linear correlation between DA and ca-
pacity market prices in 2024, depicted in Figure 18. Nevertheless, some degree of
dependence is likely to exist, as capacity committed in one market may restrict par-
ticipation in others. Moreover, DA prices are determined by the marginal technology
in the merit-order curve, implying that relationships between energy and capacity
markets may not be adequately captured through simple correlation analysis alone.

     Figure 18: Correlation matrix for DA prices against capacity market prices

Reflecting on the merit-order framework and water value concept discussed in Sec-
tion 2.2 and Section 3.1, capacity market participation is associated with an oppor-
tunity cost linked to foregone energy production, especially for flexible generation
technologies such as hydropower and gas-fired power plants. Consequently, capacity
prices may become more closely related to DA prices when the water value or fuel cost
determines market clearing price. In contrast, technologies such as wind and solar

                                                    51
Master's Thesis                                          TIØ4905

power have negligible marginal production costs and therefore weaker links between
capacity market bids and DA prices may be present. As a result, the relationship
between DA and capacity market prices is likely to vary over time depending on
which technology is marginal, making it difficult to capture through a simple linear
correlation measure.

Thirdly, historical capacity price patterns from 2022­2025 are assumed to be rep-
resentative of all the synthetic capacity price series, with greater weight assigned to
the most recent years. This choice was motivated by data availability and quality
considerations, as well as the expectation that recent observations provide a more
accurate representation of current market conditions. Accordingly, 1, 8, 17, and 18
synthetic price series were generated from 2022, 2023, 2024, and 2025, respectively.
As a result, synthetic price series from 2023­2025 occur more frequently in the model
framework, leading to a greater influence on the results.

Initially, 2022 was assigned a weight similar to that of 2023. However, a visual in-
spection of the hourly capacity market prices revealed that 2022 exhibited unusually
high price levels, particularly in the aFRR market. For clarity, this is illustrated
through weekly average capacity market prices in Figure 19 and in Figure 43 in Ap-
pendix B. A complete analysis of the drivers behind this behavior is beyond the
scope of this thesis, but two likely contributing factors are the exceptionally high gas
prices observed during 2022 and the simultaneously low reservoir levels in Norway
(NVE, 2025a; Trading Economics, 2026). Consequently, it was considered reasonable
to assign less weight to synthetic price series derived from 2022, thereby reducing the
influence of this exceptional year on projected revenues in the investment analysis.
Nevertheless, this choice also highlights an important limitation of the methodology,
as future capacity market prices remain highly uncertain.

9 Validation and framework challenges

This section focuses on validating the modeling framework and highlighting key
computational challenges. In particular, it addresses aspects of the calculations that
involve methodological complexity and discusses how these may affect the robustness
of the results. Finally, it outlines how the modeling framework captures and values
the benefit of water storage.

9.1 Validating the framework with deterministic data

To validate the modeling framework, an exercise was conducted to verify that it pro-
duces consistent results under deterministic conditions. This involved running the

city market prices. The resulting outcomes were then compared with those obtained

Figure 20 shows that the weekly reservoir levels are very similar to those obtained
in the full-year deterministic model. Furthermore, Figure 21 shows that the weekly
revenues closely matches the revenues obtained in the deterministic model. Minor
variations are expected, as additional iterations of the SDDP model would be required
to obtain a more precise water management strategy, which in turn would yield the
more precise weekly revenues across the markets. Regardless of this, it was found
that the modeling framework behaved as intended.

9.2 Risks and consequences of dry years

Introducing the framework presented in this thesis faces a risk of yielding infeasible

solutions when combining the element of uncertainty with dry years. The SDDP-

algorithm constructs policies that is intended incentivize the simulation model to save

water for later stages to avoid violating the end reservoir constraint in Equation 16.

In the algorithm, the model may use artificial water, represented by the auxiliary

variable  hewm,  at  a  penalty  cost  of  10  mEUR  in  the  final  stage  to  ensure  feasibility.
                                                GWh

However, this variable is excluded from the simulation model, as the use of emergency

water would lead to unrealistic revenue estimates when evaluating the policies.

Implementing the policies that have been constructed tend to yield the desired beha-
vior in most simulations of the 44 price-weather years. However, the simulation fails
in 1981, 1996, 2002 and 2010, due to conflicting constraints between the reservoir bal-
ance and the end reservoir constraint. Figure 22 shows that the amount of emergency
water needed conflicting constraints in these years ranges from [0.2, 9.86][GW h] with
corresponding penalty cost of [2.0, 98.6][mEU R]. While the lower bound represents
only a modest share of the total cost, the upper bound cannibalizes the annual reven-
ues, demonstrating the model would be willing to incur substantial costs to satisfy a
hard constraint. However, the width of this interval is sensitive to inflow conditions,
policies and particularly the chosen penalty cost.

Figure 22 illustrates the relationship between emergency water usage and inflow
conditions. The results suggest that the need for emergency water is driven by

may underestimate the value of stored water under extreme conditions. As a res-

storage to satisfy the terminal reservoir constraint in dry years.

policies are generated. During each iteration of the backward pass, the algorithm
adds new cuts based on the reservoir levels that have been visited up to that point

structed from a limited subset of the state space. Although this process ensures
exploration of relevant reservoir levels, it does not guarantee full coverage of the
entire storage domain. Ideally, all reservoir levels would be explored to obtain an
accurate representation of the marginal water value curve. In practice, however,
the SDDP algorithm provides only an approximation, which may explain why the
marginal value does not decrease towards zero at high reservoir levels.

10 Computational study

This chapter presents the computational study conducted using the proposed mod-
eling framework in Section 5 and 7, and input data in Section 8. The purpose of
the study is to evaluate the effects of multi-market participation and the economic
implications of hydropower capacity upgrades under uncertain market conditions.
These aspects are investigated through the following research questions, previously
introduced in Section 1.

No. 1. What is the economic value of Capacity Market Participation (CMP), and
how does CMP affect scheduling, water values and spill?

No. 2. How is the revenues across water management strategies affected by the
price level in the capacity markets?

No. 3. How does different capacity upgrade configurations of a hydropower plant
influence revenues and scheduling across energy and capacity markets?

No. 4. To what extent can the additional value derived from multi-market parti-
cipation support a capacity upgrade of a hydropower plant under uncertain prices?

10.1 Computational Study Setup

The computational study focuses on the SDDP algorithm, the simulations, and NPV-
analysis presented in Section 5. While the former is necessary to generate approx-
imations of the future value function in terms of cuts, referred to as policies, the
second is necessary to test and compare the policies against each other to evaluate
their implications for a hydropower producer. The latter is included to discuss the
maximum expected feasible investment cost of a capacity upgrade.

10.1.1 SDDP Model Configuration

In the process of generating the policies, the SDDP algorithm was run for 50 itera-

10.1.2 Simulation model configuration

The next step in the computational study was to evaluate the implications of the
derived policies with respect to the research questions addressed in this thesis. Us-
ing the simulation procedure described in Section 5.4 a forward-pass was conducted
for each year in the historical weather record from 1981­2024. To avoid bias to-
wards policies, each year was randomly assigned a synthetic capacity price year with
correspondingly weekly scaled price levels.

10.2 Technical execution

The model was implemented in Python version 3.12.10 using the open-source optim-
ization modeling language Pyomo. The optimization problem was solved using Gur-
obi Optimizer version 12.0.3. All simulations were executed on a computer equipped
with an AMD Ryzen 5 5600H processor (Radeon Graphics) operating at 3.30 GHz,
and 16 GB of installed RAM. A detailed analysis of runtime was not considered
relevant for further discussion.

10.3 Results from Computational Study

The results of the computational study are presented by sequentially addressing
the four research questions posed in this thesis. The first examines the economic
value of CMP, focusing on revenues, water values, and reservoir discharge levels.
The second investigates how total revenues across policies are affected by capacity
market price levels. The third assesses how different hydropower plant capacity
upgrade configurations influence revenues across both energy and capacity markets.
The fourth evaluates the extent to which the additional value from multi-market
participation can support capacity upgrades under uncertain capacity market prices.
Finally, two additional analyses are included to examine the effect of taxes and a key
modeling assumption related to the minimum production constraint.

Discussing the underlying price assumptions
The results analyzing CMP should be interpreted in light of the underlying price

Incorporating resource rent tax
As the previous analysis does not account for taxation effects, the present analysis
extends the results by incorporating the resource rent tax through post-processing
of the model outputs. It should be emphasized that avoiding explicit incorporation
of the tax, for instance through coefficients in the objective function, would likely
affect how the model allocates capacity. More specifically, the decisions made in the
energy markets may be considered optimistic as they are overrewarded.

As outlined in Section 3.2, a comprehensive representation of all tax components
would require detailed information on plant-specific costs and deductible expenses.
Such an approach would substantially increase the complexity of the analysis and is
therefore beyond the scope of this thesis. Consequently, the tax treatment in this
study is based on a simplified representation.

11 Concluding remarks

In conclusion, this thesis has presented the relevant background on hydropower sys-
tems, modern power markets, and the growing need for flexibility in future power
systems. This implies that hydropower producers must make trade-offs when par-
ticipating across multiple markets, balancing the benefits of market participation
against the long-term value of conserving water under uncertainty in both inflows
and future prices. Additionally, the limited opportunities for developing greenfield
projects enforces Norwegian hydropower producers to consider capacity upgrades in
order to meet the requirements of the future power system.

The literature review identified several solution methods for addressing the hydro-
power scheduling problem, including the decomposition techniques SDP and SDDP.
Furthermore, a research gap was identified concerning stochastic multi-market par-
ticipation in hydropower systems, particularly in the context of evaluating invest-
ments in capacity upgrades. To address this gap, a stochastic medium-term multi-
market optimization framework was developed, incorporating detailed representa-
tions of both energy and capacity markets. The framework was based on an SDDP
algorithm, a simulation model, and an NPV analysis. Stochasticity was incorporated
in inflows as well as energy and capacity market prices. The framework was sub-
sequently applied to address operational and economic considerations, and to assess
the maximum feasible investment cost of hydropower capacity upgrades.

First, the economic value of CMP and its impact on operational considerations was
assessed. It was found that the capacity markets account for 10% more of the total
revenues during periods of high inflow and low energy prices than during periods
of low inflow and high energy prices. Hence, the capacity markets can serve as an
important hedge when hydropower producers might otherwise face low energy reven-
ues. This highlights the value of CMP, given the significant seasonal and hydrological
variability that may occur across years. Extending on this, the results showed how
attractive capacity market conditions could incentivize increased water spillage to
preserve operational flexibility and maximize participation in upwards regulating ca-
pacity markets. This was also reflected in the operational pattern, where production
was maintained at a more restrained level. However, from a socio-economic per-
spective, such spillage behavior can be undesirable, as it could incentivize short-term
market adaptation over long-term water resource preservation.

Second, it was demonstrated that the capacity market pricing policies largely resulted
in similar simulated revenues, although the low-price policy produced slightly lower

                                                    79
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

revenues than the other policies. While this may suggest that water management
strategies are relatively insensitive to capacity market price levels, the results are
influenced by the underlying price assumptions, the simulated scenario tree, and the
uncertainty intervals used to define the price levels.

Third, it was showed that energy market revenues remained relatively stable across
configurations, whereas capacity market revenues increased substantially with in-
stalled capacity. Meanwhile, the average capture price in the DA market increased
only marginally with installed capacity. This suggests that additional flexibility is
primarily monetized through capacity markets, emphasizing the growing value of hy-
dropower plants capable of meeting increasing flexibility and ramping requirements.

The investment analysis of various capacity upgrades revealed diminishing marginal
benefits from progressively larger capacity upgrades, indicating that larger invest-
ments may depend on economies of scale to remain competitive. Although capacity
upgrades can be economically attractive when CMP is included under certain dis-
count rate assumptions, their profitability remains highly sensitive to the chosen
discount rate. No dedicated analyses were conducted for the different capacity up-
grade configurations under energy-only market participation. Nevertheless, energy
revenues remain largely unchanged when CMP is excluded, as shown in Section 10.4.
This indicates that CMP is essential for economically supporting capacity upgrades,
while energy-only revenues are unlikely to support such investments.

Lastly, the supplementary analysis showed that the minimum production requirement
affects plant operation but does not significantly impact revenues. Further, the
resource rent tax significantly reduced net energy market revenues, thereby increasing
the relative attractiveness of CMP. However, since the added value from multi-
market participation is largely derived from capacity market revenues, the tax has
little influence on the estimated maximum acceptable investment cost for capacity
upgrades.

Overall, the work has shown that the SDDP algorithm is a useful tool for solv-
ing complex hydropower scheduling problems. Furthermore, identifying appropriate
input data that reflect an evolving market composition is a challenging task, requir-
ing corresponding updates to existing frameworks. Most importantly, the findings
have contributed to a deeper understanding of the interactions between hydropower
flexibility, capacity market participation, and investment decisions in increasingly
uncertain and complex power markets.

12 Future Research

Following this work, some promising directions for future research still remain. The
relevance of these directions depends largely on whether the model is intended for
operational decision-making or investment planning. A number of these research
opportunities, including risk management, market, and cost related aspects are high-
lighted in this section.

12.1 Risk management in hydropower systems

Addressing risk management in hydropower systems is highly important due the un-
certainty associated with inflow. Two ways to address this is through environmental
constraints and the effects prolonged periods of extreme inflow conditions.

Modeling environmental constraints and its impact
Environmental constraints can play a critical role in the management of hydropower
systems. These include requirements such as minimum environmental flows or min-
imum production levels, which must be strictly enforced rather than treated as soft
conditions. The inclusion of such constraints can provide new insights and signific-
antly affect the estimated hydropower production (Arvesen et al., 2025). However,
these considerations may be insufficiently represented in policy discussions and mod-
eling frameworks.

When such constraints are explicitly enforced, the feasible solution space may be re-
duced, possibly decreasing the net total revenues. This reduction can be particularly
consequential in energy systems with a high share of intermittent renewable sources
cannibalizing revenues, and hydropower playing a key role in providing flexibility.
As a result, neglecting these constraints may lead to overly optimistic estimates to
provide flexibility and its associated income.

Building on this, the reliability of the model outputs depends heavily on the model's
ability to capture challenging operating conditions. If the model is initialized with
insufficient reservoir storage or is not exposed to extreme conditions, such as pro-
longed periods of low inflow, it may produce policies that fail to adequately account
for such operating conditions. In practice, this could lead to violations of environ-
mental regulations, particularly minimum flow requirements, resulting in financial
penalties and increased operational risk for hydropower operators.

                    81
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

Future research could therefore place greater emphasis on improving model robust-
ness under extreme conditions. This may involve assigning greater weight to rare but
high-impact scenarios during policy generation or applying techniques that explicitly
account for adverse outcomes. One approach is the use of a reservoir risk curve, as
implemented by Larroyd et al. (2017), where reservoir levels are continuously eval-
uated against predefined critical storage thresholds. If these thresholds are violated,
generation must be reduced to conserve water for future periods. Another promising
approach is to explicitly incorporate the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) into the
cost-to-go function, thereby accounting for tail-risk events in the optimization pro-
cess (Larroyd et al., 2017). Both approaches promote more conservative operation
under low-storage conditions and provide structured mechanisms for risk manage-
ment. Strengthening these aspects of the modeling framework could enhance overall
system robustness and support efficient, reliable, and compliant operation even under
extreme hydrological conditions.

Modeling prolonged periods of extreme inflow
Another important direction for future work is the incorporation of long-term dy-
namics related to water scarcity or abundance. The current modeling framework
assumes inflow variability to be independent across stages as a result of the weekly
K-means clustering procedure used to construct the Markov chain. Consequently,
the persistence of extreme hydrological conditions may be underestimated. In real-
ity, however, periods of prolonged low or high inflows often occur as consecutive
sequences, leading to sustained stress or surplus in reservoir systems. Incorporating
such temporal dependencies, through scenarios with multiple consecutive realizations
of low or high inflow would likely amplify the resulting price dynamics. For instance,
extended periods of low inflow could lead to progressively higher power prices due to
tightening water availability, while sustained high inflow could drive prices consist-
ently lower as generation capacity increases. Accounting for these long-term effects
would therefore provide a new representation of market behavior and improve the
model's ability to capture the economic consequences of hydrological extremes.

12.2 Market aspects

This subsection addresses future research related to market aspects. In particular,
further improvements in the representation of capacity market activation, capacity
market saturation, 15-minute time resolution, the sequential nature of power markets,
and the incorporation of the mFRR market could enhance the framework.

Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

Reserve market activation
Modeling reserve market activation is important to capture a more comprehensive
market dynamic. However, explicit modeling may require the introduction of bin-
ary decision variables to determine if reserved capacity was activated or not. This
can impose a computational challenges within the backward pass of the SDDP-
algorithm, as the dual variables needs to be extracted to compute the Bender cuts.
Nevertheless, incorporating reserve market activation into the forward-pass simula-
tion framework is feasible and could provide valuable insights, potentially leading
to interesting analyses and discussions regarding operational strategies and market
participation. Alternatively, a simpler but effective approach to capturing activation
behavior is to conduct statistical analysis of historical reserve market activations.
This can be used to estimate activation frequencies across different reserve markets.
These estimates can then be incorporated into the framework, enabling the model
to account for activation-based revenues.

Reserve market saturation
An important counterpoint to the recent favorable price developments in reserve mar-
kets is the potential for market saturation as participation increases. As observed
in Sweden and Finland, the share of prequalified battery volumes for participation
in FCR and aFRR markets has increased significantly (Nordic TSOs, 2025). As the
number of market participants increases, the development may reduce future rev-
enue opportunities for hydropower producers already eligible for the markets. Con-
sequently, such market dynamics and associated uncertainty could be incorporated
into the framework to better capture long-term effects of increased competition.

15 minutes time resolution
As the power markets has transitioned to 15-minutes time resolution, the incorpor-
ation of this into the modeling framework represents an interesting topic for future
research. This would possibly increase computational complexity, as each variable
must be represented at a quarter level instead of a hourly time resolution.

Sequential decision making
As the power markets consist of sequential decision-making processes, previously
illustrated in Section 2.4.1, bids are submitted at different deadlines and over varying
time horizons. Therefore, the simplified weekly coordinated bidding structure applied
in this thesis does not fully capture the operational reality of market participation.
A relevant direction for future research is to develop a more detailed representation
of sequential decision-making that better reflects actual bidding timelines in modern
power markets.

                 83
Master's Thesis      TIØ4905

mFRR markets
Incorporating the mFRR markets in future research would provide a more compre-
hensive market composition. Compared to other reserve products, mFRR is charac-
terized by slower activation times and a substantially larger energy component. Thus,
activated units may be required to sustain production adjustments over longer and
more uncertain time horizons. Consequently, modeling participation in the mFRR
market introduces additional operational complexities, particularly with respect to
uncertainty surrounding activation duration and energy delivery requirements.

Furthermore, certain hydropower plants may choose to participate in either the aFRR
market or the mFRR market, rather than participating in both simultaneously. This
operational and strategic trade-off should therefore be taken into consideration when
applying the proposed modeling framework to new case studies, as plant-specific
technical capabilities and market eligibility constraints can influence the optimal
allocation of reserve capacity across markets.

12.3 Costs and degregation

Lastly, incorporating more detailed modeling of ramping costs and degradation rep-
resents a promising direction for future research. This is particularly relevant if re-
serve activation is included and the framework is intended to place greater emphasis
on operational decision-making.

A The convexity requirement in the SDDP

The goal of the SDDP algorithm is to approximate the future value function which
is found in dynamic programs, often denoted as Bellman's equation. One of the
properties with this function is that it is convex. To approximate the future value
function, the SDDP algorithm adds linear cuts on the form  + v, and the approx-
imated future value function becomes a piecewise linear convex function (Lecl`ere,
2016). When constructing these cuts, the model depends on the dual variables from
the interstage linking constraints, hence the underlying optimization model must re-
main linear. Moreover, the feasible set of the underlying optimization problem in
this thesis is convex, since any convex combination of feasible points is also feas-
ible (Boyd & Vandenberghe, 2004). This allows linear cuts to be added without
compromising the convex structure and potentially excluding optimal solutions. By
contrast, the inclusion of binary variables makes the feasible set non-convex, because
convex combinations of feasible solutions can yield intermediate values that violate
the requirement that variables must take only 0­1 values.

C Risks and consequences of dry years analysis

      Figure 44: Comparing the use of emergency water with inflow conditions

Figure 44 provides a basis for analyzing the relationship between inflow conditions
and emergency water usage. The most prominent observation is that very dry years
combined with low inflow during the final week create a need for emergency water,
as illustrated by the years 1996 and 2010. Interestingly, although 1996 was drier
than 2010 on an annual basis, it received slightly higher inflow during the final week.
As a result, the demand for emergency water was greater in 2010, suggesting that
emergency water usage is particularly sensitive to inflow conditions in the last week
of the planning horizon.

The comparison between 1985 and 2002 provides further support for this observation.
Although 1985 was drier than 2002 on an annual basis, 2002 received substantially
less inflow during the final week and consequently required emergency water, whereas

                                                    IV
Master's Thesis     TIØ4905

1985 did not. Again, this suggests that emergency water usage is driven more by
inflow conditions in the final week than by annual inflow alone.

Lastly, the comparison between 1981 and 1993 deserves attention in this analysis.
Both years exhibit low inflow during the final week, suggesting a potential need for
emergency water. However, because annual inflow is close to average in both years,
any such demand would be expected to remain limited. The differences in both
annual inflow and final-week inflow, with 1981 receiving less of both, appear sufficient
to explain why emergency water is required in 1981 but not in 1993. Consistent
with the previous observations, 1993 appears to lie close to the threshold at which
emergency water becomes necessary and may have required emergency water under
slightly drier annual inflow conditions.

As such, Figure 22 illustrates that the need for emergency water is driven by a
combination of dry years and low inflow during the final week of the planning horizon,
with the model appearing particularly sensitive to inflow in the last week.

Market (EEM) Conference after being submitted on the 6th of March. At the time
of submission, the results represented preliminary findings focusing on the economic
viability of hydropower capacity expansions under multi-market participation. The
analysis was based on the multi-stage stochastic framework developed for this thesis,
but without stochastic capacity market prices.

The paper will be available under the title Economic Assessment of Hydropower
Capacity Expansions and Multi-Market Participation Using a Multi-Stage Stochastic
Framework upon publication.
