Abstract

As developing countries navigate the complex landscape of the energy transition, they face
unique challenges such as financial constraints, infrastructural limitations, and reliance on fossil
fuels. In this context, open-source energy system models emerge as essential tools for facilitating
informed decision-making, balancing numerous factors while ensuring transparency.

This thesis focuses on addressing critical research questions related to modeling and analyzing
the Bolivian power system using and extending open-source tools. The primary objectives are
to enhance the representation of Bolivia's energy infrastructure in the PyPSA-Earth-BO model,
investigate the impact of natural gas subsidies, implement a reliability assessment tool, and
integrate the Bolivian power system into the EMPIRE model for comparative analysis.

The first study significantly improves the accuracy of the PyPSA-Earth-BO model by enhancing
the spatial representation, critically improving the input data, and implementing constraints
aimed at ensuring a more realistic output. This enables a comprehensive analysis of the impact
of natural gas subsidies on Bolivia's energy transition. The second study introduces a composite
system adequacy assessment tool to evaluate the reliability of Bolivia's future power system
leveraging Monte Carlo simulation, facilitating an investigation and discussion surrounding
the critical nodes and components of the model used to represent Bolivia in 2040. The third
study successfully integrates the Bolivian power system into the EMPIRE model, enabling a
comparative analysis between its multi-horizon stochastic programming approach and PyPSA-
Earth's single-period deterministic model.

The findings of this thesis have significant implications for energy system modeling and policy
decision-making in Bolivia and beyond. Examining the effects of fuel subsidies which succeeded
e 5 billion, shows that the potential savings from their reduction can adequately finance the
investments necessary for a sustainable energy transition. The successful implementation of
the reliability tool in PyPSA-Earth-BO provides preliminary insights into the importance of
transmission grid investments to improve the system adequacy of the modeled power system.
When comparing PyPSA-Earth-BO and EMPIRE, significant trade-offs between their modeling
approaches become evident. PyPSA-Earth-BO tends to be overly optimistic about renewable
energy potential, while EMPIRE grapples with highly approximated load flow restrictions.
PyPSA-Earth-BO provides a more detailed transmission line expansion and local generation
distribution, highlighting the advantages of its high-resolution modeling. In contrast, EMPIRE
offers a more robust investment strategy that accounts for both weather variation and weather
uncertainty, showcasing its strength in long-term strategic planning.

Sammendrag

Imens utviklingsland navigerer det komplekse landskapet i energiomstillingen, står de overfor
unike utfordringer som økonomiske begrensninger, store behov for oppgradering av infrastruktur
og et energisystem avhengig av fossile brensler. For å muliggjøre informert beslutningstaking
som kan balansere ulike faktorer samtidig som de sikrer transparens er åpnet tilgjengelige
energisystemmodeller essensielle verktøy.

Denne avhandlingen fokuserer på å adressere kritiske forskningsspørsmål relatert til modellering
og analyse av det bolivianske kraftsystemet ved bruk og utvidelse av åpent tilgjengelige modeller.
Hovedmålene er å forbedre representasjonen av Bolivias energiinfrastruktur i PyPSA-Earth-BO-
modellen, undersøke virkningen av subsidiering av fossile brensler, implementere et verktøy
for pålitelighetsvurdering og integrere det bolivianske kraftsystemet i EMPIRE-modellen for
komparativ analyse.

Den første studien forbedrer nøyaktigheten til PyPSA-Earth-BO-modellen betydelig ved å øke
oppløsningen av representasjonen av det fysiske systemet, gjøre kritiske forbedringer i dataen
som benyttes og implementere begrensninger som sikrer mer realistiske resultater. Forbedrin-
gene muliggjør en omfattende analyse av virkningen av subsidiering av fossile brensler på
Bolivias energiomstilling. Den andre studien introduserer et sammensatt verktøy for å utføre pål-
itelighetsstudier av Bolivias fremtidige kraftsystem ved hjelp av Monte Carlo simulering. Dette
fasiliterer en diskusjon rundt de kritiske komponentene i modellen som brukes til å representere
Bolivia i 2040. Den tredje studien integrerer det bolivianske kraftsystemet i EMPIRE-modellen,
som muliggjør en komparativ analyse mellom dens multi-horisont stokastiske programmerings-
metode og PyPSA-Earths deterministiske modell.

Funnene i denne avhandlingen har betydelige implikasjoner for modellering av energisystemer
og politisk beslutningstaking i Bolivia. Undersøkelsen av effektene av drivstoffsubsidier viser
at de potensielle besparelsene fra reduksjonen av disse kan finansiere de nødvendige investerin-
gene for en bærekraftig energiomstilling, når subsidene viser seg å overgå e 5 milliarder. Den
vellykkede implementeringen av pålitelighetsverktøyet i PyPSA-Earth-BO gir en første indikas-
jon på viktigheten av investeringer i strømnettet for å forbedre påliteligheten til det modellerte
kraftsystemet. Når man sammenligner PyPSA-Earth-BO og EMPIRE, blir betydelige avvein-
inger mellom deres modelleringsmetoder tydelige. PyPSA-Earth-BO har en tendens til å være
overoptimistisk om potensialet for fornybar energi, mens EMPIRE påvirkes av svært forenklede
lastflytsbegrensninger. PyPSA-Earth-BO gir en mer detaljert utvidelse av strømnettet og lokal
strømgenerering, og fremhever fordelene ved sin høyoppløste modellering. I kontrast tilbyr
EMPIRE en mer robust investeringsstrategi som tar hensyn til både variasjon og usikkerhet
knyttet til vær, og viser sin styrke i langsiktig strategisk planlegging.

Introduction

1.1 A Reliable and Sustainable Power System

In the face of an escalating climate crisis, the need for a drastic overhaul of energy systems has
never been more urgent. Rising global temperatures, more frequent extreme weather events,
and the intensifying adverse impacts of climate change underscore the necessity for immediate
action (IEA, 2023). The electricity and heat producing sector, responsible for over 40% of CO2
emissions (IEA, 2021), is a critical focus in the worldwide transition towards renewable energy
sources. However, while essential for reducing greenhouse gas emissions, this transition poses
significant challenges to the stability and reliability of power systems.

As nations strive to decarbonize their economies, electrification is a key strategy to reduce
reliance on fossil fuels and lower emissions. The increased adoption of electric vehicles and the
electrification of heating systems and industrial operations shift the energy burden to the power
sector, necessitating a robust and flexible grid to handle these new loads (IEA, 2020; IRENA,
2023).

Depleting fossil fuel reserves add another layer of urgency to the transition towards renewable
energy. Crude oil, natural gas, and coal reserves are finite and projected to last only a few more
decades at current extraction rates (Elavarasan, 2019). This finite availability, combined with
the environmental degradation associated with fossil fuel consumption, compels a move towards
sustainable alternatives. Renewable energy resources, such as solar and wind, offer not only
environmental benefits but also energy security and sustainability as they are inexhaustible and
widely available (Elavarasan, 2019).

Managing the variable nature of renewable energy sources, particularly wind and solar power,
is essential to prevent fluctuations in electricity supply that could destabilize power systems.
Integrating high shares of variable renewable energy requires enhanced system flexibility to
ensure a stable balance between electricity supply and demand (IRENA, 2023). This transition
away from conventional fossil fuel-based systems demands innovative solutions to maintain grid
stability and reliability, as well as the diversification of low-carbon power generation mixes with
technologies like hydropower and bioenergy (IEA, 2020).

Enhancing the resilience of electricity systems to withstand and quickly recover from disruptions

                                                        1
is another crucial aspect of the transition. Prolonged outages can disrupt critical infrastructure,
affect public safety, and lead to significant economic losses. Investing in resilient infrastructure
can significantly reduce the socioeconomic costs associated with power outages (IEA, 2020).

Achieving a reliable and sustainable power system is a global challenge that requires coordinated
efforts and commitments from all nations. The Paris Agreement, which aims to limit global
temperature increases to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, with efforts to limit it to
1.5°C, provides a crucial context for national efforts to transition to sustainable energy systems
(UNFCCC, 2015). As the world moves towards a low-carbon future, addressing the challenges of
integrating renewable energy sources and ensuring grid stability and resilience will be essential
for a successful and sustainable energy transition.

1.2 The Bolivian Power System and the Energy Transition

Bolivia, as a signatory to the Paris Agreement, has reaffirmed its commitment to ambitious
climate targets in alignment with global efforts to limit temperature increases. In its updated
Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) for 2021-2030, Bolivia has pledged to achieve a 79%
share of energy consumption from renewable sources by 2030. This target supports emission
reductions aligned with a trajectory to limit global warming to below 1.5°C. Specific emission
reduction targets will be detailed in Bolivia's first Biennial Transparency Report (UNDP, 2023).

Like many other developing countries, Bolivia faces significant challenges in meeting renew-
able energy targets. These challenges include the need for substantial investments, technological
advancements, and the management of rising power demands (Nyambuu and Semmler, 2020).
Factors hindering renewable energy adoption in developing nations consist of initial capital
outlays, limited access to credit, market constraints, insufficient subsidies, and pricing complex-
ities (Asante et al., 2020). Bolivia is at a crucial point in its energy transition, with abundant
renewable resources and progress in increasing their share in the energy mix. To fully realize
the potential of sustainable energy and achieve carbon neutrality in the electricity sector, it is
essential to address infrastructure development, financing, and policy frameworks.

Bolivia's energy policy, heavily reliant on gas-powered thermoelectric plants, is impacted by
concerns over depleting natural gas reserves, potentially turning Bolivia into a net importer
of gas (Azurduy and Guaculla, 2023; Hinojosa, 2023; Manzaneda, 2023). This underscores
the urgent need for investments in renewable energy sources to ensure long-term sustainability.
The government has frozen gas prices to shield households from rising costs (IEA, 2023), but
these subsidies have become increasingly costly, reaching $684 million in 2022 (Manzaneda,
2023). Given the challenging gas market, limited reserves, and global decarbonization efforts,
it is crucial for Bolivia to explore alternatives and reduce its dependence on fossil fuels (World
Bank, 2023).

1.3 Optimization Approaches and Open-Source Modeling

The importance of innovating optimization techniques and Operations Research (OR) is grow-
ing as energy systems undergo drastic changes, particularly due to the integration of renewable
energy with its variable nature (Yu et al., 2023). Ringkjøb et al. (2018) conducted a compre-
hensive review of modeling tools for energy and electricity systems with significant shares of
variable renewables, concluding that while every challenge in the energy system domain has
been addressed individually, there is still a need for a comprehensive model that can address all
these challenges simultaneously.

One of the critical contributions of OR is its ability to handle variability and uncertainty in
energy systems. To effectively capture the variable nature of VRES, high spatial and temporal
resolution is essential, as stated by Ringkjøb et al. (2018). This level of detail is necessary to
avoid overestimating potential and underestimating capacity needs and emissions. The review
by Ringkjøb et al. (2018) reveals that few models incorporate the uncertainty of VRES, with
most models being deterministic and based on historical data. However, this uncertainty can
be addressed using methods such as stochastic programming. The conclusion regarding future
developments emphasizes the importance of considering spatiotemporal variability and openness
in modeling approaches.

Given the pressing global issues of climate change, energy security, and sustainable develop-
ment, the demand for robust and transparent energy system models is more critical than ever.
Traditional modeling approaches often rely on proprietary software and restricted datasets, lim-
iting the ability of researchers and policymakers to fully understand, verify, and build upon
existing work. This lack of accessibility and openness can hinder innovation, reduce trust in
model outcomes, and create barriers to effective collaboration. In response, the open-source
energy modeling movement has emerged, advocating for principles that prioritize transparency,
collaboration, and accessibility.

The adoption of open-source approaches in energy modeling brings numerous benefits:

   1. Transparency and Reproducibility: Open-source modeling ensures that models, data,
       and results are accessible to anyone. This transparency allows for reproducibility of
       results, fostering trust and enabling verification and validation by the wider community.
       Making both the software code and the underlying data openly available is crucial to
       achieve this goal (Hilpert et al., 2018; Openmod, 2024).

   2. Collaboration and Innovation: The models of various research groups often require
       similar input data and code, leading to redundant efforts and inefficiency. By removing
       barriers to access, open-source modeling encourages collaboration among researchers,
       policymakers, and industry stakeholders. This collaborative environment accelerates
       innovation and improves the quality and robustness of models through collective input
       and peer review (EIOPA, 2023; Hilpert et al., 2018; Openmod, 2024).

   3. Cost Efficiency and Accessibility: Utilizing open-source models reduces costs associated
       with proprietary software licenses. This financial accessibility democratizes the use
       of sophisticated modeling tools, enabling a broader range of users, including those in

                                                        3
       academia, developing countries, and non-profit organizations, to participate in high-level
       analysis and decision-making (EIOPA, 2023; Openmod, 2024).

   4. Interdisciplinary Integration: Open-source energy modeling platforms facilitate the in-
       tegration of diverse disciplinary perspectives. By bringing together experts from various
       fields, open-source modeling fosters a holistic understanding and approach to complex
       energy system problems, where engineering, economics, and environmental science in-
       tersect (Hilpert et al., 2018; Openmod, 2024).

   5. Community and Capacity Building: The open-source ethos extends to community
       building and knowledge sharing. Open-source energy modeling initiatives promote the
       creation of supportive communities that provide resources, training, and workshops. This
       communal effort helps build capacity and expertise in open-source modeling practices,
       ensuring that users can effectively utilize and contribute to these tools (Openmod, 2024).

The primary motivation of this thesis is to address and develop solutions for the identified
challenges and opportunities that power system's will face. The study specifically focuses on two

The first study extends the work on the Bolivian power system implementation in PyPSA-Earth.

The main goal is to improve the overall spacial resolution of the grid, which sequentially
reveals several additional errors that need improvement. An exhaustive analysis of the impacts

Adhering to the importance of a reliable power system, the improved model representation in
the first study paves the way for an introductory implementation of composite system adequacy
assessment in PyPSA-Earth-BO for the second study, serving as an expanded part of the initial
study.

The third study builds on the validated system from the first study, facilitating the first-ever
integration of a non-European power system into EMPIRE. This integration is made possible
and efficient through open-source modeling. The study then compares the outputs of the two
models, specifically examining the model characteristics that cause differences in the results.

Problem description

This chapter defines the scope of the thesis, identifying the specific areas that will be investigated.
It presents an overview of the main research questions that guide the study.

The Bolivian power system is currently undergoing a significant transition, driven by the need
to ensure a reliable and sustainable energy future. This transition is characterized by a shift
from a heavy reliance on fossil fuels, particularly natural gas, towards a more diversified energy
mix that incorporates a higher share of renewable energy sources. However, this transition is
not without its challenges, as it requires a careful balance between meeting growing energy
demands, ensuring system reliability, and navigating complex economic and policy landscapes.

One of the key challenges facing the Bolivian power system is the country's dependence on
natural gas for electricity generation. While natural gas has provided a stable and affordable
energy source, it is a finite resource, and the government's subsidies for gas consumption have
created socioeconomic pressures and potential barriers to the adoption of renewable energy
technologies. Understanding the impact of these subsidies on the power system and exploring
strategies for their reduction is crucial for enabling a successful energy transition.

To address these challenges and support informed decision-making, energy system modeling
plays a vital role. Open-source models, in particular, offer transparency, flexibility, and the
potential for collaborative development, making them valuable tools for analyzing complex
energy systems. However, the accuracy and usefulness of these models depend on their ability
to effectively represent the unique characteristics and constraints of the energy system being
studied.

In the case of Bolivia, there is a need to enhance the representation of the country's energy
landscape within open-source models. This includes improving the accuracy of data inputs, and
capturing the spatial and temporal dynamics of energy supply and demand. By refining these
models to better reflect the realities of the Bolivian power system, more reliable insights and
projections can be generated to guide the energy transition process.

This thesis aims to address these challenges and opportunities through three interconnected
studies, each focusing on a specific aspect of modeling and analyzing the Bolivian power
system.

2.1 The Impact of Fuel Subsides in Bolivia's Energy Transition

The first study focuses on the role of natural gas subsidies in shaping Bolivia's energy future.
Natural gas has long been the backbone of the country's electricity generation, and the govern-

To understand the impact of these subsidies on the power system, this study employs the PyPSA-

With an enhanced model, the study will analyze the impact of existing natural gas subsidies

deployment, and the implications for environmental sustainability.

Furthermore, the study will evaluate various approaches to reducing natural gas subsidies and
assess their implications for the optimal energy mix. This will involve exploring scenarios with

and overall system performance.

By providing insights into the role of natural gas subsidies and the potential pathways for their
reduction, this study aims to support informed decision-making and help chart a course toward a
more sustainable and resilient energy future for Bolivia through the following research questions:

   1. How can the PyPSA-Earth-BO model be enhanced (e.g., higher spatial resolution) to more

2.2 Composite System Adequacy Assessment

As Bolivia transitions towards a power system with a higher share of renewable energy sources,
ensuring system reliability becomes increasingly important. Variable renewable energy sources,
such as wind and solar, introduce new challenges for maintaining a stable and reliable power
supply. Assessing the adequacy of the power system in the face of these challenges requires
advanced modeling tools and techniques.

The second study focuses on developing a reliability assessment tool based on the PyPSA-
Earth-BO model to analyze the composite system adequacy of the Bolivian power system.
Composite system adequacy considers both generation and transmission systems, providing a
comprehensive view of the system's ability to meet demand under various conditions.

The study will employ Monte Carlo simulation techniques to model the stochastic uncertainties
associated with outages. By simulating a large number of scenarios with different system
conditions, the new developed model will provide a probabilistic assessment of system adequacy.
Using this tool, the study will quantify the adequacy of the Bolivian power system and identify
potential reliability challenges.

Furthermore, the study will aim to identify critical components in the power system that have
a significant impact on reliability. This could include specific transmission lines or generation
units that are crucial for maintaining system stability and meeting demand. By pinpointing these
critical components, the study can provide valuable insights for system planners and operators,
guiding investments in infrastructure reinforcement and reliability enhancement measures.

The development and application of this reliability assessment tool within the PyPSA-Earth-
BO framework will contribute to the broader field of open-source energy system modeling.
It will demonstrate the potential for integrating advanced reliability analysis capabilities into
open-source models, promoting transparency and reproducibility in power system studies.

The study aims to address these research questions:

   1. How can a reliability assessment tool based on PyPSA-Earth-BO be developed to analyze
       the composite system adequacy of the Bolivian power system, considering both generation
       and transmission systems?

   2. Which critical components in the Bolivian power system have a significant impact on
       reliability, and how can identifying these components support informed decision-making
       and investment planning for system reliability enhancement?

2.3 The Bolivian Power System in a Stochastic Investment Model

While the PyPSA-Earth-BO model provides a valuable tool for analyzing the Bolivian power
system, it is important to consider alternative modeling approaches and frameworks. Each
model has its own strengths, limitations, and underlying assumptions, and comparing different
models can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the system being studied.

This study implements the Bolivian power system into the European Model for Power system
Investments with Renewable Energy (EMPIRE), a multi-horizon stochastic programming frame-
work originally designed for European power systems. The EMPIRE model offers a different
perspective on energy system modeling, incorporating stochastic optimization techniques to
capture the uncertainties associated with renewable energy sources and energy demand.

To facilitate the integration of the Bolivian power system into EMPIRE, the study will leverage
the data improvements and enhancements made in the PyPSA-Earth-BO model. This will ensure

a consistent and accurate representation of the Bolivian energy landscape across both models,
enabling a fair comparison of their results and insights.
The study will conduct a comparative analysis between the multi-horizon stochastic program-
ming approach of EMPIRE and the single-period deterministic model of PyPSA-Earth-BO.
By examining the results and outputs of both models, the study aims to identify the strengths
and limitations of each modeling framework in capturing the complexities and uncertainties of
the Bolivian power system. This analysis will consider factors such as the ability to repres-

requirements, and the insights provided by long-term investment decisions.
The comparative analysis will also explore the implications of different modeling choices on
the resulting energy mix, system costs, and environmental impacts. By understanding how

Overall, this study aims to contribute through the research questions:

   1. How can the EMPIRE model be adapted to represent the Bolivian power system, and what

   3. What are the strengths and limitations of each modeling framework in capturing the
       complexities and uncertainties of the Bolivian energy system, and how can the comparative

            As developing countries navigate the significant shifts of the energy transition, they
        face unique challenges such as financial constraints, infrastructural limitations, and heavy

        lower-resolution network. This study underscores the significance of comprehensive energy
        modeling in guiding Bolivia's transition strategy. The findings emphasize the need for a
        carefully planned approach that addresses the specific challenges and opportunities within

3.1 Introduction

In the energy domain, developing countries face the complex tasks of ensuring societal progress
and growth while contributing to the energy transition for a sustainable future (World Bank,
2023; Falcone, 2023). A country's energy system is central to sustainability, given its significant
role in driving greenhouse gas emissions (IEA, 2021). Sustainable development is thus closely
linked to the energy system, which also serves as a key enabler of welfare and economic growth
(United Nations).

Bolivia, like the rest of the world, is in the midst of transitioning its energy system. Despite being
rich in natural resources, Bolivia is one of the poorest countries in South America (World Bank,
2022). The transition involves multiple dimensions and affects various stakeholders. Bolivia's
commitment to the Paris Agreement is reflected in its future energy production plans (Ministerio
de Medio Ambiente and y Agua, 2021). The government aims to ensure universal access to
power while significantly increasing the share of renewable energy sources. Currently, Bolivia
relies heavily on Natural Gas (NG) and oil-powered plants, with significant subsidies to keep
energy costs low for citizens (IEA, 2023). However, these subsidies pose substantial challenges
to developing renewable energy and the country's overall progress due to their high cost to
the government. Additionally, Bolivia faces a looming depletion of gas reserves (Azurduy and
Guaculla, 2023; Hinojosa, 2023; Manzaneda, 2023). With limited gas reserves, high subsidy
costs, and a challenging regional gas market, Bolivia is increasingly pushed toward finding
alternative energy sources (World Bank, 2023).

The energy system is a complex structure that must continuously balance load and production.
These requirements place significant demands on energy system models intended to guide
policy. Such models must integrate power flow analysis with top-down decision-making, yet they
traditionally excel in only one of these areas. Creating a model that adequately addresses both
aspects ensures that real power flow constraints are considered while offering valuable insights for
policy aimed at optimizing an entire country's energy system. Developing these models involves
various trade-offs. Open-source models present a distinct advantage because stakeholders can
transparently see how the model represents reality and where it makes simplifying assumptions.
PyPSA-Earth was developed with this purpose in mind (PyPSA-Earth, 2023). With its ability
to incorporate both power flow analysis and investment planning with high resolution in critical
aspects of energy modeling, PyPSA can provide valuable insights into the complex challenges
facing Bolivia.

In this study, PyPSA-Earth, adapted for Bolivia as PyPSA-Earth-BO, will be further developed
to better represent the reality of Bolivia's energy system. This enhancement aims to model the
development of the energy system until 2040. The scope is limited to the electricity system
connected to the main grid, which currently accounts for 91% of the total demand (Cheng et al.,
2022). The analysis will encompass several scenarios, modeling different trajectories for both
the subsidies of natural gas and oil, and for the sustainability of the system through reductions
in emissions.

3.2 Literature Review and Contributions

3.2.1 Energy System Modeling

Fodstad et al. (2022) summarize four key challenges that remain in the field of energy modeling.
These challenges include ensuring sufficient time and space resolution, handling uncertainty,
modeling Multi-Energy Systems (MES), and accounting for the dynamics of the energy transition
and energy behaviors. The increasing presence of Intermittent Renewable Energy Sources
(IRES) demands both high spatial and temporal resolution in models. Since IRES vary greatly
by location, models must accurately represent the real distances between energy sources and the
corresponding demand. Additionally, IRES fluctuate over short periods, introducing inherent
uncertainty regarding when and how much energy will be produced at any given time. Modeling
this uncertainty is crucial for providing results that can guide energy policy, as a robust system
must account for and plan around this variability. With the transition to a more sustainable
energy system, more work is needed to capture the interdependencies between different energy
sectors through MES modeling. Finally, accurately predicting future energy demand and the
transition dynamics of the energy system is a fundamental task.

Prina et al. (2020) reviewed state-of-the-art bottom-up energy models and identified the resolu-
tion of four categories similar to those previously mentioned as the main challenge. Although
many models achieve high resolution in one or more aspects, they emphasize that the ultimate
challenge is to develop models that provide high resolution in all four areas simultaneously.

In addition to the four categories, transparency in energy modeling is crucial to ensure that
models can effectively guide policy (Prina et al., 2020; Pfenninger et al., 2014). With black-box
models, the simplifying assumptions behind the model are not openly accessible, making it
impossible to make informed decisions based on the results.

Several studies focus on how spatial resolution is necessary to accurately model the dynamics
of renewable energy sources. Prina et al. (2017) find that in their study of the Italian energy
system, a single-node study overestimates the percentage of renewables by around 6.6 %. This
is due to underestimating curtailments. The gap is mostly present when studying systems with
a high percentage of renewable energy sources, due to the increasing importance of accurately
modeling transmission networks when the potential for generating power is highly local. Simoes
et al. (2017) assess how spatial resolution impacts the model of the Austrian energy system, and
find that the model provides different results in the cases where the optimal capacity of wind
and solar is close to the threshold capacity. In the cases where wind and solar are the clear
cost-effective solution or no major regional climatic differences are present, the number of nodes
does not induce large differences in the results.

as presented by Ritzkowsky (2023). The aims of the studies are also varied. An overview of the

models used for Bolivian case studies. Even though all models used to represent the Bolivian

attains high resolution in all.

resolution, leaving some details out.

3.2.2 Gas Situation in Bolivia

Bolivia's energy policy has a profound impact on its electricity sector, especially concerning
fossil fuels. Currently, the National Interconnected System (SIN) heavily relies on gas-powered
thermoelectric plants. Bolivia plays a critical role as a gas exporter to Brazil and Argentina
(IEA, 2023). However, concerns have arisen about the depletion of NG reserves, as indicated
by recent reports (Azurduy and Guaculla, 2023; Hinojosa, 2023; Manzaneda, 2023). Disagree-
ments persist regarding the timeline for Bolivia to become a net importer of gas, necessitating a
transition to alternative energy sources. This situation underscores the importance of accelerat-
ing investments in renewable energies such as solar, wind, and hydroelectric power to diversify
the energy matrix and ensure long-term sustainability (Azurduy and Guaculla, 2023).

Subsidies for oil and gas are a significant component of Bolivia's energy policy and have
substantial budgetary implications. To protect households from rising prices, Bolivia has frozen
gas prices for several years (IEA, 2023). However, these subsidies, while stabilizing prices, have
become increasingly costly. Sources have reported that fossil fuel subsidies totaled US$684
million in 2022 (Manzaneda, 2023). The combination of a challenging gas market in South
America, limited gas reserves, and global efforts to decarbonize the energy sector all advocate
for exploring alternatives to reduce dependence on fossil fuels (World Bank, 2023).

3.2.3 Research Gap Identification and Contributions

The discussion above underscores the necessity of developing and analyzing transition traject-
ories for the Bolivian electricity system, particularly to study the impact of fuel subsidies on
its evolution. To achieve this, an accurate investment planning tool is essential, as discussed in
Section 3.2.1. This study advances the development of PyPSA-Earth-BO, ensuring transparency
through its open-source nature, thereby effectively guiding policy decisions. Previous studies on
Bolivia using the current model identified several areas for enhancement, including improving
grid data for a more accurate representation of the energy system (Ritzkowsky, 2023), assessing
the impact of current fuel subsidies on the adoption of renewable energy sources (Ritzkowsky,

contributions of this study are as follows:

    · Tailoring PyPSA-Earth for modeling the Bolivian Electricity System (PyPSA-Earth-BO)
       by enhancing both the input data and the model. This includes improving transmission

3.3 Methodology

3.3.1 Modeling Tool: PyPSA-Earth

PyPSA-Earth serves as an open-source global energy system model, presently focusing on
electricity systems, is designed to facilitate operational studies, as well as combined analyses of
generation, storage, and transmission expansion. The tool automates key phases in energy system
modeling, covering data extraction, preprocessing, and the development of the optimization
model. The overarching goal is to offer a versatile model applicable to power systems across
various countries globally.

For this study, the PyPSA-Earth model version 3.0.0, dated January 2024 is utilized. A detailed
mathematical model formulation for PyPSA is comprehensively documented by Brown et al.
(2018). Specifics related to PyPSA-Earth are available in their regularly updated official GitHub
documentation. Given the public accessibility of this documentation, the discussion in this
section is brief and delves into the workflow, model structure, and objective function.

The sequential stages of the model are shown in Figure 3.1. Automation of the entire process is
achieved through the utilization of the Snakemake software tool, streamlining the execution of
all six steps.

PyPSA-Earth is a linear programming model designed for the comprehensive analysis of elec-
tricity systems. The model represents the electricity system as a network consisting of nodes
and arcs, modeling the flow of energy through arcs that connect nodes. Energy is produced at
nodes that contain generators and stores or storage units where the energy level has increased
after a simulation run. Energy is consumed at nodes that contain loads and stores or storage
units with a decrease in energy level after the simulation. The nodes are considered as buses
that connect these generators, stores, storage units, and loads at the given location. The arcs
can either be passive or active. Passive arcs represent lines and transformers that follow the
imposed physical laws ensuring energy balance. Alternatively, the arcs represent active branches
called links, that can be controlled and chosen through optimization. The output of the model

3.4 Data

3.4.1 PyPSA-Earth: Input Data

To become an "open-source global energy system model with high-resolution data" (Parzen
et al., 2023), PyPSA-Earth primarily relies on open-source datasets. The different datasets used
by the model are chosen carefully to keep a common global data reference and are presented in
the documentation (PyPSA-Earth, 2023). These sources ensure that PyPSA-Earth can be used
to model any country, but is not necessarily the most accurate source available for specific cases.
The accuracy and precision of the model's results are closely tied to the quality, resolution, and
availability of the input data. It is crucial to understand that the model's output is only as reliable
as its inputs; any limitations in these data sources will directly influence the reliability of the
model. Therefore, when there is more accurate data available, the data input should be based on
those sources.

PyPSA-Earth does facilitate the inclusion of custom data and features when the default im-
plementation has shortcomings. This function enhances the adaptability for modeling realistic
systems.

3.4.2 PyPSA-Earth-BO: Adjusting PyPSA-Earth Input Data

Building on the findings from prior studies (Fernandez Vazquez et al., 2022; Ritzkowsky, 2023)
and validation efforts, it becomes clear that in the context of Bolivia, as is the case with many
developing countries, there is a significant portion of data that is outdated. To more accurately
reflect Bolivia's energy landscape, this section focuses on modifications made to improve the
current adaptation.

Configuration of a complete network

A significant contribution to improving the line data has been made through the work of
Fernandez Vazquez et al. (2023). Nonetheless, the ongoing need for data updates is addressed
by the challenge of network isolation, as depicted in Figure 3.3.

The line data, downloaded from OpenStreetMap (OSM), contains noticeable gaps in the network
configuration. In the clustering process of the workflow, the transmission lines are reduced to
straight lines. Therefore, the precise line strings mandated by OSM are not essential for the
modeling in PyPSA-Earth. By analyzing the line and bus data in the report from the Comité
Nacional de Despacho de Carga (CNDC, 2022) and replacing it with the OSM data, it is possible
to map out Bolivia's transmission system more accurately and thus remove the isolation. In line
with the emphasis on open-source data, this report is publicly accessible.

To fully represent all lines and buses, adjustments to the configuration file of the model are
necessary. A key feature in the workflow, remove_stubs, is tasked with eliminating dead-
ends in the network. Disabling this feature, however, allows the inclusion of 101 buses, up
from 87, markedly improving the spatial resolution and the detailed depiction of the network.

Each energy carrier requires a foundational "shell" to allow the model to expand capacity
during optimization. Solar and wind power plants inherently possess such a shell at each
node by default, though only the actual plants have installed capacity. For other technologies,
these foundational structures must be manually incorporated into the custom file. To enable
the model to expand geothermal power plants, shells for potential plants are manually added
based on ongoing plans for implementing geothermal plants in Bolivia, specifically the ongoing
pilot project "Laguna Colorada" (GeoEnergy, 2022). Currently, PyPSA-Earth lacks support
for extendable hydropower plants due to their distinct characteristics regarding storage. As
an interim measure, extendable shells are manually incorporated into the network file prior to
solving. These added potential hydro expansions draw inspiration from two ongoing projects:
Miguillas and Ivirizu (ENDE, b,a).

Finally, to complete a multi-year optimization of the energy system, an algorithm is included
to ensure that power plants exceeding their lifetime during a given year are decommissioned.
In summary, these changes allow the model to make investment decisions involving all present
technologies in Bolivia.

Modify load distribution

The enhanced network resolution in PyPSA-Earth-BO uncovers significant discrepancies in the
distributed load, compared to reported figures (CNDC, 2022). To determine the distributed load,
PyPSA-Earth-BO employs population and GDP data for load distribution calculations. Despite
the validation of these datasets, the resulting demand profiles exhibit substantial inaccuracies.
To rectify this issue, a careful manual adjustment process is employed. This process involves
aligning the model's predicted load variations with actual monthly consumption data for each
node, as documented in CNDC (2022). The actual consumption figures are then distributed
hourly throughout the respective month, based on the daily variations in the original demand
data from PyPSA as CNDC only provides the monthly total. This ensures that the revised load
distribution more accurately reflects PyPSA-Earth-BO's seasonal demand forecasts.

Below in Figure 3.5, the distribution of load before and after the adjustment can be compared
through the size of the nodes. The initial indication of an output error was identified through the
unusually high load observed in the sparsely populated north-western node, seen in Figure 3.5a.
Moreover, the figures illustrate the concentration of load in major urban centers. The line
thickness denotes the capacity of each connection. The change in thickness is a result of the
changes described for configuring the complete network. The drastic changes underscore the
critical need for precise transmission data to reflect the network's efficiency and reliability.

However, the study faces a challenge due to the geographical clustering of several hydropower

1352 hours for Bolivian reservoirs. This adjustment reflects a more accurate potential for energy
storage within the country's hydroelectric system, providing a better foundation for analyzing
and planning Bolivia's hydroelectric capacity.

Capacity factor for biomass

Unlike wind and solar technologies, where capacity factors can be derived from weather data,
estimating capacity factors for other technologies pose greater challenges. A notable discrepancy
is observed with biomass being over-represented. To address this issue, an hourly capacity factor
of 0.72 is introduced through Constraint 3.3 in Section 3.3.2, aiming to more accurately mirror
the actual system performance. The selection of the capacity factor is based on a case study
specific to Bolivia, as detailed in Morató et al. (2020).

Boundaries on capacity installation

PyPSA-Earth-BO operates on a yearly basis, with each year considered in isolation. Con-
sequently, significant investment decisions are often made abruptly. To guide the model towards
more sensible investment decisions, a global constraint is introduced for all energy carriers. This
decision is informed by an analysis of historical expansion trends detailed in CNDC (2022),
which evaluated the cumulative investment spread over multiple years. Considering the anticip-
ated system changes, the annual addition of new investments is capped at 400 MW. An exception
is made for geothermal capacity, which is capped at 100 MW annually due to uncertainties in
identifying optimal drilling locations and limited expertise.
Similar to the challenges faced by solar and wind energy due to natural weather variations,
biomass and geothermal power generation in Bolivia encounter constraints stemming from their
energy potential. The biomass potential in Bolivia is estimated at 840 MW of installed capacity
(Morato et al., 2019), while geothermal power is estimated to range between 510 and 1260
MW (Lahsen et al., 2015). Due to uncertainties surrounding geothermal energy production, a
conservative approach is adopted, setting the maximum potential at 510 MW.

3.5.1 Transition Pathways for Bolivia's Electricity System

To explore various future scenarios for Bolivia's electricity system, the updated version of
PyPSA-Earth-BO is used to examine six different combinations of policies. An overview
of the scenarios and their relationships is shown in Figure 3.6. The scenarios vary along two
dimensions: the type of subsidy policy for fossil fuels and the imposition of emission restrictions.
All scenarios are derived from the reference scenario, represented as scenario 1.1, BASE. In this
scenario, the current fuel subsidies in the country continue throughout the entire horizon, and no
restrictions are imposed on CO2 emissions. Scenarios 2.1 and 3.1 model different trajectories
for changes in fuel subsidies, either through a sudden removal of subsidies in scenario Sudden
Cost (SC), or a gradual linear reduction in scenario Gradual Cost (GC). Scenarios 1.2, 2.2,
and 3.2 add the emission constraint to the respective scenarios for changes in fuel subsidies.
The pathway for emission reduction is the same in these three scenarios, representing Net Zero
Emissions (NZE) by 2050 (IPCC, 2022).

3.5.2 Gas Price Projection

With the current gas subsidies policy in Bolivia, the recent increase in gas prices has resulted
in higher costs for the country (IEA, 2023). However, this policy ensures that consumers who
are highly dependent on gas for heating have experienced constant prices over several years.
As projections for gas reserves in Bolivia indicate that the country will become a net importer
in a few years, different scenarios for the future of gas subsidies are included in this study to
highlight various trajectories and their resulting total costs. In the BASE scenarios, the constant
fuel price through subsidized fuel continues as it has in past years. Alternatively, the SC
scenarios represent a situation where the increased economic burden of gas subsidies forces the
country to suddenly remove all subsidies. Finally, the GC scenarios depict a more sustainable
change in subsidy policy, with subsidies gradually decreasing over fifteen years, leading to their
complete removal by 2038.

Utilizing the advanced PyPSA-Earth-BO model, this study examines diverse scenarios to map

highlights the shortcomings of lower-spatial-resolution models, particularly in evaluating re-

4.1 Introduction

The importance of a power system that reliably provides electricity cannot be overstated. Ac-
cording to the (IEA, 2020), as the world transitions to energy systems aligned with the Paris
Agreement, electricity is projected to constitute 31% of final energy consumption by 2040. This
shift underscores the growing demand for and dependence on electricity across various sectors,
emphasizing the need for a robust generation infrastructure.

In recent years, the power system has faced increasing stress alongside rising demand. Structural
changes such as the integration of large shares of Variable Renewable Energy Sources (VRES),
the evolution of energy supply into competitive markets, and the aging of infrastructure com-
ponents have contributed to this strain (Tuinema et al., 2020; IEA, 2020). The transition away
from fossil fuel-dominated systems toward those with significant VRES components presents
considerable structural challenges, placing greater pressure on system planners to ensure reliable
supply throughout this transformation.

Composite system adequacy analysis is a technique aimed at illuminating whether the power
systems can incorporate these developments without increasing the risk of unreliable power
supply. By identifying potential weaknesses and assessing the likelihood of different failure
scenarios, system planners can implement strategies to enhance reliability and mitigate risks.

While a fully reliable power system is unattainable (Abunima et al., 2018), it is crucial to manage
levels of unreliability to avoid societal costs that outweigh the expenses incurred to mitigate
them. Striking an optimal balance between reliability and cost involves understanding the risks
and impacts of power outages, and investing in adequately robust infrastructure based on this
understanding. As the energy landscape continues to evolve, ongoing adaptation and innovation
are essential to maintaining a power system that meets societal needs while minimizing the risks
and costs associated with power outages and disruptions.

While achieving a fully reliable power system is challenging, the pursuit of improved reliability
through composite system adequacy analysis and other approaches is essential. By embracing
the complexities of the energy transition and proactively addressing the associated risks, the
power system can be built to support sustainable development and ensure a reliable supply of
electricity for all sectors of society.

4.2 Background and Contribution

The security of electricity supply centers on ensuring uninterrupted access to electricity for
consumers (IEA, 2020). Tuinema et al. (2020) distinguish between system adequacy and system
security in reliability analysis of power systems. While adequacy pertains to the system's ability
to meet demand, security concerns the system's capability to respond to various events. The
International Energy Agency (IEA) further defines resilience as the ability of system components
to recover from or adapt to shocks. Given that reliability is subject to threats varying in both
impact and probability, it is informing to clearly define the type of reliability analysis conducted in
a study. For calculating power system reliability, systems can be divided into three hierarchical
levels based on the components considered (Abunima et al., 2018). Level I includes only

                                                       45
generation, while level II incorporates both generation and transmission, known as composite
systems. Level III additionally considers distribution facilities.

Consumers expect a continuous and reliable electricity supply. When this expectation is unmet,
a significant portion of society is impacted. Power outages are categorized based on their impact
and causes. During instances when the power system cannot meet demand, load shedding is
implemented, which involves deliberately disconnecting parts of the system (IEA, 2020). This
allows power system operators to prioritize essential loads. More severe are blackouts, where
large areas are left without electricity for extended periods (Cepin, 2011). The consequences
of blackouts can be severe, including transportation disruptions, food safety issues, difficulties
in maintaining law and order, and economic losses (IEA, 2020). There is always a trade-off
between investing in power system components to prevent outages and the potential societal
costs of such outages (Abunima et al., 2018; Billinton and Allan, 1992).

With the increasing shift from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources, VRES are becoming a
larger portion of power systems. This shift entails moving away from systems dominated by large
rotating masses that provide system inertia, a crucial stabilizer of power grid frequency fluctu-
ations (IEA, 2020). The Norwegian Transmission System Operator (TSO), Statnett, highlights
how the incorporation of wind and solar power introduces a new form of stability, categorized
as converters (Statnett, 2023). This necessitates measures to prevent more frequent outages as
it imposes key structural changes to power distribution. They further note that the increasing
use of converters poses a global challenge, particularly for countries rapidly transitioning from
fossil fuels to renewable energies without adequate low-carbon and dispatchable sources.

Abunima et al. (2018) provide a systematic overview of studies using reliability analysis on
composite power systems. They find that there is a substantial body of literature investigating
the impact of introducing new technologies in power systems, in addition to studies that are
conducted on systems in Asia. However, there is a notable lack of practical applications. Of the
100 thoroughly investigated articles in the review, 82 focus on educational test systems rather
than realistic systems. This illustrates the lack of research on realistic systems, in addition to
studies from a diverse set of locations, and of combining different technologies.

4.3.1 Probabilistic Component Failures

The input used for analyzing system adequacy consists of failure statistics for the various
components in the network. These statistics are derived from historical failures and are used
to estimate the probability of future failures for the same type of component. Additionally,
statistics representing the time required to repair each component after a failure are included as
repair times. In this study, the failures and repairs of components are assumed to be independent
events for each component, and correlations between different components are not considered.

stochastic variables indicating the time until a component changes state between working and
failing. Assuming a constant rate, the probability density function f(t) of the events is modeled
using an exponential distribution (Tuinema et al., 2020), as seen in Equation 4.1.

4.3.2 Monte Carlo Simulation of the Power System

While there are models designed to assess the reliability of individual components or small
systems of components, these models struggle to manage the added complexity of numerous
components in large systems (Tuinema et al., 2020). One effective method for handling this
complexity is Monte Carlo simulation, which uses probabilistic inputs to simulate the behavior
of the entire system.

Component failures in this analysis are modeled using Monte Carlo simulation with a sequential
simulation method known as the state transition method (Billinton and Li, 1993). This approach
involves sampling the time to failure and time to repair as explained above, producing a time
series indicating whether each component is operational or not (Tuinema et al., 2020). These
time series are then translated into a matrix for all hours in a year and all components in the
power network, with values of 1 indicating a working state and 0 indicating a failed state. This
process is repeated for each iteration of the simulation. The algorithm is detailed in Algorithm
1.

                M [n, t : end_t] := t,n
                t := end_t
                t,n := 0
            else
                ttr  Exp(µn)
                t := min(t + ttr, T )
                t,n := 1
            end if
       end while
       return M
   end for

The system under consideration is derived from the 2040 network presented in the study by

by 2050 pathway, which has led to significant changes compared to the current state of the real
Bolivian system. The BASE NZE 2040 network has been optimized to include the most suitable
generation and transmission capacities for normal operations without faults and failures. As a
result, the system has already been designed to operate efficiently and sustainably in adherence
to the assumptions, limitations and opportunities offered by the PyPSA-Earth-BO model. The
2040 system state adheres to a maximum limit of 1.05 million tons of CO2 equivalent emissions,
ensuring that the network operates within the boundaries set by the NZE pathway. The overall
generation capacity for each generator with capacity above 5 MW can be found in Figure B.1 in
Appendix B, while the location of the capacities are found below in Figure 4.2.
Due to the properties of the PyPSA-Earth model, solar and wind power plants at each bus
are interpreted as single large facilities for solar and wind, respectively. However, all other
components are treated as unique plants. It is important to note that the model is limited to

Figure 4.2: Overview of the generation and transmission capacity in Bolivia 2040, highlighting
key buses and lines.

expanding only existing facilities. Consequently, when the model increases the capacity of a
technology at a bus, this capacity is added to existing generators rather than representing a
separate new generator. In reality, some power plants could be separated into different units,
which are not fully represented in the model.

The line characteristics are sourced from the CNDC (2022) report, maintaining the parallel
line configurations as specified. This approach ensures that when one line fails, the remaining
parallel line still provides capacity, preventing a total loss of connection. This redundancy,
applied to 17 different connections, enhances the system's ability to withstand faults or failures
without significant disruption.

However, there are some limitations in the modeling of line expansion in PyPSA-Earth-BO from
the initial realistic representation in the year 2024 to the projected system in 2040. Similar to
the treatment of generators, new investments in the capacity of transmission lines are added only
to existing lines. Although, in reality, investments would involve installing new lines parallel to
the existing ones, the model only adds new capacity to existing lines. This simplification may
not fully capture the potential for new infrastructure development and its impact on the system's
resilience and capacity.

In addition, the investment decisions for both transmission lines and generators in PyPSA-Earth-
BO are based on the model's objective function, which aims to minimize total costs while
meeting projected demand. However, this optimization does not account for the importance of
reliability in the event of component failures, as such failures are not included in the model. A
model that included reliability in its objective function or as a set of constraints would likely
incorporate more excess capacity. Consequently, the system configuration projected for 2040,
which is used for composite system adequacy analysis, may perform worse than if reliability
considerations were explicitly incorporated into the initial optimization model.

4.6 Conclusion

The current study successfully implemented a Monte Carlo simulation for composite system
adequacy assessment in PyPSA-Earth-BO. Based on the modeled power system in Bolivia from
the BASE NZE scenario in 2040, the impact of any component failing can be explored and used
to understand key vulnerabilities of the system. Furthermore, the implementation can easily be
adapted to include a more detailed description of the Bolivian system by including any number
and type of components and more realistic failure and repair rates that are based on actual
statistics from Bolivia directly.
The composite system adequacy analysis of the Bolivian power system provided preliminary
insights into its reliability. By simulating the model both with and without emission constraints,
the significant impact of this modeling decision was highlighted. The EENS decreased by 72%
when the model utilized all available capacity without emission constraints. Despite this, the
critical buses and components remained largely consistent across both analyses. The primary
difference was the proportion of lost load attributed to generators versus transmission lines, with
transmission lines playing a more significant role in the scenario without emission constraints.
This underscores the importance of transmission grid expansion in efforts to enhance system
reliability.
These findings underscore the importance of considering both environmental policies and in-
frastructure robustness in power system planning. Future studies should refine the modeling
approach to further improve the representation of the real power system, account for multiple
unit failures, and include a broader range of scenarios, thereby better capturing the real-world
complexity of power system operations. Additionally, the results highlight the critical need
to integrate more dispatchable generators, particularly those from sustainable sources such as
hydroelectric, biomass, and geothermal. Policymakers should focus on the trade-offs between
emission constraints and system reliability, emphasizing investments in sustainable, flexible
generation options and transmission grid enhancements to ensure a combination of resilient and
environmentally responsible power systems.

Reflections on PyPSA-Earth

5.1 The Potential and Challenges of PyPSA-Earth

This chapter delves into the opportunities and limitations associated with using PyPSA-Earth-
BO to model the Bolivian power system, setting the stage for the final case study. Up to this
point, the adaptation of PyPSA-Earth for Bolivia has been thoroughly detailed, followed by
an extensive validation process. The model has been employed to assess the impact of gas
subsidies in Bolivia through the analysis of six different scenarios. Subsequently, a case study
was conducted to explore the implementation of composite system adequacy, providing an
analysis of the reliability of a future Bolivian power system.

With a strong motivation to connect all energy systems globally, the concept of PyPSA-Earth
stands out in the energy system modeling domain. It has automated the tedious processes of data
collection, processing, and formatting. By using a standard data format for all energy systems,
whether for a region, country or a continent, it enables a new set of analyses. PyPSA-Earth
boasts high spatial and temporal resolutions, allowing the model to examine both close-up, short-
term processes and large-scale systems, matching the complexity needed for various problems.
These resolutions facilitate the implementation of load flow equations, enhancing realism and
the likelihood of finding feasible solutions. Moreover, PyPSA-Earth is open, transparent, and
accessible to everyone through open-source code.

Being a tool under active development, PyPSA-Earth has several areas where it currently falls
short. The usability of PyPSA-Earth could still be improved through better user interfaces and
more comprehensive documentation, ensuring that users can easily understand and utilize its
features. The complexity of the model can make it time-consuming to become familiar with, and
it can be challenging to interpret how to run the model for specific scenarios. While PyPSA-Earth
aims to automatically download datasets globally, there may be gaps in coverage or accuracy in
some regions due to limitations in available data sources or the quality of data retrieved. In this
project, for instance, all datasets except for the solar and wind weather data need to be processed
and collected from external sources, as the data currently available for Bolivia is very deficient.
Moreover, PyPSA-Earth operates on deterministic principles, which means it assumes all inputs
are known with certainty and outputs are entirely determined by these inputs. This limitation
can affect the accuracy of long-term predictions and sensitivity analyses, particularly in volatile

                                                       61
energy markets. Additionally, PyPSA-Earth is currently limited to modeling scenarios up to one
year at a time and does not support multi-horizon analysis, restricting its capability to analyze
longer-term trends and multi-year planning strategies.

Despite these challenges, significant efforts are underway to enhance PyPSA-Earth. The devel-
opment team is actively working on improving the user interfaces and documentation, making
it easier for users to understand and utilize its features. Comprehensive datasets are continually
being integrated to cover more regions accurately. Additionally, the community is exploring
ways to incorporate stochastic elements using the Monte Carlo method to address the limitations
of deterministic modeling (PyPSA-Earth, 2023; PyPSA, 2022). This spring, the PyPSA-meets-
Earth initiative also launched the first open-source, sector-coupled energy system model with
global coverage (PyPSA-Earth-sec, 2024).

6.1 Introduction

The energy system, once heavily reliant on centralized fossil fuel-based power generation, is now
undergoing unprecedented evolution. While coal, natural gas, and nuclear plants historically
dominated, there is now a surge in renewable energy technologies. IRENA (2023) reports that
VRES will expand globally from a combined share of 9% generation in 2020 to 70% by 2050 due
to technological advancements, cost reductions, and societal pressure to mitigate climate change.
This shift is not only enhancing grid sustainability but also challenging traditional centralized
models, promoting a more decentralized and resilient energy system (IRENA, 2023).

However, the rapid integration of VRES poses significant challenges to traditional energy system
optimization models. Historically designed for stable fossil fuel and nuclear power, these models
struggle with the intermittent nature of VRES. Wind and solar energy introduce variability
and uncertainty, necessitating sophisticated optimization models capable of handling dynamic
fluctuations in supply and demand. This paradigm shift towards renewable energy requires
adaptable, scalable, and resilient modeling approaches to effectively manage the complex and
variable energy landscape (IEA, 2020).

Furthermore, transitioning to a renewable-dominated energy system requires addressing the
uncertainty inherent in supply from VRES (IRENA, 2023). It is essential to move away
from deterministic models that rely on single profiles and instead adopt stochastic modeling
approaches. Stochastic models account for the unpredictable nature of weather patterns affecting
renewable energy generation by incorporating a range of scenarios for variables such as solar
irradiance and wind speeds. These models also manage uncertainty in load projections due
to factors like economic activity and consumer behavior. By integrating probabilistic data
and scenarios, stochastic models provide a more comprehensive and resilient framework for
optimizing energy systems. This approach allows for better planning and operation, ensuring
that the grid can adapt to both expected and unexpected changes in supply and demand, thus
enhancing stability and reliability (Backe, 2023).

Stochastic modeling often comes at the cost of lower resolution and simplification of constraints
imposed by load flow equations. On the other hand, deterministic models provide higher resolu-
tion, they may overlook uncertainties, potentially leading to suboptimal decisions. The trade-off
between stochastic and deterministic modeling lies in balancing computational feasibility with
the need for realistic modeling of energy system uncertainties (Fan et al., 2022).

The paper aims to explore the effects of using a multi-horizon stochastic programming model
compared to a single-period deterministic model for power system optimization in Bolivia,
facilitated through open-source models. Leveraging improved data from Kyte et al. (2024)
for PyPSA-Earth-BO, the study implements the EMPIRE model, marking the first application
to a non-European power system. Previous studies in Bolivia have focused on deterministic
capacity planning optimization. In contrast, this study seeks to evaluate the effectiveness of the
multi-horizon stochastic model in capturing VRES uncertainty, comparing it to a high-temporal-
and-spatial-resolution deterministic model that includes load flow optimization.

6.2 Literature Review

Even though uncertainty in the input of energy models can largely affect the output, few models
take this into account. In their review of energy models with large quantities of VRES, Ringkjøb
et al. (2018) point to the challenges that arise from this such as representing short-term variability,
incorporating the effects of climate change, and ensuring transparency in the modeling process.
Regarding uncertainty, energy production from solar and wind suffers from high variability over
short amounts of time, and small changes to the input can significantly change the outcome. Most
models they review, however, base the weather data on historical data modeled deterministically,
making the results highly vulnerable to potential changes in input (Ringkjøb et al., 2018). Prina
et al. (2020) and Fodstad et al. (2022) point to the fact that energy models face uncertainty in a
wide range of parameters, but that only a fraction uses an approach to handle it. Moreover, there
is a large consensus that handling this uncertainty is currently a key priority in energy modeling.

To handle the uncertainty in energy modeling, Yue et al. (2018) identify four approaches:
Monte Carlo analysis, stochastic programming, robust optimization, and modeling to generate
alternatives. Stochastic programming is an approach that can be used to provide a hedging
strategy, showing the best course of action that accounts for future uncertainties. However, the
approach significantly increases the computational burden of the model, and only a handful
of uncertain parameters can be analyzed. The approach therefore demands that the modeler
understands which parameters affect the result the most. Pfenninger et al. (2014) present
stochastic modeling as a formal approach to handle aleatory uncertainty, the uncertainty that
cannot be reduced through better data or a better model. They also emphasize how the approach
only handles a subset of the uncertain parameters, and that there can be unexpected sources of
uncertainty. This underlines the importance of transparency in models and the assumptions, as
energy models are not verifiable against physical phenomena. Rather, the models are methods
to examine the effects of a set of assumptions, necessitating that the assumptions are clearly
stated.

As uncertainties continue to play a critical role in energy systems, the significance of stochastic
programming is poised to increase. By factoring in uncertainties, stochastic programming en-
ables more realistic and justifiable investment decisions. A study by Ringkjøb et al. (2020)
highlights that deterministic models tend to underestimate costs and overestimate the contribu-
tion of variable energy sources. This leads to an underestimation of CO2 emissions and of the
necessary flexibility to manage the unpredictable variability of solar and wind energy.

Different optimization models have been adapted to model the Bolivian energy system. However,
there are no examples in the current literature of open-source models used in the Bolivian
context that handle uncertainty. Among proprietary software tools, OptGen, developed by
PSR, is being actively used by the Bolivian government. The model integrates Stochastic Dual
Dynamic Programming (SDDP), ensuring the modeling of uncertainties such as variable weather
conditions. It is used in the Bolivian context for generation and transmission expansion planning
in the study by Thome et al. (2019). Nonetheless, the model lacks the open-source nature that
promotes transparency and wider collaboration.

Several open-source models are summarized in the context of Bolivian power system planning
by Kyte et al. (2024). These models include Dispa-SET, OSeMOSYS, LUT, and PyPSA-Earth-
BO, all of which are deterministic models addressing different resolutions of the four main

                                                       66
challenges identified by Prina et al. (2020): time, space, techno-economic detail, and sector-
coupling. Although these models are applied in contexts with high penetration of Intermittent
Renewable Energy Sources (IRES), the inclusion of uncertainty in energy planning for Bolivia
remains unexplored within the open-source modeling domain. This gap highlights the need
for future efforts to incorporate uncertainty into open-source energy models to enhance the
robustness and reliability of energy planning in Bolivia.

The European Model for Power system Investments with Renewable Energy (EMPIRE), de-
veloped by the Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU) and SINTEF, is an
open-source model based on multi-horizon stochastic programming for energy transition ana-
lyses (Backe et al., 2022). This model has been utilized in various projects focusing on European
capacity expansion planning, where strategic decisions are subject to operational uncertainties
caused by varying availability of Variable Renewable Energy Sources (VRES) and load profile
realizations. Designed to flexibly incorporate energy system input data from any part of the
world, the EMPIRE model is capable of being adapted for non-European energy systems (Backe
et al., 2022). Despite its global applicability, its use has so far been limited to European contexts.

6.3.1 Modeling Tool: EMPIRE

Christian Skar et al. (2016) present the open-source EMPIRE model as a multi-horizon stochastic
linear programming approach to optimize long-term capacity expansion planning in energy
systems. Designed to navigate both short-term fluctuations and long-term trends, the model
adeptly manages operational uncertainties, thereby facilitating more robust and strategic resource
allocation decisions. A particular emphasis is placed on the unpredictable nature of variable
renewable energy sources, such as solar, wind, and hydro power. By incorporating various
projections of hourly electricity demand and historic data of VRES power production, the model
generates stochastic scenarios (Backe, 2023).
The model operates under the assumption of perfect foresight concerning strategic input data;
however, it simultaneously accommodates a range of operational scenarios. This approach
serves as an alternative and approximate formulation in comparison to conventional multi-stage
stochastic programming. Instead, the model adopts the principles of multi-horizon stochastic
programming as outlined by Kaut et al. (2014), drastically reducing the size of the problem.

6.3.2 Modeling Tool: PyPSA-Earth-BO

In the study of Kyte et al. (2024), version 3.0.0 of the PyPSA-Earth model (PyPSA authors, 2023)
is adapted for the Bolivian power system constituting PyPSA-Earth-BO. The current version of
the PyPSA-Earth model is functioning as a single period deterministic investment planning
model. The core strength of PyPSA-Earth lies in its ability to incorporate high-resolution
data on weather conditions, electricity consumption patterns, and the technical specifications of
energy infrastructure to generate realistic and comprehensive scenarios. From an operational

                                                       70
perspective, PyPSA-Earth models the hourly operation of power systems over a full year, optim-
izing the dispatch of generation, storage, and transmission with linear power flow to minimize
costs or emissions while meeting demand. The model can be adapted to different regions and
scales, making it a versatile tool for exploring various energy transition pathways and policy
scenarios (Brown et al., 2018).

By incorporating load flow restrictions, PyPSA-Earth's optimization model becomes more rep-
resentative of real-world power systems, increasing its fidelity and applicability for various
energy system studies and analyses (Fernandez Vazquez et al., 2023). The model can bet-
ter capture the physical constraints and interdependencies within the transmission network by
considering Kirchhoff's laws and power flow equations. This allows for a more realistic repres-
entation of power flows, voltage angles, and line loading, which can impact the optimal dispatch
and capacity expansion decisions. The load flow restrictions can reveal transmission bottlenecks
or congestion points that may limit the optimal utilization of generation resources or require
additional transmission investments. Without these restrictions, the model may overestimate the
available transmission capacity, leading to infeasible or sub-optimal solutions (PyPSA-Earth,
2023).

The model has the same objective as the EMPIRE model seen in (6.1), to minimize total system
costs, however is currently limited by isolated annual optimizations. The objective function
and more details of the PyPSA-Earth model can be found in the original paper by Brown et al.
(2018), whereas the full Bolivian adaptation details are found in the study by Kyte et al. (2024).

6.4 Data

The present version of EMPIRE is primarily designed to explore different ways to reduce carbon
emissions in Europe's electricity sector. Even so, its underlying structure is quite flexible. It
can be adapted to study other geographic areas or different sectors if these can be represented
similarly to how power flows through a network (Backe, 2023). In the forthcoming chapter, the
necessary input for running the model is introduced first, followed by a detailed explanation of
the implementation using Bolivian data from PyPSA-Earth-BO.

6.4.1 EMPIRE: Input Data

To adapt the European-focused EMPIRE model for use in Bolivia, it is crucial to have modifiable
input data. Being an open-source model, EMPIRE ensures that all its data is accessible and easy
to understand.

The model differentiates its input data into two categories: network data, and data for scenario
generation. For the network configuration, data is organized across six Excel workbooks
named General, Generator, Node, Sets, Storage, and Transmission. The network-related data is
imported through .tab-files, but all datasets are maintained in Excel sheets to simplify the editing
process (Backe, 2023). In contrast, the scenario data, which typically includes historical data
that does not require frequent modification, is stored in csv files. This includes hourly records
of historical electricity demand, inflows to regulated hydropower plants, and capacity factors for

                                                       71
solar, run-of-river, and wind power plants.

6.4.2 EMPIRE for Bolivia: From PyPSA to EMPIRE

Parzen et al. (2023) introduce PyPSA-Earth with a clear objective of fostering large-scale
collaboration on energy system planning. The model can simulate the energy system of the
entire world or any specific subset thereof. Additionally, it offers high temporal and spatial
resolution, which significantly enhances the utility and applicability of the model for detailed,
geographically diverse energy analysis.

As another open-source model, PyPSA-Earth enables EMPIRE to benefit from its integrated
data retrieval from a range of sources. PyPSA-Earth leverages data from other open datasets
such as OpenStreetMap (OSM) and Copernicus. However, since some of these sources may
rely heavily on volunteer contributions, there is a potential for discrepancies and outdated
information. To address these issues, PyPSA-Earth also supports data management through
custom files, allowing for the direct access and modification of downloaded data. The work
presented in Kyte et al. (2024) on PyPSA-Earth-BO focuses extensively on improving the data
inputs for the Bolivian network, aiming to minimize discrepancies to maximize the benefits of
high spatial resolution. This meticulous work on data quality presents an opportunity to enhance
other models, such as EMPIRE, by adapting these refined data inputs.

PyPSA-Earth-BO currently achieves high spatial resolution with a 101-node configuration. How-
ever, as the complexity increases with the integration of stochastic multi-stage programming, the
Bolivian network within EMPIRE is streamlined into 30 nodes. The new network configuration
is illustrated in Figure C.1 in Appendix C. By leveraging information obtained from simulations
of both the full node network and its reduced counterpart in PyPSA-Earth-BO, one can extract
all essential data necessary to implement the EMPIRE model for Bolivia. Much of the data
used in PyPSA-Earth is per-unit and consistent across all networks, as it is available as the input
data in PyPSA-Earth. Meanwhile, data specific to each individual node is accessed only after
the network has been clustered. Therefore, the data gathering procedure for the implementation
in EMPIRE benefits substantially from the clustering process available in the PyPSA tool. The
data collection process has been streamlined, so that relevant data is imported directly from
PyPSA-Earth-BO files using pandas. This approach efficiently updates the EMPIRE Excel input
sheets, eliminating the need for manual data entry.

Like PyPSA, which collects specific annual data for a whole year, EMPIRE extends this approach
to five-year periods. For example, the load for the first period mirrors the load demand observed
in the final year. Additionally, emission caps are set so that the annual emissions for each year
within a period align with those from the final year. Also, the installed capacities determined
at the period's onset remain unchanged and fully accessible throughout its duration. This
consistency is essential as the model does not support ongoing investments or installations of
capacity within a period.

Network configuration

In the EMPIRE documentation (Backe, 2023), the input section first presents the input workbook
titled Sets, which delineates the scope of the problem. This sheet displays all the indices utilized
in the model and should be consistent across the other input sheets and the inputs specified
in run.py. Any indices introduced in other sections that do not align with those in Sets will
prevent the model from running successfully. The run.py file also specifies the nodes, which
must correspond with those listed in Sets. Furthermore, the time scope configured in run.py
influences the required inputs in Sets. However, the script used to develop new sheets for the
Bolivian data automatically updates these indices when changes are made, ensuring consistency
and functionality.

Similar to the process in PyPSA-Earth, all generators must be assigned to a node in the Sets
workbook to be able to expand. For solar and wind power plants, these are added to every node
as they are free to be expanded everywhere - only influenced by the weather potentials. Working
with the Sets workbook reveals that EMPIRE cannot run with empty sets, which presents a
challenge when modeling Bolivia, a landlocked country, with offshore nodes. To resolve this
issue, offshore nodes are excluded from the EMPIRE model for Bolivia, aligning with the
approach taken in the PyPSA-Earth-BO model.

The Generator workbook in EMPIRE begins by detailing various indices and parameters.
Cost-related parameters, efficiencies, and CO2 intensities are sourced from the costs file of
PyPSA-Earth. Conversely, most capacity-related data are drawn from a custom power plant file
also used in the PyPSA-Earth-BO model. In both models, all capacity factors are set to 1. The
capacity factors for time-dependent availability of wind, solar, and run-of-river are initially set
to zero, as these values are generated during stochastic scenario creation. The capacity factor
for biomass is implemented as detailed in 6.3.1.

Rather than using a scaling factor for decommissioning, the EMPIRE model employs the
InitialCapacity sheet, which is chosen to deduct planned decommissioning during specified
periods. Additionally, while the ramp rate parameter is not yet included in PyPSA-Earth, it is
incorporated in the EMPIRE model using predefined values.

The maximum installed capacity is capped at 840 MW and 510 MW for biomass and geothermal
power plants, respectively, as implemented in the PyPSA-Earth-BO model (Kyte et al., 2024).
Hydro run-of-river power plants are constrained to their current capacity plus any planned
expansions. This approach is adopted because hydro projects are expanded cautiously for nature
conservation concerns and because PyPSA-Earth is still in the process of enabling configured
hydro expansion. The imposed limits align with those used in the PyPSA modeling of the
Bolivian network.

In the Node data for EMPIRE, the annual load for each node is added first. These added
projections align with the forecast reported by CNDC (2022). The cost for lost load is uniformly
applied across all nodes. For every MWh of unmet demand, the model's objective function
incurs a penalty of e 22 000 (Economics, 2013). The final sheet includes the maximum annual
hydro production for each node and includes the total annual production found in the scenario
data for hydro.

In the General workbook, the most relevant data is the CO2 cap, which aligns with the Net

                                                       73
Zero Emissions (NZE) pathway by 2050. A CO2 price is not applied since the NZE pathway
solely dictates the restriction on total emissions. Moreover, seasonal scales are now dynamically
calculated within the model based on the inputs specified in the run.py file.

The Storage sheet contains data similar to the Generator sheet but specifically focuses on storage
technologies. In Bolivia, batteries are the only relevant storage technology so far, so pumped
hydro storage present in EMPIRE is excluded from the model implementation. The data in this
section largely comes from the same costs file used for generators. Notably, the relationship
between power and energy capacities is such that the installed power capacity is set to one fifth
of the installed energy capacity for storage.

Finally, the data regarding transmission lines is implemented in the Transmission sheet. It is
important to note that EMPIRE requires data for the lines in both directions, from node 1 to
node 2 and node 2 to node 1. Costs and the lifetime of components are collected from the
costs file, while the other data is taken straight from the resulting network data after running
PyPSA-Earth-BO with the 30-node network.

Scenario data

In the European version of EMPIRE, multiple years of historical data are available for scenario
generation. In contrast, the Bolivian case faces sparse scenario data. For load projection, the data
is derived from and extended using the assumptions of CNDC's 2022 report (CNDC, 2022). For
inflows related to the modeling of hydropower, there is only one year of inflow scalars available,
sourced from a previous study by Hannotte (2023). For weather data, PyPSA-Earth provides
several downloadable cutouts, with 2011, 2013, and 2018 being sufficiently tested and therefore
included in this analysis. Due to the differing data availability, load and hydro inflows have only
one sample year, whereas solar and wind potential have three sample years.

Generally, the scenario data requires aggregation into newly clustered nodes, as the data is
initially downloaded from PyPSA-Earth-BO before the clustering procedure. This involves not
only aggregating the data but also formatting it. For instance, the electric load from PyPSA-
Earth-BO needs to be aggregated by summing the loads of the original buses to match the new
clustered nodes. Similarly, for solar and wind profiles, it involves averaging the capacity factors
for the clustered nodes.

Regarding hydropower, the two models handle hydro inflows differently. While PyPSA-Earth
uses a common inflow.nc file for both hydro run-of-river and hydro regulated, the EMPIRE
model maintains two separate datasets for each (hydroror.csv and hydroseasonsal.csv).

In PyPSA-Earth, inflows for both regulated and run-of-river generators are aggregated at each
node. First, capacity factors for each individual plant are used to scale the available inflow for
that respective plant. Subsequently, the unique inflows are averaged to determine the total nodal
inflow if the node hosts multiple generators. This method assumes uniform inflow across all
hydro generators at a given node, potentially oversimplifying the variability of water availability
among different generators.

Conversely, EMPIRE manages inflows for regulated and run-of-river generators differently.
For regulated plants, inflows are calculated similarly to PyPSA-Earth by scaling according to

                                                       74
individual capacity. However, EMPIRE allocates the calculated total inflow proportionally
among the generators instead of averaging it. This approach allows each generator's inflow to
represent a fraction of the node's total inflow, providing a more nuanced representation of water
distribution.

For run-of-river plants, EMPIRE also bases inflows on the scale of individual capacity but
averages these capacity factors when multiple generators are present within a node. As the
capacity factors are not used to scale the inflows before solving, this model adjusts inflows
for new investments, enabling a dynamic response to capacity changes. This feature is absent
in PyPSA-Earth, where inflows remain constant irrespective of capacity changes. This could
limit PyPSA-Earth's ability to model investment in run-of-river projects effectively, especially
in nodes where average inflow is low or when there's a significant discrepancy in capacity,
potentially restricting production potential. However, both methods are rather oversimplified.

6.4.3 Study Pathways

To validate the EMPIRE model for Bolivia, scenarios 1.1 BASE and 1.2 BASE NZE, as
detailed in Kyte et al. (2024), are replicated using the input data discussed above in this chapter.
The BASE scenario functions as a business-as-usual case, whereas the BASE NZE scenario
incorporates an emissions cap aligned with the NZE by 2050 pathway. After verifying the
outputs, these scenarios are critically examined to compare the performance of the EMPIRE
and PyPSA-Earth-BO models. Additionally, scenario 2.1 Gradual Costs (GC), introduced in
the same study, is run and explores the models' responses to a gradual reduction in oil and
gas subsidies. This comparative analysis aims to highlight the differences, shortcomings, and
advantages of each model, particularly focusing on the distinct characteristics of a single-period
deterministic model with load flow versus a multi-period stochastic model. Such an evaluation
enhances the understanding of their suitability and effectiveness in modeling Bolivia's energy
landscape.

Following the comparative analysis, the 1.2 BASE NZE scenario is run with a longer horizon.
The motivation is to highlight the impact that choice of time frame can have on the investment
strategy as more information is revealed.

6.5.2 Single Period Deterministic Model vs. Multi-Period Stochastic Model

So far, the primary goal has been to align the results of the two models to validate EMPIRE's
solutions for Bolivia. This was done by comparing EMPIRE's results for Bolivia with the
already validated outcomes from PyPSA-Earth-BO. Moving forward, the distinctive features
of the EMPIRE model come back into play. These include the benefits of enhanced scenario
generation that leverages three distinct weather years for assessing solar and wind potential, the
inclusion of ramp rates, and unrestricted multi-horizon optimization. The implemented change
to PyPSA-Earth-BO as presented by (Kyte et al., 2024), meaning a restricted annual expansion
to maintain reasonable investment levels, is now included. Both models are allowed to expand

6.6 Conclusion

This study compares the multi-horizon stochastic programming model, European Model for
Power system Investments with Renewable Energy, and the Python for Power System Analysis-
Earth model, a single-period deterministic model. EMPIRE accounts for uncertainty over
multiple time periods, providing a comprehensive analysis of energy system planning under
various future scenarios. In contrast, PyPSA-Earth focuses on annual optimization of the energy
system without considering the uncertain characteristics of weather and demand potentials,
though it incorporates a more realistic modeling of load flow restrictions. The comparison,
using the same inputs, has highlighted significant differences in outputs due to variations in cost
calculations, time resolution, and approximations of complexity.

Importantly, both PyPSA-Earth and EMPIRE benefit from being open-source models. This
transparency enables continuous improvement through community contributions and shared
methodologies, fostering a more inclusive and dynamic development environment. Such col-
laborative efforts ultimately drive advancements in energy system optimization and planning.

PyPSA-Earth is particularly well-suited for medium- to long-term planning, focusing on determ-
inistic scenarios and regional-scale studies, offering high temporal and spatial granularity. This
study highlights its significant advantage in accurately determining necessary transmission line
capacities and the optimal distribution of generation closer to load centers.

In contrast, EMPIRE excels in modeling longer expansion plan horizons with a stochastic
approach. The stochastic model shows a more robust investment strategy, particularly in terms of
investments in variable renewable energy sources (VRES) by incorporating weather uncertainties
in addition to weather variations. Additionally, the modeling of Bolivia's energy transition
towards 2060 reveals the added value of a longer horizon. More information over a longer
period changes the investment strategy, even at the beginning of the horizon.

Notably, the largest discrepancies between the models are observed in power systems with high
renewable adoption, influenced by both the difference in modeling of stochasticity and of the
real restrictions induces by load flow equations. It becomes apparent that a deterministic model
tends to overestimate the actual potential of VRES, accounting for variability but not uncertainty.
Conversely, the stochastic model's lack of load flow restrictions allows it to benefit from reduced
transmission line capacity and more centralized generation.

Despite these differences, both models tend to converge towards similar system operations by
the end of the 2040 horizon, albeit with different investment strategies. Ultimately, they arrive
at a similar capacity portfolio in terms of total carrier capacity in the analyzed cases.

The validation and results reveal that the inclusion of load flow would enable a more thorough
analysis. Future research should focus on integrating load flow constraints into EMPIRE to better
capture the complexities of power systems with high shares of renewable energy. Additionally,
exploring hybrid approaches that combine the strengths of both deterministic and stochastic
models could provide more accurate and reliable insights for energy system planning.

Moreover, the extended modeling horizon to 2060 provides interesting insights but are con-
strained by the modeling assumptions. To better understand the future of the Bolivian energy
system and leverage the multi-horizon modeling in EMPIRE, future studies should revisit the

restrictions on investments in dispatchable energy sources such as hydropower, biomass, and
geothermal.

Chapter 7

Concluding Remarks

This thesis set out to address several critical research questions related to modeling and analyzing
the Bolivian power system, with a particular focus on leveraging open-source tools to support
the country's energy transition. The primary objectives were to enhance the PyPSA-Earth-BO
model's representation of Bolivia's energy infrastructure, investigate the impact of natural gas
subsidies on the power system, implement a reliability assessment tool, and integrate the Bolivian
power system into the EMPIRE model for a comparative analysis with PyPSA-Earth-BO.

The first study made significant strides in improving the PyPSA-Earth-BO model's accuracy in
reflecting Bolivia's energy landscape. By refining data inputs, capturing spatial and temporal
dynamics, and incorporating new constraints, the enhanced model provided a more realistic
representation of the complexities and constraints inherent to the Bolivian power system. This
improvement laid the foundation for a comprehensive analysis of the impact of natural gas sub-
sidies on Bolivia's energy transition. The study explored various subsidy reduction strategies,
through gradual and abrupt cuts in subsidies with and without CO2 emission reduction con-
straints. The results highlighted the potential for up to e 2.5 billion in cost savings while
adhering to a Net Zero Emissions by 2050 pathway. The optimal system across strategies com-
bines dispatchable and renewable sources such as geothermal, biomass, and hydropower with the
significant solar power potential in the country. The findings of this study emphasize that there
is no trade-off between cost savings and reducing emissions; rather, these two objectives result
in the same optimal power system when considering the total costs, which include subsidies.

Building upon the enhanced PyPSA-Earth-BO model, the second study introduced a composite
system adequacy assessment tool to evaluate the reliability of Bolivia's future power system. Two
separate analyses were conducted both including and excluding constraints on the emissions of
the system. The tool, based on Monte Carlo simulation, allowed for a probabilistic assessment of
system adequacy under various conditions. By quantifying the expected energy not supplied, loss
of load probability, and identifying critical components, the analysis emphasize the importance of
transmission grid expansion and the integration of more dispatchable generators from sustainable
sources. This combination is essential to ensure system reliability while reducing emissions.
Future studies should enhance the modeling approach to better capture the real behavior of system
components in the event of power outages, providing more valuable insights to policymakers
and transmission system operators.

                                                       91
The third study marked a significant milestone by successfully integrating the Bolivian power
system into the EMPIRE model, leveraging the data refinements from PyPSA-Earth-BO. This
integration not only expanded the geographical scope of the EMPIRE model but also enabled a
comparative analysis between its multi-horizon stochastic programming approach and PyPSA-
Earth's single-period deterministic model. The study revealed key differences in how each
model handles uncertainties, long-term planning, and system complexities. EMPIRE's ability
to incorporate stochastic elements and optimize over multiple time horizons provided a more
comprehensive assessment of the impact of uncertainties on investment decisions and system
performance. On the other hand, PyPSA-Earth-BO's detailed spatial resolution and inclusion
of load flow constraints offered a more granular representation of the physical constraints and
dynamics within the power system.

The comparative analysis highlighted the strengths and limitations of each modeling framework,
providing valuable insights for the ongoing development and improvement of open-source
energy system models. The study demonstrated the importance of considering uncertainties
and long-term dynamics in energy system planning, particularly in the context of increasing
renewable energy penetration. It also underscored the need for models that can capture the
complex interactions between generation, transmission, and demand, while accounting for the
variability and uncertainty inherent to renewable energy sources.

The findings of this thesis have significant implications for energy system modeling and policy
decision-making in Bolivia and beyond. Studying the impact of fuel subsidies revealed that
potential savings from reducing subsidies could be sufficient to fund the investments required for
sustainable energy transition. The enhanced PyPSA-Earth-BO model and the newly developed
reliability assessment tool provide powerful resources for evaluating the technical, economic,
and environmental dimensions of Bolivia's energy transition. The integration of the Bolivian
power system into the EMPIRE model expands the toolkit available to energy system planners
and researchers, enabling a more comprehensive exploration of transition pathways and policy
scenarios.

However, the work presented in this thesis also opens up several avenues for future research. A
sensitivity analysis of gas and oil price projections would provide a more nuanced understanding
of the impact of fuel price fluctuations on Bolivia's energy transition. In addition, the current
limitations of dispatchable and sustainable generation sources should be further assessed to
ensure that these dependable and attractive technologies are not overly constrained.

Further enhancements to the reliability assessment tool, such as incorporating more detailed
component data, exploring correlations between failures and repairs, and incorporating failure
and repair rates representative of the components in Bolivia would strengthen its predictive
capabilities. Moreover, by incorporating the reliability directly into the optimization model, a
more realistic assessment of the resulting power system configuration can be conducted.

Investigating hybrid modeling approaches that combine the strengths of deterministic and
stochastic models could offer new insights into the complexities and uncertainties of energy
systems with high renewable penetration. More in-depth studies of the long-term evolution of
the Bolivian energy system could provide insights into even more overarching optimal develop-
ments of the system.

Moreover, the successful integration of the Bolivian power system into the EMPIRE model

                                                       92
paves the way for extending its application to other non-European countries. By leveraging the
lessons learned from the Bolivian case study, researchers can refine the model's adaptability
and transferability, enabling a broader exploration of energy transition pathways in diverse
geographical and socioeconomic contexts.
