                            Abstract

Significant investments in renewable energy sources are being made globally [1].
Integrating wind and solar power, which are intermittent energy sources, presents a
substantial challenge for power systems. The mismatch between energy generation
and demand fluctuates [2]. Addressing this challenge requires developing flexibility
within the system to effectively manage these unregulated energy sources [3].
Pumped Hydro Energy Storage (PHES) is a regulative renewable energy source
which offers long- and short-term energy storage [2]. As an environmentally
sustainable resource, hydropower enhances flexibility and storage capacity within
the energy grid. These attributes are crucial for improving grid stability, supporting
the integration of intermittent renewable sources and facilitating load balancing [4].

This thesis explores the implementation of PHES in the eastern part of the Røldal-
Suldal power system (RSK). Furthermore, it examines the effects of different pump
sizes on the operational dynamics of the system. The program used for simulating
the hydropower system is ProdRisk, which employs a combination of Stochastic
Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP) and Stochastic Dynamic Programming
(SDP). These methods are ideal for optimizing hydropower scheduling [5]. The
simulations of the RSK system were run on the ProdRisk portal using the code
language python. The simulations examined pump sizes of 10 MW, 20 MW, 30
MW, and 40 MW over a 30-year period, utilizing simulated electricity prices from
the EMPS model.

In summary, implementing a pump in the eastern part of RSK is projected to
increase income from electricity sales compared to a system without a pump. A
plant equipped with a bigger pump has a longer operation time, both for the
turbine and pump. This is particularly notable during years of low inflow. While
a turbine with a larger pump may have lower eﬀiciency, the pump empowers the
turbine to generate more electricity when the demand is high. The annual mean
production is increasing from 962.9 GWh in the baseline simulation (0 MW) to 1000
GWh for the 40 MW pump simulation. However, annual net production declines
with larger pumps, dropping from 962.9 GWh in the baseline simulation to 955
GWh for the 40 MW pump simulation. Moreover, revenue from electricity sales
increases by 2.56% for the largest pump of 40 MW compared to a baseline simulation.

Overall, this thesis demonstrates the application of ProdRisk and the beneficial im-
pacts of PHES implementation. Utilizing PHES could be crucial in enhancing the
stability and flexibility of future power systems, while also helping to ensure that
global temperatures remain below 1.5ºC.

Denne masteroppgaven utforsker implementeringen av pumpekraft i den østlige

simulert i programmet ProdRisk. ProdRisk baserer seg på en kombinasjon av
Stokastisk Dual Dynamisk Programmering (SDDP) og Stokastisk Dynamisk
Programmering (SDP). Disse metodene er ideelle for optimalisering av vannkraft-
planlegging [5]. Simuleringene av RSK-systemet ble utført i ProdRisk-portalen ved

for både turbinen og pumpen. Driftsperioden for både turbinen og pumpen øker i
år med lav tilsig. En større pumpe har lavere virkningsgrad men den gir kraftverket
mulighet til å produsere mer strøm når etterspørselen er høy. Den årlig gjen-
nomsnittsprodusjonen Den årlige gjennomsnittsproduksjonen øker fra 962,9 GWh
i basissimuleringen uten pumpe til 1000 GWh for 40 MW pumpe-simuleringen.
Imidlertid avtar den årlige nettoproduksjonen med større pumper, og faller fra
962,9 GWh i basissimuleringen til 955 GWh for 40 MW pumpe-simuleringen. I

Masteroppgaven viser gjennom anvendelse av ProdRisk de gunstige virkningen av
å implementere PHES. Bruk av pumpekraft kan være avgjørende for å styrke sta-
biliteten og fleksibiliteten i fremtidige kraftsystemer, samtidig som det bidrar til å
sikre at globale temperaturer forblir under 1,5ºC.

1.1 A transforming energy market with the need for
       storing energy

Worldwide, power grids are swiftly shifting from fossil fuel-based systems to embrace
renewable energy sources [1]. The European energy system and the Norwegian sec-
tor are progressing towards achieving carbon neutrality. According to the Norwegian
Transmission System Operator Statnett’s long-term market analysis for 2022–2050,
the electrification of society results in a significant increase in demand for power.
The report states that future energy will mainly come from wind and solar power
[3]. Significant investments in solar and wind power are being made in several coun-
tries worldwide [1]. These sources are characterized by their variability and lack of
regulation in the power output, leading to a mismatch between energy generation
and variations in energy demand [2]. To smooth out the considerable fluctuations
in generation, it is essential to foster the development of substantial flexibility [3].
Flexibility refers to the capability of producers, consumers and energy storage sys-
tems to modify their energy production, consumption and storage in response to
changes in the power system. Flexible consumption can be rescheduled, reduced or
completely shut down for varying durations [6]. One form of flexibility is large-scale
energy storage.

The year 2023 experienced the most substantial rise in the capacity of renewable
energy sources. The world collectively added nearly 473 GW of renewable energy
capacity. That is an increase in renewable energy by 13.9% from the previous year,
2022 [7]. Also, 86% of the newly added power generation capacity worldwide came
from renewable energy sources. The growth in renewable energy is mainly due to the
increased expansion of solar and wind power. Solar power made up three-quarters of
the additional renewable capacity, with 346 GW of newly installed capacity, whereas
wind energy had an addition of 116 GW. The annual renewable capacity additions
need to be 1050 GW for the rest of this decade to meet the target of limiting the
rise in global temperature with 1.5◦C [7].

The transition in the power system towards renewable energy sources introduces
new challenges in maintaining supply security and managing power, frequency, and
voltage regulation. Pumped Hydro Energy Storage (PHES) is a technology that can
deliver short-term and long-term energy storage [2]. PHES involves the process of
pumping water from a lower reservoir to an upper reservoir during periods of surplus
power generation, such as windy and sunny days. Subsequently, the stored water
is released from the upper reservoir, flowing back to the lower reservoir through a
turbine to generate electricity during times of insuﬀicient supply. At times, utilizing
PHES can be advantageous, especially when there is surplus of power capacity and

                                                      1
the electricity prices are low [1]. PHES is the world’s largest energy storage technol-
ogy both in energy volume and in power capacity [2, 1].

Numerous PHES systems have been strategically developed in tandem with conven-
tional hydroelectric systems situated along rivers. In countries with hydropower, it
plays a significant role. By varying the flow of water through a turbine, hydropower
effortlessly adapts to variable loads in the power system [1]. In addition to being en-
vironmentally sustainable, hydropower offers flexibility and storage capacity. These
qualities make it possible to improve grid stability and facilitate the implementation
of periodic renewable energy sources such as wind and solar and offer load balancing
in the grid [4]. Additionally, PHES has fast response speed and flexible start and
stop [8]. PHES provides several ancillary services to the power grid, including volt-
age stability preservation and frequency variation, among others [4]. More details
on the characteristics and services of PHES can be found in the theory section 3.1.
Lastly, as mentioned in the previous paragraph, PHES is environmentally sustain-
able. Unlike wind and solar power, PHES is a renewable energy source that can be
regulated. Additionally, because PHES has developed in conjunction with conven-
tional hydropower systems, expanding PHES has a smaller environmental impact
compared to building wind and solar farms. Moreover, a key advantage of PHES is
its ability to integrate fluctuating renewable energy sources such as wind and solar
into the grid. This capability positions PHES as a prime candidate for investment
in renewables, due to its flexibility and storage properties that enhance grid stabil-
ity. Additionally, PHES emerges as a key solution for achieving the goal of limiting
global warming to 1.5ºC [4, 1].

1.2 Norway and its hydropower history

Reservoirs for hydropower in Norway comprise approximately 50% of Europe’s total
energy storage capacity within hydropower systems. The country has 87 TWh of
energy storage in reservoirs, with 10 to 20 TWh accessible most of the time. The
Norwegian hydropower reservoirs have a large potential to enhance energy storage
capabilities on a European scale by expanding their pumped storage capacity. Nor-
way is the sixth largest in the world in generating renewable energy from hydropower
[2]. Norway generates a total of 156.9 TWh of energy annually, with a significant
136.9 TWh coming from hydropower. In 2023, hydropower accounted for 87.3% of
Norway’s energy production, utilizing its 1781 power stations [9].

There are ten existing pumped storage plants in Norway with a total capacity of
1369 MW and an energy storage capacity of 5 TWh. Despite the total installed
hydropower capacity being 33 852 MW, PHES remains underexploited in Norway,
especially considering the prominence of hydropower in the country. The PHES are

                                                      2
located in the west and central parts of Norway. The PHES facilities were commis-
sioned between 1955 and 2005 and all are open-loop systems with natural inflow.
These facilities have a net energy production of 1.8 TWh per year, where gross en-
ergy is 2.6 TWh and 0.8 TWh is used for pumping. In Norway, the design of pumped
storage plants is specifically tailored for seasonal use. Consequently, the capacity of
the upper reservoirs is significantly larger than that of the lower reservoirs across
these facilities [2].

The development of hydropower and PHES was strongly influenced by the industry
development in Norway. In 1991, the Energy Act was adopted in Norway. The law
changed the organization of power supply and remains valid today. Before the law,
local authorities and parliament (Stortinget) decided the electricity prices. The law
facilitated the implementation of market-based principles for generating and sell-
ing electrical power. The electrical grid, due to its unique status as a monopoly,
was placed under specific regulatory oversight. The production and sale of electrical
power were subjected to competition. The Energy Act provided the legal framework
necessary for optimizing the power market. Also, The Energy Act ensuring that
production is maximized and developed in a manner that best serves socio-economic
interests. After the introduction of the Energy Act, the development of hydropower
stations and PHES stations significantly decreased. The main reason was supply
exceeded power demand. Due to the combined factors of power producers being
required to ensure power supply in their designated areas and the limited market
flexibility, there was a drive towards investing in excess capacity. This is also one of
the reasons why Norwegian pumped storage is designed for seasonal pumping and
not for short-term pumping. Furthermore, due to Norway’s abundant hydropower
resources and large energy reserves, there is less need to invest in expensive system
services to maintain energy balance and grid stability. There has not been a need in
the Norwegian power system for PHES because of the access to power and energy
reserves due to the huge number of reservoirs in Norway [10].

1.3 The Nordic power system and power prices

The Nordic power system is closely interconnected. Norway has seven connections
to Sweden, one to Finland, four to Denmark, and one to The Netherlands called
NorNed. Recently, Norway built a connection to Germany called NordLink and a
connection to Great Britain called North Sea Link. The connections were established
in March 2021 and September 2022, respectively [11, 12]. The power exchange op-
erates such that power flows from areas with low prices to areas with high prices.
The advantages of the power trade are income to Norwegian power producers and
increased power supply for Norwegian consumers. If the power prices are lower in
the other trading countries, Norway will import electricity and conserve water in

                                                      3
reservoirs for future use [13].

The Norwegian and the Nordic power system is strongly influenced by the European
energy transformation through electricity prices, the EU Emissions Trading System,
power exchange and new regulations. Additionally, increase in demand and cheaper
technology influence the electricity prices.

The Nordic power consumption will see a substantial increase by 2050, driven by
electrification and increased industrial activity. Additionally, power production is
expected to double. Overall, in the Nordic countries, wind power will be the leading
supplier of power, with contributions from solar, hydro and nuclear power. These
trends are anticipated to continue from the current situation through 2050. Accord-
ing to Statnett [3], the Nordic power consumption will increase to 850 TWh in 2050,
this amount represents a doubling from the 400 TWh recorded in 2023. Further
on, in Norway, consumption is increasing significantly. This rise is driven by efforts
to reduce emissions through electrification and the development of new industries
and commercial activities. The Norwegian consumption will increase to 220 GWh
in 2050, a rise of 80 TWh from today’s consumption. Solar and hydropower will
have to increase their generation by 30 TWh by 2050. Moreover, offshore wind will
deliver 40 TWh by 2050 [3].

The power market in Europe will have high short-term volatility in the first five to
ten years. By 2035–2040, the expansion of more affordable flexibility will lower price
volatility [3]. According to Statnett, there are several factors for the high electricity
prices. The first reason is the high prices of gas and coal due to the ongoing war in
Ukraine. The war influences both the power market and the supply in the rest of
the world. The weather is also an important factor. As the reservoir level decrease,
the value of the remaining water increase , which correspondingly affects the spot
price. There has also been much debate on how much the two new cross-border con-
nections have impacted the power prices in Norway [13]. The North Sea Link is a
720 km deep-sea cable with a capacity of 1400 MW between the Suldal municipality
in Norway and the Newcastle area in England. NordLink is also a deep-sea cable
between Sirdal municipality in Norway and Wilster in Germany. The cable is 623 km
and has a capacity of 1400 MW [11, 12]. According to studies by Statnett, the price
impact of the two new power cables to Germany and England accounts for about
10% of the unusually high electricity prices since the autumn of 2021. Even without
these cables, electricity prices in Southern Norway would have been at record highs
[14]. Additionally, the prices of CO2 quotas have risen significantly from 2017 to the
present. In 2017, the cost of one ton of CO2 was approximately 5 euros, peaking
at around 97 euros per ton in 2023. As of 2024, the price stands at about 70 euros
per ton [15]. This increase has led to high power prices in Europe and in the Nordic
countries. CO2 quotas are an important instrument to make the expansion of wind

                                                      4
and solar power more profitable, and they enhance the competitive edge of renew-
able energy sources [13].

Norway is divided into five electricity price zones: Østlandet (NO1), Sørlandet
(NO2), Midt-Norge (NO3), Nord-Norge (NO4) and Vestlandet (NO5). The Nor-
wegian power market, driven by the weather and natural energy sources, varies
significantly across these regions. Price zones are primarily determined by bottle-
necks in the transmission network, where the capacity to transfer power is limited.
For instantnce, the transportation of electricity between Nord-Norge (NO4) and
Østlandet (NO1) passes through Sweden. However, between Vestlandet (NO5) and
Østlandet (NO1), the transmission capacity is substantial due to the high demand
for power transfer. This stems from extensive power production in western Norway
and comparatively lower production in the east. Moreover, the east, around Oslo,
is home to the majority of Norway’s population. The configuration of these price
zones not only indicates the balance of power scarcity or surplus but also ensures
the power system’s safe operation [16].

1.3.1 Hydropower scheduling

Due to significant price volatility and the increasing demand for large-scale energy
storage, there is a substantial need for the development of PHES systems. These
systems are capable of providing both short-term and long-term solutions. Given
the interconnectivity with the rest of Europe, questions are arising about the neces-
sity and profitability of PHES in Norway.

Hydropower is widely used in Norway. In 2023, there were 1781 hydropower stations
scattered throughout the country, with a significant concentration in the western
part. The Norges vassdrags- og energidirektorat, NVE, maps of power stations [17],
in Norway illustrate a complex network, where many plants utilize the same river
systems and are interconnected within the same system. Furthermore, a hydropower
system may comprise one or several plants, along with reservoirs and a complex net-
work of rivers. Numerous factors must be considered to achieve the most eﬀicient and
sustainable operation of hydropower system [18]. Additionally, hydropower stations
in Norway sell power to various electricity markets, including the spot and reserve
markets. These markets are expected to operate at shorter intervals. This means
that periods during prices are established, trades are executed, data is collected
and distributed will be shorter than current intervals. This will require hydropower
operators to respond more quickly to changes in demand. Consequently, producers
require advanced tools to support their decision-making process, ensuring that they
maximize the value extracted from the water resources stored in their reservoirs [18].

Scheduling hydropower generation is a specialized area of research. It involves solving

                                                      5
complex mathematical problems and requires technical knowledge of operating cru-
cial components such as turbines, generators, and water flow. Moreover, it includes
forecasting inflows and market prices, applicable to both long-term and short-term
time resolution [19].

The research organization SINTEF has developed several models to optimize hy-
dropower scheduling. The first model, EMPS, focuses on power system analysis and
long-term scheduling. The second, which is employed in this thesis, is ProdRisk, a
model for long-term hydropower scheduling optimization. The third is the SHOP
model, designed for short-term daily market engagement to ensure optimal opera-
tion. The majority of Nordic hydropower producers utilize this range of models to
ensure the eﬀicient management of their systems [18].

1.3.2 Day-ahead electricity prices and scenarios of future prices

Electricity price zones, also known as price areas, form the market framework for
trading electricity. Electricity prices are set for each specific area on an hourly basis
for the next 24 hours. These prices are not government-regulated but result from
the supply and demand for power within each price area, as reported to NordPool,
the Nordic power exchange. NordPool is owned by the Nordic system operators [20].

The day-ahead market at NordPool functions as an auction-based exchange where
electricity is physically delivered. In this market, producers and consumers submit
bids and offers, allowing NordPool to calculate an hourly price that balances these
factors. NordPool then sets the price for each hour of the next day. Every morn-
ing, customers place their bids in the auction for the following day, specifying the
amount of electricity in megawatts they are willing to buy or sell at predetermined
price levels (EUR/MWh) for each hour of the day [21].

Moreover, intraday markets facilitate the trading of electricity closer to the time of
delivery. These markets operate simultaneously with the day-ahead market. This
arrangement helps maintain a balanced supply and demand. As the production of
renewable energy, which can be intermittent, increases, there is a growing interest
in participating in the intraday markets. This interest originates from the challenges
that market participants face in maintaining balance after the day-ahead market
closes. The intraday market operates continuously, 24/7, allowing trading up until
one hour before delivery, and in some cases, even closer to the delivery hour. Prices
are determined on a first-come, first-served basis, with transactions prioritizing the
most favorable prices, the highest buying prices and the lowest selling prices [22].

As power systems increasingly rely on intermittent renewable energy sources, energy
supply becomes more unpredictable, leading to greater fluctuations in electricity

                                                      6
prices [3]. Furthermore, as previously mentioned, renewable sources pose challenges
in power planning due to their non-regulative nature. To enhance predictions of
power demand, it is crucial to employ market models that can provide forecasts
about future market conditions.

To accurately forecast future electricity prices, one can utilize the EMPS model. The
EMPS model, EFI’s Multi-area Power-market Simulator model, is a market model
[23]. The model is a software for optimizing and simulating hydrothermal power
systems. It is widely used in deregulated regions to forecast future spot prices. Ad-
ditionally, the model is adept at providing forecasts for various aspects such as
production, reservoir levels, water flow, consumption, rationing, import and export.
In the model each price scenario is linked to a specific weather year. The EMPS
model is used for forecasting future electricity prices [24].

1.3.3 Electricity Reserve Markets

As previously mentioned, one of the benefits of PHES, given its characteristics, is
its ability to regulate power-grid frequency. Frequency stability in the power system
is reliant on the rotating mass of synchronous machines connected to the grid. Most
of this rotating mass is located in hydro and thermal power plants.

The power system must maintain equilibrium at all times, ensuring that the sum
of production and import consistently matches the sum of consumption, export and
losses. Maintaining this balance is crucial to keep the system’s frequency around
50 Hz. This is a standard requirement of the Nordic power system. Reserves and
flexibility services, either through additional power production or consumption, are
activated when the frequency deviates from this norm. If the frequency rises above
50 Hz, it indicates that supply exceeds demand, conversely, if it falls below 50 Hz,
demand outstrips supply. Statnett, Norway’s transmission system operator is re-
sponsible for maintaining this balance. The most critical tool for managing these
imbalances is purchasing flexibility from the electricity reserve market. In Norway,
there are four types of reserves: primary, secondary, tertiary and fast frequency re-
serves. Each type has distinct aims and characteristics, but all are designed to help
maintain system balance.

The first line of response to frequency changes in the system is the fast frequency
reserves, also called Fast Frequency Reserves (FFR). These changes occur when the
systems’ inertia, which is the ability to resist changes, falls below a certain level. The
primary goal of FFR is to halt these frequency shifts. Following the initial response,
the primary reserves, known as Frequency Containment Reserves (FCR), take over.
The purpose of FCR is to stabilize the frequency at a new level. After stabilization,
the secondary reserves, or Automatic Frequency Restoration Reserves (aFRR), kick

                                                      7
in. Their objective is to return the frequency to its normal range. Finally, the ter-
tiary reserves, the Manual Frequency Restoration Reserves (mFRR), maintain the
balance until a new equilibrium is achieved in the power market [25].

Due to the widespread utilization of hydropower and significant reservoir capacity
in Norway, there is growing interest in exploring the necessity and profitability of
PHES systems in the country. Interest is particularly strong in integrating PHES
with existing hydropower facilities [26]. The Røldal-Suldal hydropower system is a
prominent example of such integration.

Given the extensive use of hydropower in Norway, coupled with enormous reservoir
capacities, there are increasing intrests about the necessity and profitability of PHES
systems in the country. Consequently, several conventional hydropower systems are
being evaluated for potential integration with PHES technologies. One such system
under consideration is the Røldal-Suldal hydropower system.

2 Literature review

In this section, a short review of current research on hydropower scheduling using
the SDDP method is provided, emphasizing its role in optimizing a hydropower
plant across various priorities.

The SDDP method is among several methodologies developed for optimizing the
scheduling of hydropower systems. In this thesis, this method has been used for hy-
dropower scheduling of the eastern part of RSK with the implementation of PHES.
The SDDP method has been implemented in several studies. For instance, in the
study Optimal Medium-Term Hydropower Scheduling Considering Energy and Re-
serve Capacity Markets, by Helseth et al. [23], the SDDP method was employed to
transition from solely focusing on the energy market to a combined approach that
includes both energy and reserve capacity markets. The purpose of the study is to
assess the expected changes in scheduling the water values when selling to both the
energy and reserve markets. The study considered three scenarios: in the first sce-
nario, the hydropower plant was optimized and scheduled solely for selling to the
energy market. Scenarios two and three were optimized and scheduled for selling
to both markets. These scenarios were compared. The study demonstrated that in-
troducing a reserve capacity market is a feasible addition to an existing scheduling
model.

Further on, in the article Approximating Ramping Constraints in Hydropower Schedul-
ing, by Helseth et al. [35], the SDDP method is used for ramping constraints on dis-
charge in medium-term hydropower scheduling. Similarly, in the HydroCen reports
Ramping Constraints in Hydropower Schedulling by Helseth et al. [36], and Environ-
mental Constraints in Seasonal Hydropower Scheduling by Helseth [34], both studies
use SDDP-based programs aimed to address ramping constraints and environmental
factors. In the first report [36], the authors aim to examine the integration of ramp-
ing limitations for particular water discharge included in the mid-term hydropower
scheduling. The study shows that the current model setup for ramping constraints,
which updates hourly or less frequently, overestimates how quickly water discharge
can be adjusted. To address this, the paper presents a new method that calculates
the costs of strictly following the rules for changing discharge rates, and it considers
the financial losses from not generating power at the best prices. However, making
the model more accurate in showing these changes would need a lot more computing
power, which is tough since the model is already complex. The new approach has
been tested and looks promising, providing results similar to a more detailed model
without needing a lot more computing time. In the second report [34], the aim of the
study is to identify constraints that are challenging to handle in existing models and
at the same time are considered essential for users of the model to present accurately.
The focus is on environmental constraints and on the SDDP method. Environmen-

                                                     11
tal constraints in the article refer to limits or boundaries that are put on reservoir
volumes and release plans. The study identified that environmental constraints can
be dealt with if state-dependent maximum discharge and virtual reservoirs can be
modelled.

The SDDP method serves as a valuable tool for forecasting future scenarios and
providing insights into potential outcomes. It offers hydropower producers indica-
tors for planning and operating their schemes eﬀiciently. These studies highlight the
power of SDDP by how it can simulate scenarios with various constraints, including
ramping limitations, environmental considerations and the reserve markets. These
scenarios are highly relevant for both the Nordic and the European power electricity
markets.

The method combines stochastic dynamic programming (SDP) and stochastic dual
dynamic programming (SDDP). The model has three stochastic variables, which
are the weekly inflow to each reservoir and the weekly average energy and reserve
capacity prices.

Today, hydropower producers mainly obtain their income from selling energy in the
day-ahead (spot) market. The active role of hydropower in balancing markets, due
to its flexibility, will in turn challenge the traditional methods of operation and
scheduling of hydropower systems. Deciding whether to use hydropower for electric-
ity production or as reserve capacity affects the strategic assessment and planning
of resource allocation. In the study Optimal Medium-Term Hydropower Scheduling
Considering Energy and Reserve Capacity Markets by Helseth et al. [23], the schedul-
ing process aims to optimize the anticipated profit from sales in both the spot and
the reserve capacity markets. The purpose of the study is to measure the expected
changes in scheduling and water values when selling to both markets. The method is
applied in a case study of a Norwegian watercourse containing seven reservoirs with
corresponding power plants. The hydropower system has a total capacity of 986 MW.

For this study, the scheduling period lasted 2 years. Scenarios for energy and reserve
capacity prices were sourced from the EMPS model. The EMPS model in the study
was executed using a system description of the Nordic power system, incorporating
80 years of historical inflow data, and applying reserve capacity constraints for each
price zone.

Three cases were considered in the study. Case A considered the energy market only
and therefore served as a reference case. Case B and Case C involve selling to both
the capacity and energy markets. The scenarios vary, with Case C incorporating
volume requirements. Consequently, the solution for Case C ensures that there are
suﬀicient water levels behind the turbines to facilitate the activation of reserves.

                                                     12
The study concludes that the method is suitable for solving hydropower scheduling
while maximizing the profit when selling to both the energy and capacity markets.
The method permits the sequential allocation of resources, with the sale of reserve
capacity occurring before energy sales. This study shows that introducing a reserve
capacity market is feasible as an addition to an existing scheduling model. The lim-
itation of the model is that the linearization error can have a considerable impact
on the expected profits and the overall operation of some systems. Linearization is
a process of simplifying complex relationships into linear ones. The error varies in
the specific case. While the model is not intended to provide precise operational
plans, the error from linearization might result in a misleading representation of the
system’s operation and profitability. The error’s impact becomes more significant
when considering spinning reserve capacity sales [23].

Moreover, the Nordic and European power markets are currently facing challenges
related to environmental focus. Environmental regulations (obligations) within the
hydropower sector are changing, focusing more on the sustainable use of water re-
sources. The focus is to balance the use of water on hydropower, irrigation, wa-
ter supply, flood control, and recreation. As a result, producers require scheduling
models that accurately and consistently represent environmental constraints. In the
article Approximating Ramping Constraints in Hydropower Scheduling by Helseth
et al. [35], the authors present the modeling of ramping constraints on discharge in
medium-term hydropower scheduling. Ramping constraints in the article refer to the
maximum allowed changes in water flows in a hydropower system. Ramping refers
to the speed at which a measured quantity varies within a physical system. The pur-
pose of the article is to present a new technique for approximating constraints on
discharge ramping by using a medium-term hydropower planning model. The used
model is founded on SDDP and stochastic market prices are addressed in an outer
layer through SDP. The method is a combination of SDDP and SDP programming.
This model considers inflow and power prices as external stochastic variables. The
model also includes ramping constraints and transition cuts (TC). With discrete
time, changes in the discharge rate from one time step to another are presumed to
be instantaneous. However, in reality such changes will take some time due to law
of physics. Time discretization in the model enhances the apparent capability of the
plant to adjust power generation in response to price changes. This means that be-
cause time is divided into intervals, the power plant’s ability to quickly adapt power
output in response to price fluctuations appears significantly better than it actually
is. Since power price in the model is set exogenous, it allows for the calculations
of costs that cover the expenses associated with changing the discharge rate. It’s
important to note that changes in discharge rate take time, and thus the transition
costs account for these adjustments.

The model was applied to the Norwegian hydropower system Røssåga in the northern

                                                     13
part of the country. The hydropower system consists of two large power stations with
large upstream reservoir capacity. The optimization time for the system is 156 weeks,
employing 56 time steps of 3 hours each within a week. The ramping constraints is 90
m3/s per 3-hour block. In the study, four cases were run. The cases were NoRamp,
Ramp, Ramp-TC20 and Ramp-TC100. The cases NoRamp and Ramp were without
and with ramping constraints respectively, and Ramp-TC20 and Ramp-TC100 were
with ramping constraints and included 20 and 100 transition cost cuts respectively.
The results show that the implementation of ramping constraints alone appears to
slightly decrease the maximum profit in the Ramp case compared to the NoRamp
case. Furthermore, the cases with TC cuts provide a notable decline in profit and
an increase in computation time.

Moreover, the reservoir trajectories, which are the water levels in the reservoir, show
that these were very similar for all the cases. However, in the Ramp_TC20 and
Ramp_TC100 scenarios, the application of TC, leads to a more stable operation of
the hydropower system due to reduced ramping frequency. There were fewer changes
or variations in the water flow rates (discharge) between the minimum discharge,
most eﬀicient operation point and maximum discharge. This implies a more con-
trolled and consistent operation of the hydropower system [35].

Although the article concludes that the study successful introduced a new method
for estimating ramping constraints costs in hydropower scheduling, there are op-
portunity for improvement when it comes to the calculations of TC cuts. The TC
cuts were determined based on the assumption that the direction of power generat-
ing ramping, either ramping up or down, is directly linked to changes in the power
prices between two consecutive time periods. Accordingly, if power prices increase
from one time step to the next, the model anticipates an increase in power genera-
tion. Opposite, a decrease in price leads to a decrease in generation. However, in a
complex system, this straightforward relationship may not always hold true. There
may be scenarios where it might be strategically or operationally beneficial to ramp
up power generation when prices are decreasing or to ramp down when prices are
increasing due to various reasons not directly tied to immediate price changes. That
being said, the TC cuts contribute to a more stable operation of the hydropower
system, which likely enhances eﬀiciency and could lead to a better economic outcome.

Further on, the increased supply of intermittent renewable energy will give control-
lable generation like hydropower plants possibilities to respond to these fluctuations.
At the same time, both physical and environmental demands associated with hy-
dropower operation are changing, which restrain hydropower production. Therefore,
hydropower operators must adapt their production schedules to reflect market price
trends while also accommodating new operational limitations.

The term “hydropower” refers to the process of generating power, typically in the
form of electricity, by harnessing the energy of falling water [37]. A conventional
hydropower plant consist of several components such as an inlet and a dam, a wa-
terway, a power station building, turbines, generators, a switchgear station and con-

from entering and damaging the turbine. The waterway consists of pipes, tunnels
and valves. The water makes the turbine rotate. The turbine is connected to the
generator, which transforms mechanical power into electrical power. The switchgear
station consists of switchgear and transformers that connect the station to the grid
and transforms the voltage up to the power-grid voltage. At last, the control system
includes SCADA/pls, sensors and computers [38].

Many PHES systems are integrated with traditional river-based hydroelectric sys-
tems [4]. PHES involves accumulating water in a reservoir by pumping it uphill

the hydropower plant during periods of high power demand [2]. An illustration of
PHES is shown in figure 3. Moreover, there are two types of PHES: open-loop and
closed-loop. Open-loop systems are defined as a plant that is permanently linked
to a naturally occurring water source. A key benefit of open-loop systems is their
ability to leverage existing water resources and infrastructure, reducing the need
for extensive land use and construction. Closed-loop systems, on the contrary, are
defined as not continuously connected to a naturally flowing water feature. A key
benefit for closed loop systems is that they are less environmentally disruptive than
open loop systems because they use less water and do not interfere with natural
bodies of water [4].

The technical characteristics of PHES systems provide multiple benefits for the
power grid. This including energy storage, enhanced grid stability, seamless inte-
gration of renewables and effective load balancing. PHES systems offer large-scale
energy storage abilities. This makes PHES optimal for storing surplus power gener-
ated beyond current needs and releasing it during periods of high demand. PHES
systems respond quickly to fluctuations in supply and demand, thus effectively main-
taining grid stability. The capability of PHES systems to store and release energy as
needed makes them highly effective at integrating renewable energy sources, such as
wind and solar. These energy sources are intermittent by nature, thereby facilitat-
ing their board integration into the grid. PHES systems are excellent tools for load
balancing, enhancing the power grid’s overall eﬀiciency by shifting electricity gener-
ation from periods of low demand to times of high demand. Also, PHES systems are
known for long operational lifetimes and limited environmental footprint compared
to other renewable energy sources. Lastly, PHES systems supply crucial ancillary
services. These are, e.g., frequency regulations, voltage support, load shifting and
system resilience [4].

PHES systems utilize a range of pumps and turbines to effectively store and convert
energy [4]. The design of a pump-turbine primarily features two main configura-
tions. The first and most widely employed configuration is reversible machine sets,
consisting of a motor generator and a reversible pump turbine [39]. The Francis
reversible pump turbine stands as the most prevalent choice [40]. Based on the ro-
tation direction, the reversible machine functions either as a pump or a turbine [41].
The second configuration is a ternary system, which consists of a motor generator,
a separate turbine and a pump. The turbine is typically Pelton or Francis. When
functioning as two distinct hydraulic machines, the motor generator can maintain
the same rotational direction in both modes of operation [41]. Ternary units are most
commonly utilized for PHES with large head pressures, that is, between 400–600 m.
Reversible turbines are used for heights lower than 400 m. The benefits of reversible
turbines include their relatively compact size compared to ternary units, as well as
lower investment, maintenance, and operational costs. On the other hand, ternary
systems offer higher eﬀiciency for both turbines and pumps, as each component is
individually optimized for its specific function [40].

Pturbine = Q · ρwater · g · H · ηturbinemode.                            (1)

The density of water ρwater is 1000 [kg/m3]. The gravitational acceleration, g is 9.81
m/s2. H refers to the head, the vertical distance the water falls. The head varies
based on the water levels in the two reservoirs involved. Turbine mode eﬀiciency
ηturbinemode takes into account the difference between the potential energy in the
water at the upper reservoir and the electrical power output the plant deliver to the
grid. The turbine mode is the operation of running water through the turbine to
generate power.

The turbine mode eﬀiciency is given by equation (2)[46]:

ηturbinemode = ηwaterway · ηturbine · ηgen · η · ηtrans.                 (2)

The turbine mode eﬀiciency, ηturbinemode accounts for all the loss contributions.
ηwaterway represents the energy losses attributed to the roughness of waterways,
which can vary considerably. As water passes through the turbine, some of the po-
tential energy is converted into heat. The power loss in the turbine is represented by
ηturbine and is between 0.8–0.95. Additionally, the generator and transformer expe-
rience minimal losses, accoun ted for by ηgen and ηtrans, which typically range from
0.8 to 0.95 [37].

21
The power consumed by the pump or by the plant in pump mode is given by equation

(3) [46]:

                      Ppump          =  Q  · ρwater · g ·  H  ,                    (3)
                                           ηpumpmode

where Q represents the volume of water pumped from the lower reservoir to the up-

per reservoir, measured in [m3/s] and correlates directly with the power consumption

of the pump. H is the head in this context. It denotes the same vertical distance

as in equation (1), but it measures the ascent of water during pumping. ηpumpmode
quantifies the eﬀiciency loss, representing the difference between the energy used to

lift the water and the potential energy stored by the water elevation. This indicates

that the energy required to lift the water exceeds the potential energy gained by the

water [37].

The pump mode eﬀiciency is given by equation (4) [46]:

                      ηpumpmode = ηwaterway · ηpump · ηgen · η · ηtrans.           (4)

The pump mode eﬀiciency also takes into account losses from the waterway, gener-
ator and transformer. The ηpump is the eﬀiciency of the pump itself.

The eﬀiciency for one cycle is given by equation (5):

                      ηcycle = ηturbinemode · ηpumpmode                            (5)

The eﬀiciency of the cycle, also known as round-trip eﬀiciency is determined by
multiplying the eﬀiciency of the turbine mode, where electricity is generated, by
the pump mode eﬀiciency, which is the process of water being pumped back to the
upper reservoir [1].

Plant Kvanndal simulated in the thesis, is a combination of a turbine and a pump.
These components never operate simultaneously. The overall eﬀiciency for the plant
is given by equation (6):

           ηplant  =  (ηturbinemode  · %tturbinemode) + (ηpumpmode  · %tpumpmode)  (6)
                                       %tturbinemode · %tpumpmode

The ηplant represents the plant eﬀiciency over a period, which in this thesis is one
year. The terms %tturbinemode and %tpumpmode are the percentage of time that the
turbine and pump respectively are operational throughout the year.

The energy production E to a PHES station is given by equation (7) [46]:

                                     E = Pavg · t                                  (7)

Pavg is the average power output and t is the time period. The unit for energy E is
given in [J].

3.3 Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP)

SDDP is an optimization method used in hydropower scheduling. The strategy aims

to eﬀiciently allocate water power, accounting for the uncertainty in inflow and mar-

ket price. The method aims to optimize resource allocation, thereby enhancing the

potential of hydropower station planning. The SDDP algorithm employs a dynamic

programming approach where future costs are modeled by hyperplanes, often re-

ferred to as cuts. This relies on linear programming. A crucial requirement is that

the problem must be either linear or convex. The optimization time is divided into

discrete time steps, indexed by t, and employs a finite time horizon T . A typical

T for medium-term scheduling is 2 to 3 years. The time steps t are generally one

week in length [5, 47]. The time optimization within a week may vary from one hour

resolution to three hour resolution. Moreover, for a power station, it is assumed that

its power generation P :

                                   P = f (Q)h/h0.                                        (9)

Function f (Q) is a piecewise linear function, tailored for each power station. In the
SDDP algorithm, f (Q) must be concave. The variable h represents the head and h0
is the nominal head. The SDDP algorithm cannot directly accommodate variable
head values. The head correction factor h/h0 in the equation must be employed
with estimated values for h. For many Norwegian power plants, this is a reasonable
approximation. This is because the head is typically quite larger relative to head
variations.

The remainder of the theory underlying the SDDP algorithm is detailed in the ar-
ticle An Algorithm for stochastic medium-term hydrothermal scheduling under spot
price uncertainty by Gjelsvik et al. [47] and the chapter Long- and Medium-term
Operations Planning and Stochastic Modelling in Hydro-dominated Power Systems
Based on Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming by Gjeldsvik et al. [5]. The appli-
cation of the SDDP method is described in the Methodology section 4.1.

4 Methodology

This section provides an explanation of the SDDP method used in the ProdRisk
program, as well as an overview of ProdRisk itself. Additionally, the implementa-
tion of PHES is discussed. Finally, the origin of the price scenario is explained.

The program used for simulating the hydropower system is ProdRisk [33]. ProdRisk
is a program for medium-term scheduling in hydropower, which considers both in-
flow and power price stochastic variables. The program is based on a combination of
stochastic dynamic programming (SDP) and stochastic dual dynamic programming
(SDDP) [33].

4.1 SDDP method

The SDDP method was developed in Brazil and is a huge breakthrough in the plan-
ning and optimization of operational strategies for hydropower systems by Pereira
et al. [48]. Further on, Gjeldsvik et al. [5] emphasize in their chapter Long- and
Medium-term Operations Planning and Stochastic Modelling in Hydro-dominated
Power Systems Based on Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming from Handbook of
Power systems I [49]: “Using SDDP technics, however, allows stochastic optimization
for multi-reservoir systems, which means that more realistic and detailed models can
be dealt with.”

Determining the best operational strategies for a hydropower system poses a signif-
icant challenge when dealing with a power system that heavily relies on hydropower
[5]. In the Nordic power market, hydropower constitutes a substantial portion of the
power production, especially in Norway where hydropower contributed 87.3% of the
energy production in 2023 [9]. This makes SDDP an excellent tool for hydropower
producers aiming to maximize profits in their hydropower operations [5].

SDDP is used for optimizing hydropower scheduling. SDDP is highly capable in han-
dling the uncertainty inherent in both water inflows and to reservoirs and electricity
market prices. This is very important for hydropower systems, where future water
availability and market conditions can be unpredictable. The method also optimizes
multi-reservoir systems. This provides a more realistic and detailed model of a hy-
dropower system. For large-scale systems with interconnected reservoirs and power
stations, such as those found in the northern market, this complexity is particularly
important. The algorithm improves the decision-making by incorporating stochastic
variables, inflow and price, into the scheduling process. With this, SDDP helps to
make more informed and eﬀicient decisions regarding water release from reservoirs,
electricity generation, and market participation. The method is useful for medium
and long-term operational planning in hydropower systems. This involves determin-

                                                     24
ing the optimal strategy for water reservoir management over several months to
some years. It’s balancing between energy production and maintaining adequate
water levels.

The SDDP uses a dynamic programming technique that iteratively improves the
operation strategy. Future costs are represented by ‘hyperplanes’ and the algorithm
works through forward and backward recursion to find optimal solutions for hy-
dropower scheduling. One complete iteration of the algorithm consists of a forward
and a backward recursion [5]. As stated by the ProdRisk portal [33]: “The purpose
of a forward simulation is to find a set of reservoir trajectories and compute the
expected profit (lower bound). The purpose of a backward recursion is to compute
cuts for each stage that describe the relationship between the future expected profit
seen from that stage and the reservoir level at the end of that stage. The backward
recursion also provides the future expected profit seen from the beginning of the
planning horizon, which provides an upper bound. After ProdRisk has reached con-
vergence or the maximum number of iterations has been reached, a final simulation
is performed. The final simulation is a final forward simulation using the strategy
(represented by cuts) created.” ProdRisk simplifies the overall scheduling challenge
by breaking it down into weekly decision problems. Each of these problems is struc-
tured as a problem of linear programming.”

Due to the significant reliance on hydropower, market prices can be greatly influ-
enced by hydrological conditions, making it important to consider the variability of
water inflow. The model is designed for a system operated by a single power company
that acts as a price taker, unable to impact the market prices. Consequently, the
market price is treated as an external stochastic variable. SDDP is combined with
ordinary stochastic dynamic programming to handle price modeling as an exogenous
stochastic variable. A forecast of the spot price is generated through simulations con-
ducted with the EMPS model. EMPS is a long-term model spanning multiple areas
and years, using the spot price as an intrinsic variable. In the EMPS model, each
price scenario is linked to a specific weather year [5].

4.2 ProdRisk and the model of Eastern RSK

In the thesis, the Prodrisk API is utilized through the ‘ProdriskCore’ class, which is
implemented in Python. The code for this was developed in Jupyter Notebook. The
primary concept of the API is to provide essential functions and simple data struc-
tures to facilitate data exchanges with ProdRisk. Its adaptability allows for easy
integration with multiple programming languages. Furthermore, the object types
and attributes within the API are designed to provide specific functionalities, em-
powering developers to perform certain operations or actions effectively using the

                                                     25
software’s API [50].

In Prodrisk there are five primary object types that can be modified by the user.
The five types are modules, pumps, inflowSeries, areas and settings. The module
object represents a combination of a reservoir and a plant, featuring attributes that
detail the physical characteristics of the system. These are the reservoir and plant
attributes. The InflowSeries object contains water inflow data, while the area ob-
ject represents the entire hydropower system. General model details are kept in the
setting object, e.g., the number of SDDP iterations and price periods, to mention
some. In a ProdRisk simulation, there is only one setting and one area object, but
it may contain several modules, inflowSeries and pumps. Each module is required
to connect to an inflowSeries using an identification number and only a single in-
flowSeries can be connected to multiple modules.

The model of Eastern part of Røldal Suldal hydropower system consists of four mod-
ules. The two initial modules are each composed of a single reservoir: Isvann and
Holmevann, respectively. The third module integrates the Kvandandalsfoss reservoir
and the Suldal II power plant, while the fourth encompasses the Sandvann reser-
voir and the Kvanndal power plant. This collective area is referred to as NO2. The
topology is arranged such that the water follows distinct paths from Isvann and
Holmevann to the Sandvann reservoir. From there, it flows to the Kvandal power
station, then proceeds to the Kvandalsfoss reservoir, before passing through the Sul-
dal II power station and ending at Suldalsvannet. Moreover, modules one, two, and
four are linked to inflowSeries 1, whereas module four connects to inflowSeries 2.
This implies that the Kvandalsfoss module includes extra rivers feeding into it.

Each module’s reservoir is characterized by multiple attributes that provide infor-
mation about it. The attributes are: max reservoir volume, an ID number which
is used to connect the module to an inflow series, mean regulated inflow per year,
mean unregulated inflow per year, net nominal head used, the outlet line in masl,
the relation between volume in Mm3 and head in masl of the reservoir and the start
volume of the reservoir. Also, modules three and four have power plants, which are
also characterized by multiple attributes that provide information about the plants.
The attributes are plant name, the PQ curve (power P , discharge Q curve) which
shows the relation between power and discharge, the energy equivalent constant, the
maximum discharge in m3/s, the maximum production in MW and the maximum
bypass constant. All of these elements were established in the model developed by
SINTEF. A modification was made in the model when implementing a pump in the
system.

A pump was implemented between reservoir Sandvann which belongs to Kvanndal
power station and reservoir Kvanndalsfoss which belongs to Suldal II power station.

                                                     26
The pump was built according to the attributes: maximum and minimum pumping
height, maximum and minimum discharge, average power and pump topology in
relation to the reservoirs. The position of the watercourse was at plant Kvandal,
it pumped water from the reservoir Kvandalsfoss to the reservoir Sandvann. The
pump is a separate pump which uses the same pipelines as the turbine. The maxi-
mum pumping height was set to the height difference between the lowest reservoir
level of Kvandalsfoss and the highest reservoir level of Sandvann, and the smallest
pumping height was set to the highest reservoir level of Kvandalsfoss and the lowest
level of Sandvann. The discharge was calculated using equation (3), by solving it for
Q. The eﬀiciency for the pump was set to ηpump = 0.8. The simulation was run with
different pump sizes and discharges. The pumps sizes was Ppump = 10 MW, 20 MW,
30 MW and 40 MW.

When running the model, the simulation interval has to be in whole number of
weeks. For the thesis, the number of weeks was set to 208, totaling four years. The
timestamp was set to 7. January 2030. Prodrisk Core does not maintain a precise
correspondence with actual dates and times. The starting time is determined based
on the price scenarios and the explanation is provided further down. In the simu-
lations a three-hour time resolution is applied, whereas the actual market operates
with a one-hour resolution. The input and output data have 56 three-hour time steps
per week.

The number of SDDP iterations was 50 which means it was done 50 forward sim-
ulations and 50 backwards recursion. Increasing the number of iterations leads to
improved accuracy in the model. Further on, the quantity of inflow scenarios and
price scenarios must be the same. Each price scenario aligns with its respective in-
flow scenario in sequence. The number of scenarios in the thesis was 30. The time
duration of each scenario needs to be at least the length of the simulation interval.
This means that each inflow and price period in the simulation should cover 208
weeks. The inflow scenarios are required to span a minimum of 52 weeks.

The model ran in serial mode, that is, the simulated scenarios are constructed as
sequential years. This means that the reservoir level after 52 weeks in scenario 1
becomes the initial reservoir level in scenario 2. The linking of scenarios follows a
cyclic pattern. Scenario 1 initiates from the concluding state of the last scenario.
In this approach, the initial reservoir level for one scenario is set based on the
final reservoir level from the preceding scenario in the previous iteration. Thus, the
aforementioned 30 scenarios are in serial mode and therefore 30 sequential years of
simulation.
As previously mentioned, the simulation interval was for four years. When running
in serial simulation, the input scenarios need to be created in accordance with the
same cyclic coupling assumed by the algorithm. This means that the first scenario

                                                     27
consists of years 1, 2, 3 and 4; scenario 2 consists of years 2, 3, 4 and 5; the last
scenario, number 30 consists of years 30, 1, 2 and 3. Results are shown for 52 weeks
per scenario.

4.3 Price scenarios

The price series dataset originates from the ‘Low Emission’ scenario described in
the report Power Price Scenarios by Schäffer et al. [51] from the HydroCen research
center. The only difference is the entire price series was scaled up to have an average
value of ca. 210 EUR/MWh, which was the average day-ahead price in NO2 in 2022.
The goal is a better understanding of price features and the factors influencing prices
in 2030. In the report, two price scenarios are defined. A reference scenario and a
low emission scenario for 2030. The low-emission scenario is designed to represent
the latest goals and aspirations for northern Europe’s power system in 2030. In
the scenario, a greater portion of power production relies on wind and solar energy
sources. The reference scenario is built around a renewable energy target of 27% in
the energy mix by 2030. The study utilizes the EMPS model with a 2030 European
dataset. This dataset encompasses 53 areas, each modeled in detail [51].

6 Discussion

The research questions explored in this thesis were How does employing PHES with
the SDDP method impact the eastern Randal-Suldal hydropower systems operations?
How do pump size variations affect the Kvanndal power plant’s eﬀiciency and per-
formance? Consequently, this study delves into the operational effects of PHES on
the hydropower system, specifically examining the implications of utilizing pumps
of varying sizes. Additionally, it explores the economic impacts of integrating PHES
and identifies the most financially viable solutions for implementation

In the thesis, a PHES was placed at the Kvanndal power station, utilizing Sandvann
as the upstream reservoir and Kvanndalfoss as the downstream reservoir. One of the
reasons for positioning the pump at this location is to make use of the water from
Kvanndalfoss. Kvanndalfoss is significantly smaller reservoir, with 1.6 Mm3, in con-
trast to Sandvann’s 66 Mm3 and Holmevann’s 96 Mm3. Kvanndalfoss receives water
from Kvanndal and adjacent streams. During flood season, a considerable amount
of water is spilled as the reservoir lacks the capacity to contain all the floodwater.

In March 2024, Lyse DA submitted a concession application for the Kvanndal 2
PHES. In the application, it is noted that the significant unregulated inflow in Kvan-
dalsfoss leads to considerable water and potential energy losses in the eastern part
of RSK. During the flood season, the Suldal power station is forced to operate at low
power prices to prevent water loss. Furthermore, the concession application reveals
that a primary motivation for constructing a PHES is the growing frequency of hours
when energy prices are near zero or negative. These negative energy prices signify
a surplus of production, which in turn yields low societal benefit. The application
further highlights the potential advantages of PHES. With PHES in the system, it
enables the storage of water in reservoirs for use during periods of high consumption
and low output from solar and wind power sources. Thus, PHES has the capabil-
ity to transform energy with low societal benefit into energy with greater societal
value. Based on the motivation, it can be inferred that economic considerations are
the primary drivers behind the concession application. Also, in general, profitability
largely dictates whether a hydropower producer should consider expanding or mod-
ify production. This is also evident in the master thesis, where the SDDP method
has been utilized for optimization based on revenue. From the optimization, the pri-
mary focus has been to identify which pump size generates the maximum revenue.

The concession application confirms that PHES could be profitable in the eastern
part of RSK. However, the scenario presented in the application differs from that in
the thesis. Lyse DA intends to use the Kvanndalsfoss reservoir as the downstream
reservoir and Holmevann as the upstream reservoir. Holmevann has a regulative
height of 10 m from 958 masl to 948 masl, but in the concession they want to ex-

pand this with an additional 5 m. The choice of placement for the pump appears
wise, given the substantial reservoir capacity of Holmevann. In this thesis, one of the
primary emphasis was examining how implementing a pump to the existing Kvan-

plant with PHES. That being said, Sandvann also possesses a significant regulatory
reservoir capacity as it can regulate between elevations of 950 masl and 924 masl,
which is a 26 m range. This substantial regulatory height provides the reservoir with
good conditions to function as an upper reservoir for a PHES.

In this thesis, the ProdRisk program was utilized, which employs the SDDP algo-
rithm. This method optimizes and schedules power production and revenue by taking
into account stochastic variables such as inflow and power prices. The optimization
is based on the data sets of price scenarios and inflow patterns. The dataset detail-
ing inflow comes from NVE’s database and represents local inflow data spanning the
area from 1981 to 2010. This 30-year period encompasses a wide variety of inflow
conditions, likely reflecting a significant range of variability across different years.

The power price dataset used comes from the HydroCen study Power Price Scenar-
ios by Schäffer et al. [51]. As noted in the method section 4.3, this dataset specifically
draws from the Low Emission scenario, which aligns with the latest climate targets
for Northern Europe set for 2030. This scenario anticipates a significant increase in
power production from wind and solar sources. In contrast, the reference scenario is
based on the EU’s target of achieving 27% renewable energy in the European energy
mix by 2030.

The study utilized the EMPS model, analyzing a dataset projected for Europe in
2030. This dataset encompasses 53 detailed modeled areas and three exchange con-
nections, with simulations spanning 58 years and capturing 56 three-hour time steps
per week. The analysis includes calculations of water value based on 58 historical
weather years. According to the report, the integration of renewables in the Low
Emission scenario contributes to increased unpredictability and larger price fluctu-
ations. This impacts the power prices significantly.

The EMPS model is widely used for forecasting future spot prices in the Nordic

                                                     44
power systems, and has also garnered interest in Asia. However, this market model
primarily relies on historical data to make predictions, which limits its ability to
forecast extreme price variations. Consequently, the model tends to underestimate
the fluctuations in extreme prices, a significant limitation since these extremes are
challenging to predict. This underestimation could lead to speculations that power
producers might actually expect higher revenues than the model suggest.

The price series in the dataset has been scaled up to have an average value of 210
EUR/MWh. This is the average day-ahead price in 2022 for zone NO2. This is con-
siderably higher compared to the average of both previous and future years [52].
Additionally, the price scenario article predicts that the average power price in 2030
for the Low Emission scenario in southern Norway will be significantly lower at 39
EUR/MWh. This discrepancy suggests that the price data in the dataset has been
scaled up substantially, maintaining the same fluctuations but at an elevated level.
Consequently, this adjustment may lead to expectations of higher revenue than what
might realistically occur. The values depicted in figure A.11 in the appendix indi-
cate unrealistically high revenue. However, figure 16 compares these values relative
to the no pump simulation, showing that the proportions remain consistent even if
the price series were scaled up to a higher average price.

Further examination of the high revenues depicted in figure A.11 in the appendix
reveals that the revenue is approximately 239 million euros without the pump and
around 245 million euros with a 40 MW pump. Despite these unusually high num-
bers, they do not conclusively determine the profitability of investing in a pump
or the optimal size of the pump, for several reasons: Firstly, ProdRisk does not
account for maintenance costs, which were not included in the simulations. Had
maintenance been considered, which implies operational pauses, the projected rev-
enue would likely be lower. Additionally, when contemplating the investments in
a pump, producers must consider associated costs such as new tunnels, downtime
at the power station and other expenses related to the investment in PHES. While
investing in a pump could potentially increase annual electricity revenue, there is
no guarantee that such an investment would be profitable.

Moreover, considering revenue, in 2022 the effective ground rent tax was set to 45%,
resulting in an overall marginal tax rate of 67%. Consequently, power producers
exceeding a certain size are required to pay 67% of their profits in taxes [53]. This is
a significant factor, as it substantially reduces the post-tax earnings of hydropower
producers. Additionally, between 2022 and 2023, the government introduced a high-
price contribution for hydropower, setting the duty at 23% for any monthly price
that exceeded NOK 70 øre/kWh. This meant that when spot prices surpassed 70
øre/kWh, producers were obligated to pay 23% of their revenue generated above
this threshold [54]. Although the government decided to abolish the high-price con-

                                                     45
tributions in late 2023, it remains important to recognize that the exploitation of
Norway’s natural resources is subject to heavy taxation [55].

Moving on to the methodology, the SDDP method has been employed by numer-
ous power companies for several years, as noted by Gjelsvik et al. [5]. Despite its
widespread adoption, the method is not without drawbacks. As highlighted by sev-
eral authors, it requires a long computation time. Furthermore, the power system
in the thesis was scheduled primarily for revenue optimization. However, the model
failed to incorporate maintenance time, which significantly impacts revenue. Within
ProdRisk, there is a feature for both the module and the pump named ‘maintenance’.
This attribute makes it possible to implement capacity that is not available due to
maintenance. Utilizing this attribute would have yielded a more realistic revenue
outcome.

Further on, the primary limitation of the method is its lack of condition-dependent
restrictions. This limitation became apparent when calculating pump eﬀiciency us-
ing equation (3). The resulting values are displayed in figure A.4 in the appendix
and show that the eﬀiciency values are around 0.77 across all pump sizes. Initially,
these values were set at 0.8 during the implementation of the pump. However, when
calculating the eﬀiciencies, the head H was determined based on reservoir levels
and discharge by using extrapolation. The power values and discharge values were
determined from the simulations. According to the physical formulas provided in
the theory section 3.2, the eﬀiciency should have been the same as initially given.
Further on, SDDP simulations are executed twice; initially, the head coeﬀicients are
generated during the first run. These coeﬀicients are then applied in the subsequent
and final simulation. This process is defined as the initial run and the final run,
respectively. This dual-run approach is necessary because condition-dependent re-
strictions mean that initially, there is no established head between the reservoir and
the power plants. This is developed during the 50 main iterations, which consist of 50
forward simulations and 50 backward recursions. In the initial run, trajectories of the
reservoir are established, from which the head is calculated. The condition-dependent
restrictions in the program could explain the observed deviations in pump eﬀiciency.

In summary, implementing a pump in the eastern part of RSK is projected to in-
crease income from electricity sales compared to a system without a pump. A larger
plant equipped with a bigger pump has a longer operation time, both for the turbine
and pump. This is particularly notable during years of low inflow. While a larger
pump may have lower eﬀiciency, it enables the power plant to generate more elec-
tricity.

Building a new power station requires significant intervention in nature, involving
tasks such as laying pipes and blasting mountains. In contrast, adding a pump to
an existing power station is less invasive. However, considering the eastern system
as a whole, the main limitation is the Kvanndalfoss reservoir, which has minimal
capacity. To address the issue of an overfilled reservoir, one could either install a
pump between Sandvann and Kvandalsfoss, as discussed in this thesis, or add a tur-
bine at Suldal II to increase the discharge rate. Yet, installing a pump alone does
not offer the same flexibility as a PHES system would. Numerous combinations and
simulations need to be run to determine the most profitable solution. It is believable
that Lyse DA has extensively used a scheduling optimization program for various
simulations, concluding that constructing a new PHES between Kvanndalfoss and
Holmevann is the most lucrative option. Moreover, it is plausible that their analy-

additional opportunities.

In Norway, most PHES systems operate seasonally, utilizing the pump during the
summer months and the turbine during the winter. Additionally, several pumping

                                                     47
stations draw water from lateral reservoirs. By setting the equations (1) and (3)
equal to each other and canceling out all components except for the heads and
eﬀiciencies, it becomes possible to determine the necessary height of the pump to
balance the energy used for pumping with the energy generated by the turbine. This
means the pumping head Hpumpmode is set to be equivalent to the turbine mode head
Hturbinemode, calculated as the height of the waterfalls multiplied by the product
of the eﬀiciencies of the turbine ηturbinemode and the pump ηpumpmode. Pumping
from lateral reservoirs consumes less energy than pumping from lower reservoirs
for the plant. This makes it particularly cost-effective during periods of low power
prices. Considering the simulation with the 40 MW pump, the turbine eﬀiciency
ηturbinemode was 0.904 and the pump eﬀiciency was ηpumpmode is 0.773. If the turbine
head Hturbinemode is set at the nominal head of 314.8 m, the required pumping head
is 220.0 m. This indicates that the pumping height needs to be 70% of the turbine
mode head for the energy used in pumping to equal the energy generated by the
turbine. It is also worth considering a scenario where the lateral reservoir pumping
head is only 50% of the turbine mode head, which would mean even less energy is
required for pumping compared to what the turbine generates. This could be signif-
icantly more profitable than pumping from the lower reservoir. The calculations is
found in section A.2 in the Appendix.

Lastly, it would be interesting to explore how the eastern part of RSK with PHES
would perform in selling power on both the spot and reserve markets. With the
flexibility and storage capacity that PHES provides, it potentially offers significant
opportunities for selling power to the reserve market. According to the study by
Helseth et al. Optimal Medium-Term Hydropower Scheduling Considering Energy
and Reserve Capacity Markets [23], introducing a reserve capacity market alongside
the spot market proved to be profitable.

Furthermore, Statnett [56] indicates that the demand for fast response reserves FFR
is particularly high during periods of low load and production, especially when there
is a substantial proportion of wind power and imports. In such situations, PHES
could serve as an effective tool by utilizing the pump for consumption and harness-
ing energy from wind farms. Statnett emphasizes that these conditions are most
commonly observed during the summer, particularly in years with low hydrological
levels. This highlights even more the importance and necessity of PHES. In years
when hydrological levels are low, a pump can enable the reuse of water, enhancing
resource eﬀiciency.

This further extends to considerations of climate change. According to the Meteoro-
logical Institute, when comparing the weather period from 1991-2020 to the earlier
period of 1961-1990, there is a 7% increase in annual precipitation. Additionally,
rainfall is heavier on wet days. Given the limited reservoir capacity of Kvandalsfoss,

                                                     48
it is likely that the turbines at Suldal II will not be able to manage all the water
inflow. However, if a pump were installed to transfer water to either Holmevann or
Sandvann, much more of the water could be utilized, minimizing losses to bypass
flows [57].
As highlighted in the introduction, PHES is a crucial regulatory renewable energy
source for the future. It allows for the later use of wind and solar energy, ensuring
that their energy is not wasted. As discussed earlier, PHES can transform energy of
low societal benefit into energy of higher societal value. This is particularly valuable
during years of low inflow, as the flexibility provided by PHES can significantly
benefit society.
Lastly, unlike wind and solar power, PHES is a renewable energy source that can
be regulated. Furthermore, since PHES has been developed alongside conventional
hydropower systems, converting existing hydropower stations to PHES has a smaller
impact on nature compared to the development of new wind and solar power farms.

                                                     49
7 Conclusion

The thesis explored the operational impacts of integrating PHES into the Røldal-
Suldal hydropower system and assessed how different pump sizes affected system
performance. It also evaluated the economic benefits of PHES integration, focusing
on identifying the pump size that maximized revenue and offered the greatest eco-
nomic advantage.

In the eastern part of RSK, larger pump sizes result in higher revenue. With a bigger
pump, the operational time for both the turbine and the pump increases, thereby
boosting production. A larger pump moves and utilizes more water, which accounts
for the extended operational times. However, with higher discharge, the turbine ef-
ficiency decreases. Specifically, the turbine eﬀiciency with the 40 MW pump was
lower compared to turbines equipped with smaller pumps. To determine the prof-
itability of investing in a pump in the eastern part of RSK, further calculations of
investment costs are necessary. However, the simulation with the biggest pump, 40
MW achieved the highest revenue.

Incorporating PHES into the energy system allows for the storage of water in reser-
voirs, which can be utilized during periods of high consumption and low output from
solar and wind power sources. As such, PHES can transform energy of low societal
benefit into energy with greater societal value. A key advantage of PHES is its flex-
ibility and the supply security it offers. As today’s power market transitions from
fossil fuels to renewable energy sources, this shift presents challenges, particularly
because solar and wind are intermittent energy sources. As the European power
market transitions from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources, it faces challenges
due to the intermittent nature of solar and wind energy. Norway, with its vast reser-
voir capacity, serves as Europe’s green battery. Norwegian shares a common power
market with the other Nordic countries. The Nordic market is increasingly integrat-
ing with the broader European power market. Therefore, it is crucial to have PHES
systems in place to store surplus energy for later use. PHES offers multiple benefits
to the power grid, including energy storage, enhanced grid stability, seamless inte-
gration of renewable energies, and effective load balancing.

PHES systems offer large-scale energy storage abilities. PHES systems respond
quickly to fluctuations in supply and demand, thus effectively maintaining grid sta-
bility. PHES systems effectively integrate intermittent renewable energy sources
like wind and solar by storing and releasing energy as needed, thus facilitating their
broader integration into the grid. PHES systems are good tools for load balancing,
enhancing the power grid’s overall eﬀiciency by shifting electricity generation from
periods of low demand to times of high demand.

                                                     50
With the numerous advantages that PHES offers, it is clear that this technology is
a key driver in addressing the climate crisis. By enabling a shift towards entirely
renewable energy sources, PHES can play a crucial role in achieving the goal of
limiting global warming to 1.5ºC.

A.2 Calculations

Calculations of the required head from lateral reservoirs to equate the energy used
for pumping with the energy generated by the turbine. To calculate the required
head, equation (1) and equation (3) are equated which is shown in equation (10):

                                                 Pturbine = Ppump.                                 (10)

The parameters for calculating the power of both the turbine and the pump are
incorporated into equation (11):

            Q · ρwater  ·g       · Hturbinemode  · ηturbinemode  =   Q · ρwater · g · Hpumpmode .  (11)
                                                                              ηpumpmode

The components Q, ρwater and g are eliminated, simplifying the expression to equa-

tion (12):

                                 Hturbinemode    · ηturbinemode      =  Hpumpmode .                (12)
                                                                        ηpumpmode

Resolving equation (12) for Hpumpmode and substituting the turbine eﬀiciency,
ηturbinemode to 0.904 and the pump eﬀiciency, ηpumpmode to 0.773. Also, setting the

                                                 68
turbine mode head Hturbinemode to the nominal head of 314.8 m. This leads to equa-
tion (14):

Hpumpmode = 314.8m · 0.904 · 0.773.                    (13)

By multiplying the eﬀiciencies together, one obtains:

Hpumpmode = 314.8m · 0.698.                            (14)

Solving equation (14) yields a required pumping head of 220.0 m. This result implies
that the pumping head needs to be 70% of the turbine mode head to balance the
energy expended in pumping with the energy generated by the turbine.
