Uncertainty about future prices and inflow, introduced in Section , is central to hydropower scheduling. Belsnes et al. (2016) show that a stochastic strategy earns more than a deterministic one in every case they test. The main reason is that it sees a risk of spill that the deterministic plan does not. The reviewed studies differ in which inputs they treat as uncertain, when the information is assumed known, how the uncertainty is represented over time, and how the behaviour of prices is described.

Inflow and energy prices are both unknown when the water is allocated, and most medium-term studies treat them as stochastic (Löhndorf et al., 2013; Helseth et al., 2016; Kleiven et al., 2023; Alic et al., 2023; Bore and Brensholm, 2026). Some also treat reserve-capacity prices as stochastic (Helseth et al., 2016; Helseth et al., 2017). For pumped storage the choice matters more than for a conventional plant. Löhndorf et al. (2013) find that the gain of a stochastic policy over a rolling deterministic plan grows with the power capacity relative to the reservoir. Ideally every price the plant trades on would be stochastic. However, each added variable multiplies the number of states and the data needed to estimate the transitions between them. Helseth et al. (2016), for example, have to adjust their transition probabilities when few scenarios are available.

When several inputs are uncertain, their dependence is a further choice. Kleiven et al. (2023) start from the negative correlation between Norwegian prices and inflow. Ignoring it changes water values and reservoir operation in their case, although the loss in expected revenue was small. In contrast, Helseth et al. (2016) assume prices independent of inflow. The two differ in what the solution method allows. A joint price--inflow chain fits the lattice of Kleiven et al. (2023), whereas the combined SDP and SDDP scheme of Helseth et al. (2016) requires the price process to be independent of the inflow process. Whether dependence is represented therefore depends as much on the method as on the data. Both studies concern conventional plants without pumping.

The timing of information also varies across studies. Helseth et al. (2013) and Bore and Brensholm (2026) assume that the current week's prices and inflow are known once the week starts and keep the uncertainty between weeks. Abgottspon and Andersson (2014), in contrast, let information arrive within the week for a Swiss pumped-storage plant. The former note that a week is too coarse to capture the need for balancing wind power. The latter find that treating the week as known overvalues the water, and that the within-week model gives a higher expected profit in their case with weekly reserves. Although the difference is partly explained by which markets are included, the timing of information should be chosen in line with the products the plant sells. Reserves are committed before the energy prices are known.

Uncertainty is typically represented through a small number of states per week, and the reviewed studies share the same steps. First, historical or simulated series of prices and inflow are collected. Second, the series are clustered into a few states per week (Alic et al., 2023; Bore and Brensholm, 2026). Third, the transitions between states are counted, and the probabilities are corrected so that the conditional expected prices are preserved (Helseth et al., 2016; Mo et al., 2001). Across the studies the number of states varies from three to nine. More states describe the variation better, but each rests on fewer observations. Consequently, the data length and the number of states together decide how much of the year's price persistence the chain can carry, since a Markov chain forgets its initial state at an exponential rate (Mo et al., 2001).

Nordic day-ahead prices show long memory, strongest in the Norwegian areas, which Haldrup and Nielsen (2006) relate to the slow variation of reservoirs. Consequently, both how prices vary within a week and how long high- or low-price conditions persist affect what a storage plant can earn. Both change with the system. Henden et al. (2016) find that more transmission to the continent raises the variability of southern Norwegian prices, whereas more pumped storage lowers it. Studies that relate price behaviour to storage value use known prices. Löhndorf and Wozabal (2023) find that coordinating day-ahead and intraday trading benefits a pumped-storage plant far more than a reservoir plant, and Ak et al. (2019) earn most from pumping in the year with the largest daily price variation. The within-week pattern also depends on what a state contains. Alic et al. (2023) give each state a weekly average price with a fixed daily profile, whereas Bore and Brensholm (2026) keep the hourly series of each cluster. These studies collectively show that price behaviour matters for storage value. Yet none of them varies volatility and persistence separately or relates them to the value of pump, turbine and reservoir capacity.
