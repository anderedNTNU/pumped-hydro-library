Abstract

The growing share of Variable Renewable Energy Source (VRES) increases the need for
Demand Side Management (DSM) to balance the grid by, for example, load shifting.
However, this can introduce additional grid challenges in other hours in the form of
rebound effects. This thesis explores a controlled load shifting strategy, achieved by
shifting consumption to predefined time slots. This provides Distribution System
Operators (DSOs) with alternatives to mitigate consequences of rebound effects, and is
referred to as temporal redistribution.

A situation where the DSO requests load reduction in a Energy Community (EC) for an
hour is investigated for various types of temporal redistribution. The simulated EC
consists of 25 households, a Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) and smart Electric
Vehicle (EV) charging, and is modeled using linear programming. A Rolling-Receding
Horizon (RRH) framework captures the daily revelation of spot prices, while a
Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP) algorithm accounts for the long-term
consequences of operation. The costs analyzed are the volumetric electricity prices and
the demand charge, allowing for an analysis of performance under different strategies.

When the demand charge is not considered in the optimization formulation the load is
shifted to the cheapest hour available. When the demand charge is included the load is
distributed in an attempt to avoid an increase in the peak grid import. Including the
demand charge in the optimization is superior to excluding it. Benefits include an
increase in offered amount of flexibility, stable power import and battery- friendly
operation. Shorter time slots improves control over duration and occurrence of rebound
effects, though this comes at the cost of higher total expenses. Across all tested
temporal redistribution strategies operational costs increase by maximum 0.79%. From
the most liberal to the most restrictive case, the marginal cost ranges from 0.012 - 8.20
NOK/kWh/h. The low increase in total and marginal cost suggests that the true value
lies in grid benefits. Consequently, the remuneration to the EC should reflect not only
the operational costs but also the value provided to the grid.

Sammendrag

Den økende andelen av varierende fornybare energikilder (VRES) øker behovet for
laststyring på forbrukersiden (DSM) for å balansere nettet, ved hjelp av for eksempel
lastforflytning. Dette kan introdusere nye utfordringer i nettet på andre tidspunkt
gjennom “rebound”-effekter. Denne masteroppgaven utforsker en kontrollert
lastforflytingsstrategi gjennom å flytte forbruk til definerte tidsperioder. Dette gir
nettselskaper (DSOs) alternativer for å begrense konsekvensene av rebound-effekter, og
kan kalles temporal forflytning.

Et tilfelle hvor DSO-en ber om lastreduksjon hos et energisamfunn (EC) i en time er
undersøkt for flere typer temporal forflytning. Det simulerte EC-et består av 25
husholdninger, et batterisystem (BESS), og smart elbil (EV)-lading, og det er modellert
ved hjelp av lineærprogrammering. Et “Rolling-Receding Horizon” (RRH) rammeverk er
laget for å simulere hvordan spottprisen blir avslørt daglig, opererer sammen med en
“Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming” (SDDP) algoritme som fanger de langsiktige
driftskonsekvensene. Kostnadene som er analysert er de volumetriske elektrisitetsprisene
og effekttariffen. Dette tillater en analyse av ytelse under ulike strategier.

Når effekttariffen ikke blir hensyntatt i formuleringen av optimeringsproblemet blir
lasten flyttet til den billigste timen tilgjengelig. Når den blir tatt hensyn til blir lasten
fordelt utover i et forsøk på å unngå en økning i topplast. Å ta hensyn til effekttariffen
er gunstigere enn å ikke ta hensyn til den. Fordeler inkluderer økt mengde tilbydd
fleksibilitet, en jevnere lastimport og en mer batterivennlig drift. Kortere tidsperioder
gir økt kontroll på varighet og forekomst av rebound-effekter, men kommer på
bekostning av høyere totalkostnader. Den maksimale kostnadsøkningen for alle
temporale forflytningsstrategier er på 0.79%. Fra de mest liberale til mest begrensede
tilfellene varierer den marginale kostnadsøkningen fra 0.012-8.20 NOK/kWh/h. Den
lave økningen i totalkostnad og de lave marginalkostnadene indikerer at den faktiske
verdien ligger i fordelene nettet opplever. Dermed burde godtgjørelsen til EC-et ikke
bare reflektere den økte kostnaden, men også verdien den gir til nettet.

Introduction

1.1 Background and Motivation

The increased power production from Variable Renewable Energy Sources (VRESs)
creates a need for demand-side balancing [1]. To support grid balancing, end users must
be flexible and adjust their power consumption to match changes in production and
local grid conditions. One way to achieve flexibility is by adjusting consumption based
on varying electricity prices, called Price-Based Demand Response (DR), as the
electricity price reflects the current grid status. Additionally, emerging markets enable
Incentive-Based DR participation by altering, often reducing, the power consumption
upon request from a Distribution System Operator (DSO) or another enabler.
Studies have examined how smart Electric Vehicle (EV) charging [2], utilizing a Battery
Energy Storage System (BESS) [3], participation in DR programs [4], markets [5] and
other flexibility measures can support grid stability. A problem with temporarily
reducing the power consumption is that the reactivation of curtailed loads can cause a
sudden surge in power consumption, known as a rebound effect. This can lead to new
issues and shift the problem to another hour of the day [6]. To the authors’ knowledge,
there is limited research on controlled reactivation of curtailed loads as a way to
mitigate these rebound effects. Today, DSOs do not possess viable frameworks to either
control or affect the occurrence of rebound effects, and the mitigation of these. This is
the foundation of this thesis.

                                                        1
 1.2 Objective and Contributions

 The objective of this thesis is to develop an optimization model in Python to assess how
 an Energy Community (EC) can operate cost-efficiently, and also support the local
 DSO in relieving grid strain. This is done by scheduling a shared BESS and smart EV
 charging in response to Price- and Incentive-Based DR signals. Price-Based DR reduces
 operational costs in the EC by shifting electricity consumption to hours with lower
 electricity prices. It can also lower the demand charge of the grid tariff by minimizing
 the monthly peak consumption. Further, participation in an Incentive-Based DR
 program allows the DSO to request the EC to limit power consumption to support the
 grid when strained or unbalanced. To avoid rebound issues after a period of load
 reduction, the EC proposes solutions of controlled load shifting, referred to as temporal
 redistribution. This allows the DSO to choose predefined time slots to shift the load to,
 given the predicted rebound effects and the associated costs of this flexibility service.

 The main objective of this thesis is to answer research questions related to an EC’s
 ability to avoid creating rebound effects when participating in an Incentive-Based DR
 program, while minimizing operational costs. The main research question aims to
 pinpoint the current research gap.

 Main research question: Is temporal redistribution of load a viable method for
 mitigating the grid’s rebound effect when reactivating curtailed loads in a simulated
 Incentive-Based DR event?

 To explore the main research question, RQ1 and RQ2 supplement it:

RQ1: Which implications do temporal redistribution introduce in terms of level of
         flexibility and costs?

RQ2: How can flexible resources be scheduled to benefit the EC and the DSO?

 Further, this thesis has the following contributions:

      • Application of a Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP) framework
         together with a Rolling-Receding Horizon (RRH) approach to model future
         consequences of operation and the daily spot price revelation.

      • Simulation of an EC operating with a shared BESS and smart EV charging,
         considering spot price, volumetric grid tariff, and demand charge.

      • Simulating Incentive-Based DR participation by reducing energy usage for one
         hour upon request from the DSO.

                                                          2
    • Modeling of load shifting to predefined time slots to assess rebound effects and
       how optimizing for the demand charge may mitigate these.

    • An operational cost analysis for redistributing various quantities of energy.

1.2.1 Limitations

This thesis utilizes historical data at an aggregated level. The operational costs are
calculated at a common metering point of the EC, and do not reflect the individual
consumption. Only operational costs are considered, an investment cost analysis and
remuneration schemes are excluded to focus on short-term flexibility and system
operation rather than long-term economic feasibility. The results of the temporal
redistribution are limited to model feasibility. A recommendation to the DSO regarding
the optimal temporal redistribution is not provided, as this would require a detailed
description of the grid, which is outside the scope of this thesis.

this master’s thesis. The aim is to provide the reader with sufficient knowledge to follow

The chapter begins with an overview of the Norwegian power system in Sec. 2.1. This is

2.1 The Norwegian Power System

The power system is the infrastructure of power generation, transmission, and
distribution, which operates together to ensure a reliable supply of electricity to end
users [9]. Fig. 2.1.1 presents a general overview. Maintaining a balance between
electricity production and consumption is critical to avoid system collapse [10]. The
Norwegian power system is relatively robust, with a supply reliability exceeding 99.96%
in the year 2024 [11].

                                                        5
            Figure 2.1.1: Simplified model of the Norwegian power system [8].

2.1.1 Power Production

Norway’s power production is heavily influenced by hydropower, which in 2025 accounts
for 89% of the total installed capacity [12]. Another emerging source of capacity is wind
power, accounting for around 11%. As a consequence, power production is largely
weather-dependent. For instance, in periods of low water levels in hydro reservoirs or
low wind conditions, it is economical to import power [13].

Today, Norway’s electricity demand ranges between 130 and 140 TWh per year [14]. As
of 2024, Norway is self-sufficient in power production, with a total annual generation of
158 TWh [15]. However, in 2050, the electric power demand is projected to increase
significantly to 200-260 TWh [16], [17]. In Trøndelag, the consumption demand is
expected to increase by 166%. However, the planned new production will only increase
capacity by 14% [18]. Norway is facing an energy deficit, with national energy
consumption projected to surpass generation within the next five years [19].
Additionally, the growing share of VRESs, such as wind power, may cause congestion
and voltage issues in the grid if preventive measures are not implemented [20].

2.1.2 Transmission

The transmission grid functions as the motor highway of the power system. It consists
of high-voltage lines operating between 132 kV to 400 kV [21]. These lines transfer
power from power plants to various regions within the country and abroad through
interconnections. The Transmission System Operator (TSO) in Norway is Statnett.
Statnett is responsible for building, maintaining, operating, and stabilizing the
transmission system during power imbalances to prevent system collapse [22]. The
transmission system is a natural monopoly because building multiple parallel lines is
economically irrational. Consequently, the TSO operates under strict regulations by the
Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Directorate (NVE), ensuring the benefit of
society and incentivizing innovation, optimal operation, and maintenance of the system.

                                                        6
Some of the issues today on the transmission level include congestion and balancing
challenges, which are costly [23]. The increased share of VRES raises the need for
flexibility and balancing of the grid [16]. Currently, the load on the transmission grid is
unevenly distributed, causing line capacity ratings in certain regions to be periodically
challenged. Reinforcement efforts cannot keep up with the demand growth, meaning
that these grid issues may persist. Future production and demand planning must
account for the geographical distribution of generation and consumption, alongside
flexible operation, to solve these challenges.

2.1.3 Distribution

The distribution grid transports power from the transmission grid’s point of attachment
to the end users [24]. It operates at a lower voltage level than the transmission system,
ranging from 132 kV down to 230 V. The distribution grid is managed locally by a
designated Distribution System Operator (DSO). The responsibilities of the DSOs
include maintaining and improving voltage stability [3], which may be achieved by
upgrading lines, transformers, or both [25]. Like the transmission system, the
distribution network is a regulated monopoly. The Norwegian Energy Regulatory
Authority (RME) ensures that the DSOs complies with the legal requirements and
limits profit to ensure sensible operation of the grid [26].

The distribution grid faces many of the same challenges as the transmission grid [16].
With the increase in varying power-intensive loads, such as EV charging, the higher
load and intense power peaks create additional challenges [9]. Consequently, DSOs often
operate the network with larger security margins to accommodate such peaks. However,
this leads to suboptimal grid utilization, as much of the line capacity remains unused
for large amounts of the time.

2.1.4 End-User Flexibility as a Solution to Grid Issues

By implementing flexible solutions, the need for grid upgrades may be postponed [27].
An example of this is by renting a BESS from a third party [28] (DSOs are not allowed
to own BESSs themselves under EU legislation [29]). Further, facilitating end user
flexibility to improve grid operation is another possibility for DSOs and TSOs. This is
further explored in Sec. 2.5 and Sec. 2.6.

                                                        7
2.2 The Energy Markets

The energy markets facilitate the purchase and sale of energy and power by connecting
producers, retailers, traders, TSOs, and large-scale end users on common platforms [30].
Participants interact via market exchanges and by contracts, forming a complex yet
organized system. Energy markets aim to balance power production before dispatch
[31], however, due to uncertainties and natural fluctuations, they also include
mechanisms to make real-time adjustments. Several energy markets exist depending on
the time horizon and utility traded. Understanding how they operate is essential for
comprehending the current role of flexibility, how it is marketed, and functions
financially and physically.

2.2.1 The Forwards Market

The forwards market involves long-term contracts between power producers and
consumers, where a fixed price for electricity is set over a set period. This ensures stable
revenues for producers, predictable costs for consumers, and it contributes to the
security of supply [32]. As it falls outside this thesis’ scope, it is not discussed further.

2.2.2 The Capacity Markets

Capacity markets allow participants to offer power capacity to address future frequency
imbalances [22]. Bids are filtered to prevent congestion if activated [33]. Participants are
paid for reserving capacity, regardless of whether the offered capacity is activated [33].

Figure 2.2.1: Timeline in the event of a grid frequency drop and relative power response
from each reserve, illustration from [31].

                                                        8
There are four reserve types, FFR, FCR, aFRR, and mFRR, each distinguished by time,
duration, and reason for activation [22]. These are all procured in separate markets, and
the same capacity may not be offered in more than one at a time. Fig. 2.2.1 illustrates
the timeline of reserve activation, showing when and for how long each is activated to
restore the grid frequency. The rise of VRES in the generation mix contributes to an
increased need for flexibility and balancing services to maintain grid stability [33].
In the event of a real-time system imbalance, the TSO may need to activate the
reserved capacity. The participants are rewarded for activating the reserved capacity
[33], and settlements occur afterward.

2.2.3 The Day-Ahead Market - Elspot

The day-ahead market determines the generation schedules and power prices for the
following day. In Norway, this market is called Elspot and is operated by Nord Pool
[34]. Norway is divided into five price zones, as shown in Fig. 2.2.2, which may have
different prices due to line transfer capacities between areas [35]. Producers in each area
estimate production costs [36], including factors like weather, CO2-prices, and salaries,
while retailers forecast the consumers’ demand and willingness to pay. Both submit
anonymous bids with price, quantity, and time for the following day. Daily at noon,
Nord Pool matches supply and demand to determine the zonal prices using a
“pay-as-cleared” method.

                  Figure 2.2.2: Price zones of Norway, illustration from [35].

                                                        9
2.2.4 The Intraday Market - Elbas

The intraday market allows participants to trade energy continuously after the
day-ahead market is closed [37]. For Norway, this market is called Elbas, and it is
operated by Nord Pool. The market is open until one hour before dispatch, allowing for
adjustments based on updated forecasts or unplanned events, thereby enhancing
economic efficiency and grid stability [38]. In contrast to the day-ahead market, it is a
continuous market where the lowest price offers are prioritized [37].

In addition to continuous trading, the intraday market includes three intraday auctions
to allocate cross-border capacity in Europe [39]. Thus, prices are more accurate, and
liquidity and flexibility in the market are increased. The continuous trade of power, in
combination with the three daily auctions, helps balance the power grid, and potential
future grid issues are mitigated.

2.2.5 Grid Re-dispatch

After the closure of the intraday market, the TSO conducts load flow calculations to
identify upcoming bottlenecks [40]. To mitigate these, the capacity market’s bids are
used to instruct market participants to adjust their production or consumption. The
affected participants are compensated for their adjustments. While not a market in
itself, grid re-dispatch plays a crucial role in power flow scheduling by ensuring security
of supply and minimizing the need for capacity activation. Additionally, consumer
flexibility may contribute to mitigating congestion [41].

2.3 End User Pricing

The end users experience their electricity costs through two contracts, one with their
energy retailer of choice and one with the local DSO. The costs involved in these
contracts are the electricity price and the grid tariff, respectively.

2.3.1 Electricity Price

The electricity price is the amount the end users pay for utilizing electric energy,
measured in [NOK/kWh]. Energy retailers purchase power from the energy markets and
resell it to the end users. Prices depend on the choice of energy retailer and the type of
contract. The spot price contract is used by 80% of Norwegians [42], which has a time
variant price reflecting the market price obtained in the day-ahead market.

                                                       10
2.3.2 Grid Tariff

The grid tariff is a charge payed to the DSO to cover the cost of transporting energy
from a power producer to the end user. The tariff consists of three key elements: energy
costs, power costs, and government fees [43].

First, the end users have a volumetric cost based on the amount of energy supplied to
the end user, measured in [NOK/kWh]. The cost is typically higher during the day
(06:00 - 22:00) than at night (22:00 - 06:00). Second, the end user is charged for their
peak power demand [kWh/h] within the month, called the demand charge. For private
consumers, the demand charge was implemented in 2022 to incentivize a more effective
utilization of the grid [44]. Today, the charge is calculated by averaging the three
highest hourly peaks within a month. The resulting value determines the cost according
to predefined price brackets of power demand set by the local DSO. Higher
simultaneous power consumption results in increased system losses [23] and thereby
increased costs for the DSO. The third and last element of the grid tariff is the
government fees [45]. The fees include Value Added Tax (VAT), an electricity tax to the
government, and the Enova fee to the Climate and Energy Fund.

2.4 Flexibility

Flexibility relates to actively coping, overcoming, and adapting to changes from
standard operation while maintaining desired performance [46], however, an
agreed-upon and unified definition of flexibility does not yet exist [47]. The scope of
flexibility in this thesis is how end users, represented by an EC, adapt to deviations
from optimal operation while ensuring power reliability and cost efficiency. These
deviations must not come at a loss of comfort for the end users [48]. Today, end users
generally have limited knowledge and resources on flexible energy usage. Meanwhile,
the increasing share of VRES makes balancing the power system more challenging.
Thus, consumers must become more flexible and adjust their energy consumption in
line with production to maintain system balance [1].

Flexibility is provided to the power system by a flexibility resource [46]. A flexibility
resource can be defined as a component that can modify its scheduled operation on
request by either having a buffer (stored energy) or shifting consumption in time [49].
These resources are either from the supply side or the demand side. Supply-side
management is outside of the scope of this thesis and is not discussed further.

                                                       11
2.4.1 Demand Side Management

The aim of Demand Side Management (DSM) is to implement measures for end users
to modify their consumption. These measures change the load shape, intending to
benefit the power system [50]. There are six DSM-measures [51], these are presented in
Fig. 2.4.1. The arrows indicate how the load profile changes according to the type of
DSM measure. This thesis focuses mostly on the measures peak clipping, valley filling,
and load shifting.

                    Figure 2.4.1: Demand Side Management measures [51].

To achieve the DSM measures, the choice of flexibility resource depends on the technical
characteristics. Some examples of implementations include, but are not limited to,
BESSs, smart EV chargers or other smart home appliances through Price-Based or
Incentive-Based DR [50]. Price-Based and Incentive-Based DR will be presented in the
following section.

2.4.2 Demand Response

Building on DSM, DR refers to activities and programs that influence or motivate when
and how energy is consumed through dynamic pricing, advanced metering, and other
enabling technologies [51]. End users who are flexible with their power usage, energy
usage, or both, fit into one of two categories. The first is Price-Based DR, and the
second is Incentive-Based DR. The goal of both is to contribute to a more cost-effective
operation of the power grid, however, they differ in strategy.

                                                       12
2.4.2.1 Price-Based Demand Response

With Price-Based DR, the end users are motivated to voluntarily adapt either their
power or energy usage, or both, in line with price signals to reduce costs [1]. One of
these signals may be the spot price. Power prices are generally lower during periods of
sufficient power production and bidding zone transfer capacity. This encourages
consumers to increase their demand during these hours. In contrast, power prices are
higher during periods of limited production or transfer capacity between bidding zones.
High prices encourage consumers to reduce their demand during these hours. Another
price signal is the demand charge. This price signal focuses on evening out energy
consumption and lowering peak power demand, as this lowers the cost.

A key aspect of Price-Based DR is that it is voluntary, and only applies to costs end
users are already subject to. The intention of Price-Based DR is to lower these existing
expenses by increasing the awareness of either their power usage or energy usage, or
both. It is also possible to completely ignore the price signals, however, this will not be
cost-optimal for the end users, nor benefit the grid operation.

If Price-Based DR is performed by a substantial number of the end users, it may help
with load shifting in the grid, thereby contributing to a more stable grid operation.
This kind of operation strategy may apply to smart EV charging, space heating, electric
water heaters, and other power-intensive home appliances. However, to achieve these
benefits, the end users must become more price-conscious.

2.4.2.2 Incentive-Based Demand Response

For Incentive-Based DR, the aim is to motivate end users to alter their power
consumption upon requests from their DSO, TSO, or any other flexibility service
provider [1]. The goal with this is to aid grid operators in voltage, frequency, or
congestion problems, or a combination of these. For example, consumers may restrict
power import for a short period at the DSO’s request. When restricting power import,
consumers may be remunerated based on how far in advance the signal is sent and the
amount of load reduction [52]. In contrast to Price-Based DR, end users must actively
participate in Incentive-Based DR activities. The consumption of the end users is
altered to improve the current or future state of the grid, which is possible without any
loss of comfort for the end users [48].

Since the power consumption of a single user is relatively small compared to the overall
power system, this flexible behavior can be aggregated across multiple households, or
ECs, to have a substantial effect on the grid. This will be further explored in Sec. 2.6.

                                                       13
2.4.3 Flexibility Potential of End Users

In Incentive-Based DR, end users adjust their power consumption, energy consumption,
or both, in exchange for economic compensation [49]. The amount of load an end user
can adjust is known as their Flexibility Potential (FP). The amount of FP is a
fundamental element when determining the level of activation and remuneration for the
end users [53]. The end user’s baseline consumption represents their expected load
curve in the absence of any external influences [53], as illustrated in Fig. 2.4.2. It is
typically estimated using historical data or various predictive modeling techniques [54].
In contrast, the metered load is the actual load curve observed in real-time when the
end user actively adjusts their consumption in response to an Incentive-Based DR
signal. The FP is calculated by measuring the difference between the actual metered
load and the estimated hypothetical baseline load over the service duration [54]. A
challenge in determining the remuneration of FP is that consumers may exploit the
baseline calculation into make it appear higher than it actually is, thereby increasing
their remuneration [53]. On the other hand, the calculation method may also allow the
responsible party to underpay participants. This highlights the importance of using a
robust method when calculating baseline consumption.

Figure 2.4.2: Flexibility potential of reduced load during a demand response event [53].

End users may either increase or decrease their power usage relative to their intended
levels. When consumption is increased, the FP is quantified by subtracting the baseline
load from the main fuse’s capacity. This increase in power consumption may be
achieved by activating energy-intensive loads. In the event of large solar power
production in the grid, it may be beneficial to consume more power to avoid
over-voltage issues. When decreasing the consumption, the FP is quantified as the
difference between the baseline and the minimum metered load, as shown in Fig. 2.4.2.
The minimum load typically includes essential services, such as space heating and

                                                       14
lighting. The load intended to be utilized within the service duration is shifted to
another time period. Postponing EV charging, water heater operation, or using the
dishwasher or washing machine later may lead to a rebound effect with an increase in
power consumption at a later time. This is further explained in the next section.

2.4.4 Rebound Effect

A rebound effect occurs when electrical loads that were temporarily turned off or used
less power, such as during an Incentive-Based DR event, return to their normal
operation all at once [6]. This sudden surge in power consumption can lead to
unintended frequency problems in the grid [6]. Additionally, if it is known that power
consumption will be reduced in the future, consumption may increase in advance to
prepare for this. This behavior can be considered a rebound effect resulting from
proactive countermeasures. The rebound effect has been documented in several studies
[55].
When using DSM measures to load shift the FP, it is important to consider the
potential for a rebound effect. Particularly within load shifting of the FP when
participating in Incentive-Based DR programs. The original intention of mitigating grid
problems risk only being shifted in time and creating a rebound effect, thereby
counteracting the initial benefit. Thus, the timing of load activation is essential in
mitigating rebound effects, especially when large loads are involved, such as EV
charging stations. Incorporating a BESS may mitigate rebound effects [56].

Figure 2.4.3: Comprehensive illustration of important characteristics of flexible re-
sources and the rebound effect [57].

illustrates the power capacity, energy capacity, reaction time, and service duration of

2.5 Energy Communities

To the authors’ knowledge, there is no uniform definition of the term “Energy
Community” (EC). Recurring elements in the literature describe local communities that
together contribute to the green transition, providing environmental, economic, or social
benefits through conscious electricity consumption [58]. The European Commission has
derived its definition through the EU’s Clean Energy for all Europeans package [59]. It
emphasizes that an EC should be an open and voluntary entity, controlled by its
members or shareholders, with the goal of providing local community benefits rather
than solely maximizing financial profits through different energy services. Two
directives within this package specifically mention ECs: Directive 2018/2001 (RED II)
[60], which outlines the characteristics of energy communities, and Directive 2019/944
(IMED) [29], which describes the markets in which they should operate.

ECs may include battery storage, solar generation, smart EV charging, the use of heat
pumps, among others [61]. In the Nordics, energy storage, EV charging stations and the
use of heat pumps are typical characteristics of an EC [62]. A key element of ECs is
that the energy assets and management systems are actively controlled by the members
or shareholders of the EC, enhancing social acceptance and participation in the energy
transition [24]. Power produced locally may be utilized for internal consumption,
stored, or sold to the grid [24]. The aim is to reduce the need for importing power from
the grid, thereby lowering electricity bills [62], and aiding grid balancing [24]. The
primary motivation for establishing an EC is the integration of renewable energy
sources [62]. Economic motivation and self-sufficiency are other important factors.

ECs may support their local DSO by acting flexibly in their energy consumption and
production for the benefit of grid stability [24]. The advantages of adapting ECs include
improved energy efficiency, more green job opportunities, and increased social
acceptance of renewable energy projects [58]. At the distribution level, enabling ECs
may postpone the need for grid investments [27]. However, ECs are affected by
regulatory uncertainties [61]. For example, the implementation and frequent changes to
the Norwegian government’s “energy support scheme” [63] may prevent the ECs in
developing reliable financing plans. Other barriers include social acceptance and
economic rational implementation, both of which depend on appropriate regulations
and incentives [24]. On an economic level, ECs generally lack a viable business model,
as the services provided are not yet available in existing markets or other standardized
arrangements [27].

                                                       16
2.6 Emerging Products and Markets

Emerging programs and markets are enabling owners of flexible resources to actively
participate in electricity markets, and support their DSO with various grid services [20].
An example of this is providing capacity for frequency stabilization in case of
unbalances. This can be done by participating in Incentive-Based DR programs.
Participants can be private end users participating through aggregators, companies, or
other entities utilizing flexible resources for profit.
The Clean Energy for all Europeans package defines a framework for designing a flexible
market in which consumers may participate [64], incentivizing them to be flexible in
their energy consumption. These markets [5], [65]–[67] aim to enhance energy efficiency
and grid utilization. A market is essential for coordinating participant bids [59].
Additionally, geographical location must be considered to avoid creating grid issues [16].
Many of these markets are either pilot projects or still in the early stages. Engaging the
local population is challenging flexibility enablers, as only a handful of people are
interested in the topic or even aware of the existence of these emerging markets [20].
There is a lack of a proper framework for participation and a robust value chain.
Remuneration to the flexibility participants should at least cover the cost of altering
their consumption from the baseline consumption [24]. Pricing varies depending on the
flexibility resources, adding complexity for aggregators in determining a fair amount of
remuneration for the participants [20]. An overview of some of the existing flexibility
markets is provided in Sec. 3.4.

3.1 EV Charging

In recent years, the number of EVs in Norway has grown significantly [68], putting
additional strain on the grid. At the same time, smart EV charging can support the
grid and act as a flexible resource in DR programs. These perspectives provide a basis
for studying the operational and economic impacts of controlled EV charging.

                                                       18
3.1.1 Impact of EV Charging on the Grid

A study examines the power grid and the negative impacts of EV charging [2]. It also
discusses measures to support EV integration. Solutions are proposed to reduce grid
strain and losses, stating that “... EV deployment can be profitable for the operation of
the power grid and EV owners” [2]. EV charging is proposed to contribute to load
shifting in the grid, resulting in reduced power losses and operational costs. However,
battery degradation, complex hardware requirements, and social barriers challenge their
realization.

The impact of EV charging on a Taiwanese distribution grid seen from the grid
perspective is examined [69]. The study considers effects such as average losses and
voltage drops. By using real-world measurement and survey data, low voltage is found
when the EV penetration becomes high. However, the number of buses and EV
chargers in the grid affects under-voltage and over-current events. Smart EV charging is
suggested as a measure to mitigate the security problems.

Geographically concentrated loads in the Great Britain power system are considered in
[9]. Distribution networks with a high number of EV charging points may experience
voltage and frequency issues. The article indicates that smart EV charging is vital to
protect both the grid stability on transmission and distribution levels. However,
optimizing EV charging for the distribution grid may have a destructive effect on the
transmission grid and vice versa.

3.1.2 EVs and Flexibility

A case study from Trondheim examines the FP of EV charging in apartment buildings
[70]. The aim is to describe charging habits, analyze load profiles and quantify the FP.
The study finds EV chargers, when connected to private charging points, are plugged in
for twice as long as for shared charging points, leading to a larger FP. The study states
that EV charging is the main source of flexible electricity use in apartment buildings.

How EVs may be a source of flexibility for the DSOs is investigated in [71]. The aim is
to describe the flexibility services that EVs may provide at the distribution level. The
study states that it is possible for flexible loads to control the voltage through
modulation of active and reactive power. It further suggests that flexible loads “... could
be modulated as an effective way to mitigate congestion and overloading”. Drawbacks
include the need for algorithm implementation, coordination, new markets, and it also
requires the DSO to take on a new role as flexibility enablers. Future work highlights
the importance of calculating the value of flexibility.

                                                       19
An empirical Norwegian study evaluates how end users can reduce charging costs
through a digital smart EV charging platform [72]. When aiming to shift the charging
time from peak to off-peak hours, users significantly reduce costs compared to
conventional charging. From the grid’s perspective, this load shifting contributes to
peak clipping, reducing both power losses and congestion. The study highlights the
benefits of coordinated users’ charging behavior for lowering costs.

3.2 Battery Energy Storage Systems

A BESS offers valuable flexibility to the grid through fast response times and the ability
to store and discharge energy as needed. When properly operated, it can enhance grid
stability and reduce electricity costs for the end users. However, the economic
effectiveness depends on different market and tariff structures.

3.2.1 BESS and Flexibility

The operation of a hypothetical local Finnish EC contributing capacity to the m-FRR
market is investigated in [73]. The community’s flexible resources, including a BESS
and multiple EVs, are coordinated. The study aims to optimize the scheduling and
operation of these resources through a two-stage model seeking to maximize community
profit. The results show that providing m-FRR can be profitable. Future work suggests
expanding the range of services offered and incorporating other flexibility resources.

A study on the Norwegian FFR-market participation of a campus with a BESS and EVs
capable of vehicle-to-grid functionality is investigated in [74]. The study aims to
evaluate the economic and operational impact of different contracts, while also
considering the suitability of the resources due to their rapid activation times. Results
show a reduction in average total costs by 5.1-6.8% under the different contracts.
However, the absence of an investment cost analysis makes the economic savings
uncertain. Additionally, participation in the FFR-market has negative impacts on
limiting peak loads.

A study examines how a Tesla Powerwall BESS can be scheduled to mitigate rebound
effects caused by electric water heaters in a 500 house residential neighborhood [56].
The study is unclear about whether the method used is deterministic or not.
Additionally, as the analysis is for only one summer day, the credibility of the results is
questioned. Nevertheless, the results show great potential that a BESS can mitigate
rebound effects when the discharging occurs after a DR event.

                                                       20
3.2.2 BESS Operation and Sizing

A Norwegian study examines the cost-effectiveness of an EC, and how a BESS may
provide voltage support from the EC to the distribution grid [3]. The primary aim of
the study is to coordinate the BESS to improve voltage at the distribution level. The
study finds an optimal BESS modeling that results in fewer voltage spikes and no cases
of overvoltage. Operating in a battery-degradation-friendly way increases the lifetime of
the BESS, postponing battery replacements and the costs that follow because of this.
Emphasis is put on optimizing for the demand charge, as this could lower peak
consumption further and result in fewer grid issues.

A Finnish study aims to profitably optimize the sizing of the BESS and PV system for
an apartment building [48]. The study highlights that the correct sizing of the battery
is heavily dependent on the type of DR, the design of the electricity tariff, and
investment costs. The study emphasizes that sizing of the BESS must be tailored to
each specific DSM measure.

3.3 Demand Response

DR are signals that encourage end users to alter their consumption, either through
price signals, Price-Based DR, or a grid operator or another enablers, request signal,
Incentive-Based DR. This makes the end user an active part in grid support and allows
them to reduce their electricity bill. In DR programs, the focus is on the end user
perspective.

3.3.1 Price-Based Demand Response

Price-Based DR encourages consumers to shift usage based on varying electricity prices
and grid tariffs, enabling cost savings for end users and reducing grid stress for the
DSO. This section focuses on time-of-use pricing and demand charges, examining how
these signals influence operation and flexibility for end users.

A paper investigates cost-minimization for a single South African household by the use
of Price-Based DR measures [75]. The electricity price contains fixed costs and varying
costs depending on time-of-use, similar to the current end user pricing in Norway. By
implementing a home battery, monitoring the electricity price, and altering the usage
with respect to it, the household can shift its consumption. This resulted in cost savings
of 23-29%, along with a peak reduction.

                                                       21
BESS, EV charging, and space heating in a Norwegian residential building are
investigated for their influence on long-term operational costs in [76]. The flexible
resources, electricity pricing, and future cost curves created are comparable to this
thesis’ model and that of an EC. When exposing Price-Based DR for both the
volumetric energy costs as well as the demand charge, the total electricity cost
decreases. Flexible EV charging is most influential on total costs, reducing the total
electricity cost by 14.6% compared to passive charging.

An EV parking lot with vehicle-to-grid capabilities is considered in [4], aiming to
determine the optimal level of participation in the energy and reserve markets in
addition to performing Price-Based DR. The latter is achieved by considering aspects
such as the time-of-use, critical peak power periods, and real-time pricing. The specific
time-of-use tariff presented is deemed the most profitable option, though it significantly
limits its ability to participate in Incentive-Based DR programs. A combination of the
DR methods could yield higher profits.

3.3.2 Incentive-Based Demand Response

Incentive-Based DR enables grid operators, or other enablers, to request load reductions
from end users. Sufficient notice time and remuneration are important factors for end
users to deliver the requested flexibility and motivate participation. These implications
are of interest in this thesis.

The aforementioned study by [4] also investigated participating in an Incentive-Based
DR program. Based on a price signal, the EV parking lot partly or fully curtailed their
import for one hour. The study achieves its goal of profitable operation and cost
reduction for its customers, but it does not mention how long in advance the signal
arrives, which could significantly affect total costs.

The optimization of an EV parking lot connected to an office building while also
providing flexibility services through an Incentive-Based DR program is studied in [77].
Grid import is restricted for one hour with varying notice times, each linked to different
levels of demand charge reduction. The study finds that only the 12-hour notice time is
an economically viable outcome while still meeting charging demands. Sufficient notice
time is crucial to shift the necessary load before activation, as flexibility is limited
afterwards.

The same study in Sec. 3.2.2 aims to provide voltage control for the DSO [3]. However,
this voltage control comes with an increased cost for the consumers, the participants
should at least be remunerated with e15 a year to compensate for the 0.12% increase in
yearly electricity costs and battery degradation. This arrangement, where consumers

                                                       22
offer voltage control services in return for remuneration for their expenses, is an
example of a potential Incentive-Based DR program.

Belgian DSOs purchasing flexibility from residential consumers with Photovoltaics (PV)
installations is modeled in [23]. The aim is to postpone or limit the need for grid
expansion investments. The model provides an indicative price per unit of flexibility
and quantifies the benefits for the DSO. The flexibility is modeled as a percentage of
the total consumption. Results show that the savings in expansion cost exceed the
remuneration to the end users, resulting in an overall cost reduction. The price benefit
for end users is approximately e10 per kWh.

3.4 Flexibility Programs and Markets

Sec. 2.6 introduced emerging flexibility products and markets aimed at increasing
energy efficiency and grid support. This section presents a selection of existing
programs and market implementations, providing context for evaluating how different
DR signals impact flexibility and costs.

Grid Rewards is a program launched by Tibber (a Norwegian power retailer) that
targets private end users’ EV charging habits [5]. By activating smart EV charging and
participating in the program, participants may reduce their power bill. A limitation is
that it solely targets private end users with EVs and not larger entities nor other home
appliances. Additionally, whether the demand charge is considered or not depends on
the user’s local DSO.

Euroflex is the next generation after the 4-year pilot project called Norflex [78].
Euroflex is a large-scale Norwegian demo project utilizing the Nodes-platform, working
with end users, energy retailers, and DSOs to provide flexibility and finance the
program. Participation volume is 1 kWh - 5 MWh, thus inviting numerous candidates.
By targeting customers with smart houses, electric water heaters, or EV chargers, the
participants are paid directly or receive a discount on their power bill.

Glitre Nett [66] and Lede [67] are two Norwegian DSOs that offer participation in
Incentive-Based DR programs. Each motivates participants to reduce peak power
consumption using different incentives. For Glitre Nett, participants with yearly
demands exceeding 100 MWh may receive a signal between 12 hours and 0 minutes
before complete grid disconnection. Depending on the notice time, the demand charge
reduces by 40% to 80% [66]. Lede offers a 30% discount on the demand charge for
simply participating in the program. If the flexibility is activated and the customer is
disconnected from the grid, the demand charge is reduced by 95% for the following 12
months [67]. Currently, there is no standardized approach to Incentive-Based DR

                                                       23
among DSOs, as neither of them specifies the disconnection duration. Glitre Nett does
not specify the flexibility service the end users provide, and Lede does not inform on the
notice time.

potential in offering flexibility services and reducing costs. Further, electricity and grid
tariff design affect the effectiveness of Price-Based DR. The participants of
Incentive-Based DR programs see increased cost when providing the DSO with a
service. Various remuneration schemes are presented, proving that the DSOs value the
support received.

As EVs become increasingly popular, it is widely acknowledged that EV charging can
create grid issues within distribution networks. As varying prices indicate the state of
the grid, scheduling flexible resources through Price-Based DR can be done to save
costs for end users and relieve grid strain. Further, BESS and EV charging can shift
load effectively. Their controllable charging power and timing make them valuable for
grid support through Incentive-Based DR programs.

While existing literature highlights the benefits flexible resources provide with load
shifting for reducing grid strain and costs, it often overlooks the rebound effect.
Limitations include controlled load shifting to predefined time slots as a way to mitigate
the rebound effect in an Incentive-Based DR program, and the operational and
economic effects of this.

4.1 Foundations of Optimization

Operation research uses mathematical methods to solve complex problems and identify
optimal solutions for resource allocation and system operation [79]. To implement
Incentive-Based DR signals and temporal redistribution, along with other operational
features of the EC, an optimization model must be created. The first building block for
creating an optimization model is to be familiar with the basic concepts of linear
programming and dual theory.

4.1.1 Linear Programming

In linear programming, both the objective function and the constraints consist of linear
expressions. It is one of the most important mathematical methods used in operations
research [79]. The objective function defines what is to be maximized or minimized. In
the energy sector, typical objectives are minimizing power losses or maximizing profits
[79]. Constraints capture the problem’s physical and operational limits, such as
conservation of energy, BESS operation, and EV charging requirements. They define
the feasible region of possible solutions by limiting resources and controlling the
interactions between variables. The unknowns to be determined are called variables,
often organized into sets when there are multiple variables involved. The coefficients are
known as model parameters.

4.2 Time-adaptive Optimization

In time-adaptive optimization, the optimization model simulates decision-making over
time by allowing for input data to be updated or revealed. The optimization model is
then able to react to the new information, selecting the optimal solution based on the
available input data. Two methods that implement this feature are the Rolling and
Receding Horizon frameworks.

4.2.1 Rolling and Receding Horizon

Rolling Horizon and Receding Horizon are optimization frameworks that allow for the
inclusion of time dynamics in the simulation. This is achieved by iteratively solving the
optimization model in predefined time stages, and allowing for updates in the input
data to be made during execution [80]. A visual representation is shown in Fig. 4.2.1.

Both Rolling and Receding Horizon are dependent on defining three different time
horizons. The first is the scheduling horizon (SH). This is the entire period the
optimization model is run. The next horizon defines how much information the model is
given in each iteration, i.e., the available information. This is referred to as the
prediction horizon (PH), and the length of this depends on the selected method. The
information given in the PH can be updated at each time step. Lastly, the control
horizon (CH) is defined as the time unit of optimization. This is what is solved and
stored before moving on to the next iteration [80].

The Rolling Horizon method is illustrated in Fig. 4.2.1a. In this approach, the PH
remains constant in length throughout the entire simulation and is shifted forward by
the length of the CH in each iteration. At each step, the model only has access to a
portion of the SH. The PH is shorter than for the Receding Horizon method, which
typically results in lower computational demands per iteration. However, as the PH
does not extend to the end of the SH, it cannot account for long-term consequences [80],
and it requires other methods to obtain this information. Additionally, a fixed PH can
be unrealistic in practice, for example, the number of hours of known spot prices are not
fixed.

The Receding Horizon method is illustrated in Fig. 4.2.1b. In this approach, the initial
PH is equal in length to the SH. Thus, Receding Horizon is better at handling future
consequences than Rolling Horizon. After each iteration, the length of the PH is
reduced by the size of the CH. This process continues until the end of the SH is
reached. The model has data for the full SH from the start [81]. This increases the size
of the optimization, making each iteration slower [82] and more computationally
intensive compared to the Rolling Horizon method. Another drawback is that full data
for the entire PH may not always be available in real life, and it may be necessary to
create an uncertainty model in order to create a prediction of it.

4.3 Multi-stage Stochastic Programming

A multi-stage optimization problem is solved sequentially over several stages. Decisions
at one stage influence the outcome and costs in future stages. The variables linking
decisions across stages are known as state variables [83]. Their values must be
transferred between stages, as the outcome of one stage becomes the input for the next.
For example, the State of Charge (SoC) of a BESS at the end of a day is used as the
initial SoC for the next day. In stochastic programming, uncertainty is included in the

                                                       29
optimization model to handle random or unknown parameters. This approach allows for
a more realistic representation of real-world situations and enables the use of future
predictions as input data. By combining these concepts, it allows for modeling systems
where decisions are made over time, and each stage presents new stochastic information.
Several methods handle multi-stage stochastic problems well, one of which is the SDDP
method [83].

4.3.1 Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming

SDDP is a method for solving large multi-stage optimization problems using dual
solutions to approximate the expected-cost-to-go function, which conveys information
between stages. The stages are decomposed and solved iteratively through so-called
forward and backward passes until convergence is achieved and a final solution is
obtained. This method avoids the use of discretization of state variables, and the “curse
of dimensionality” is mitigated. Also, the method manages stochastic problems well. A
drawback of the method is that the problem must be convex [83].

A key challenge of solving multi-stage optimization problems is not only transferring
the value of the state variables, but also capturing the future impact of current
decisions [83]. A solution that appears optimal when considering a single stage may be
suboptimal when evaluated with respect to all stages. For example, a battery might
discharge completely at the end of the day to avoid wasting energy. However, a more
optimal solution might be to store some energy for the following day. SDDP uses the
expected-cost-to-go function as a means to communicate this information between the
stages.

Stochasticity is introduced by each stage having several possible scenarios, each with its
probability of realization. For instance, the spot price of a stage may be divided into
three scenarios: a high, normal and a low price scenario. By incorporating these
uncertainties into the optimization model, the strategy that best deals with the effects
of uncertainty is selected [83].

Fig. 4.3.1 illustrates the SDDP method for an arbitrary number of stages, n. The green
arrows represent the forward pass, while the orange arrows denote the backward pass.
The procedure starts with a forward pass, where each stage is solved sequentially, and
state variable information, xn, is transferred from one stage to the next.

4.4 Optimization Literature Review

This section reviews relevant studies applying Rolling Horizon, Receding Horizon, and
the SDDP method. The aim is to understand how these methods are implemented
under various conditions and how they perform in practice. Their strengths and
limitations are analyzed to identify best practices that can inspire the selection of
methodology for this thesis. Particular attention is given to how these approaches adapt
to updated information and how they interact when combined.

4.4.1 Rolling- and Receding Horizon

Rolling Horizon
The work in [84] investigates how to optimally schedule energy and heat supply and
demand in a microgrid, aiming to minimize operational costs. Weather forecasts are
updated every 30 minutes, which is the length of the CH. A Rolling Horizon approach is
applied for different PH lengths, ranging from 2 to 24 hours. The SH is 24 hours. Using
longer PHs leads to more accurate solutions, as more information is taken into account
and updated regularly. However, this also increases computational demands. Only a
24-hour SH is considered, reducing the effect of analyzing the long-term implications of
the operation.

The management of a micro-grid on a university campus in Malta, aiming to minimize
the operational cost, is explored in [85]. A Rolling Horizon approach is implemented,
with the CH of one hour and the SH of one day. By testing different lengths of the PH,
11 hours is found to be the minimum length that obtains the same results as in a
deterministic case. The results show high optimality for the stochastic case, achieving
net cost savings for the micro-grid.

In [86], an investigation of how a household with a PV, BESS, flexible domestic
hot-water tank, and EV with vehicle-to-home functionality can be scheduled to
minimize operational costs, also including the grid tariff. An optimal operation schedule

                                                       32
is found by using a Rolling Horizon framework, with the SH equal to one year, the PH
one day and the CH one hour. When all flexible assets are active, a yearly cost
reduction of 7.93% is achieved. A different grid tariff model with capacity subscriptions
is used instead of the demand charge based upon the monthly peak, as the Rolling
Horizon method alone cannot account for end-of-month peaks and requires additional
methods to capture long-term operational consequences.

Receding Horizon
The study in [81] investigates how a BESS can reduce system costs in a commercial
building in Trondheim, using DSM strategies like peak clipping and load shifting. A
Receding Horizon approach is used, where the optimization is run over a month, with
an hourly resolution. The PH has 8 hours of certain data, and a scenario tree of three
scenarios representing the remainder of the month. Both peak clipping and load shifting
prove effective, lowering system costs by up to 17.8%. Incorporating a BESS on the
system level is more efficient than on the individual level. The continuous revealed
information of Receding Horizon allowed for better adaptability over time. Only three
scenarios are investigated due to limited computational capacity. Also, the Receding
Horizon does not model the revealing of spot price information accurately.

Knudsen et al. [87] present a report from an experiment on a radiator in a highly
insulated residential building, controlled by a Receding Horizon framework. The aim is
to use space heating for short-term energy storage as a measure. The optimization
model has access to a heating meter, two temperature sensors and the weather forecast.
The CH is half an hour long, updating the temperatures and forecasts. The experiment
runs over 14 days. The results indicate that substantial DR potential can be achieved
by utilizing a Receding Horizon framework to control the radiator and exploit the
thermal mass in a residential building.

4.4.2 Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming

Hafiz et al. [88] investigate using SDDP to minimize electricity costs for a community of
five households by optimally scheduling and sizing a battery and PV system. The
uncertainties lie in solar generation and electricity demand. The nine state variables
indicate that SDDP is a viable method to avoid the curse of dimensionality seen in
other optimization methods [83]. The results indicate that the SDDP method
coordinates storage and generation under uncertainty, with peak energy savings of 17%.
The method is applied without the update of new information, which is not favorable as
the weather forecast changes over time. Implementing this could make the solution
more accurate.

In [89], SDDP is applied to medium-term hydropower modeling for an investment
analysis for a Norwegian power producer. The SDDP algorithm creates weekly

                                                       33
expected-cost-to-go functions over a horizon of 104-208 weeks, considering inflow and
power price as uncertain parameters. This medium-term model provides cuts to a
deterministic short-term model, thereby enhancing its detail and accuracy. The results
indicate that the simplifications made in the medium-term modeling lead to a higher
net income compared to the short-term model. The authors emphasize the trade-off
between improved accuracy due to finer time resolution and the associated increase in
computational demand.

Helseth et al. [90] study how physical and legislative environmental constraints can be
respected while maximizing profits for a hydro power producer. SDDP is used to avoid
the “curse of dimensionality”, as the runtime is three years with a time step of one week.
Using the approach, the constraints introduced non-convexity in the formulation, and
challenge accurate modeling of extremely high and low inflow values. This is a known
problem of SDDP. Relaxation of constraints showed feasible results without losing the
performance of the SDDP algorithm.

4.4.3 Combined Rolling- Receding Horizon with SDDP

A study addresses the optimal scheduling of a hydro-thermal power system, by
combining various combinations of SDDP and the Rolling Horizon approach [91]. The
uncertainties lie in variable power demand and river flows. Using SDDP alone is
computationally intensive and only captures the most likely optimal path, missing
broader possibilities. Combining SDDP with a Rolling Horizon reduced computation
time and improved adaptability, resulting in more stable and realistic planning
outcomes. The study warns that overly strict constraints in the Rolling Horizon can
lead to infeasible solutions.

A study examines the cost and benefits of an EV parking lot participating in an
Incentive-Based DR program [77]. The method implemented a SDDP method to
provide future-cost curves to a combined Rolling and Receding Horizon framework. The
cost curves aim at reducing the demand charge of an EV parking lot, and the RRH
framework manages the short-term operation. The results show that SDDP performs
well in reducing the monthly peak and scheduling flexibility regarding the spot price
variations. Also, the combined RRH framework handles the notice time for the
Incentive-Based DR signal well, as long as the notice time is sufficient. Given that the
system only contained one state variable, the authors indicate that other methods can
be better suited.

                                                       34
4.4.4 Summary

Updating input data through Rolling and Receding Horizon frameworks adds
adaptability to short-term scheduling, allowing the system to respond dynamically to
new information when it becomes available. However, long planning horizons are
computationally demanding and depend on possessing all the future information
needed, limiting their practicality. For this thesis, a shorter, dynamically updated
planning horizon is more realistic and effective. While Rolling and Receding methods
performs well in short-term adaptability, they do not account for long-term uncertainty.
SDDP offers a valuable complement, especially in systems involving multiple state
variables. Combining SDDP with Rolling and Receding Horizon approaches enhances
the overall performance by integrating the ability to convey long-term operational
consequences with the agile responsiveness in short-term operation.

Method

This chapter presents the structure, algorithms, and mathematical framework of the
optimization model. The aim is to explain how the EC is modeled and justify the choice
of method. The optimization model aims to represent real-world behavior through
simple, linear, accurate expressions, resulting in more credible results. The EC is
modeled as an aggregated neighborhood, where the collective electricity cost is reduced
through optimally scheduling the BESS and EV charging. To simulate the operation of
the EC over time, SDDP is used in combination with a RRH framework.

The model design and other important features of the EC are described in Sec. 5.1. The
algorithm for solving the optimization problem is presented in Sec. 5.2, before the
mathematical framework and relevant assumptions are presented in Sec. 5.3 and Sec. 5.4
respectively. Lastly, Sec. 5.5 states the code implementation of the optimization model.

The mathematical model of the EC builds upon the work done in [8]. The model is
extended to include the RRH framework, the SDDP algorithm, BESS extension model
and temporal redistribution functionality. Parts of Sec. 5.3, 5.4 and 5.5 originate from
the project thesis in [8], and have been revised to align with the objectives of this
Master’s thesis.

5.1 Model Description

The goal of the optimization model is to reduce the operational costs of an EC
concerning the energy supply over a month, by following Price-Based DR signals. The
costs considered here are the varying spot price and the grid tariff. Thus, optimal
scheduling of EV and BESS charging is necessary to shift high loads to hours with low
pricing, while still keeping the demand charge as low as possible. Additionally, the EC

                                                       39
receives a signal from their local DSO with a request to lower the grid import for one
hour. This is to simulate participation in an Incentive-Based DR program. The EC is
also equipped with features to enable temporal redistribution of load in an
Incentive-Based DR event.

5.1.1 Model

An illustration of the structure of the modeled EC is shown in Fig. 5.1.1. It consists of
several aggregated households with a shared BESS connected on a common metering
point to the grid and EV charging point. The arrows indicate the possible directions of
power flow, which are all time-dependent. The EC depicted is representative of a
typical ECs presented in the literature [3], [24], [48].

       Figure 5.1.1: Graphical representation of model with possible power flows.
Normally, the EC imports electricity from the grid freely, indicated in the illustration
by the variable yimp. From the metering point, power flow is directed either to the
households or the EVs charging point, illustrated by yhouse and yEV respectively. The
EC’s collective cost of operation is based on the import of energy [kWh] and power
[kWh/h] in this metering point. This is done for modeling purposes and is not possible
in real life as sharing a common meter is not allowed by the regulatory authorities [92].
The power flow to the households covers both the household demand and the potential
charging of the BESS, bcha. The household demand itself is considered inflexible.
However, the BESS may cover the household demand partly by discharging, indicated
by bdis, thus making the household demand flexible in practice. A BESS solution to
cover the entire households’ demand requires large capital investments and is not
cost-efficient as the system would be over-dimensioned for large parts of the time [48].
The EV demand is modeled to be flexible with shiftable charging, allowing for
additional or postponed charging. The BESS is not modeled to cover EV charging.

                                                       40
Since EV charging is already flexible, using the BESS for EV charging would overlap
their roles and reduce clarity. The separation makes it easier to track power flows in the
EC and evaluate each resource’s contribution.

5.1.2 Incentive-Based DR Signal

An Incentive-Based DR program may aim to reduce grid import during a specific hour,
henceforth referred to as the “restricted hour”. It is assumed that the EC participates
voluntarily in such a program and must therefore be prepared to respond to reduction
requests. A request signal, referred to as an Incentive-Based DR signal, is issued by the
DSO or another enabling entity. The signal is received a certain time before the
restricted hour. For example, notice times of 12 hours, 2 hours, 15 minutes, and 0
minutes are used in Glitre Nett’s flexibility scheme [66] and in the work of [77].
Before the restricted hour, calculations on the expected change in cost caused by
limiting the grid import are performed. This is achieved by calculating the total cost
change when restricting the grid import in decremental steps of 1 kWh/h, from the
baseline consumption to the minimum possible metered load before Energy Not
Supplied (ENS). In this context, ENS refers to situations in which energy demand
within the EC cannot be met, resulting in discomfort due to forced reductions in energy
use. The results can be presented as a marginal cost curve. A generic marginal cost
curve can be seen in Fig. 5.1.2.

                         Figure 5.1.2: A simplified marginal cost curve.

Negative values occur when a more optimal solution is found compared to the original
plan. Zero values represent changes in import that do not change the total cost. This
may, for instance, be the utilization of hours with identical spot price. Clvoowl represents

moving import to an hour with slightly higher volumetric cost, while Chvioglh refers to an

certain threshold, the demand cannot be met and ENS occurs. This is penalized by a
very high cost, represented by CENS in the illustration.

5.1.3 Temporal Redistribution

During the restricted hour, the intended energy consumption for the EC must be shifted
to other hours. Traditionally, this could result in shifting load to less cost-effective
hours, in addition to risking shifting load to hours where consumption is already high,
causing an increased demand charge. This results in a higher total cost of the EC.
Additionally, this load shifting does not allow the DSO to foresee the possible grid
issues caused by rebound effects. However, by shifting the energy to predefined periods,
referred to as “blocks”, the DSO can assess the implication of choosing one block over
another, act proactively to mitigate rebound effects that may stress the grid and
evaluate the resulting cost changes.

A marginal cost curve, as in Fig. 5.1.2, can be calculated for each block. This serves as
a means of economic comparison between them. These marginal cost curves for each
block may be sent to the DSO, which then determines which of these blocks to activate
and how much FP to shift. It is ideal to move the FP to a block that results in the
lowest cost for the DSO, one that is favorable from a grid-operational perspective or by
any other factors that the DSO finds beneficial. In this way, the DSO is an active
participant in flexible grid operation.

5.2 Algorithm

To schedule the operation of the EC, an optimization model is developed. As a
consequence of the system being exposed to receiving new information during operation,
such as an Incentive-Based DR signal, the model must support dynamic updates. To
handle this, a combined RRH framework is implemented. However, since the demand
charge is calculated based on the monthly peak, and the model only optimizes over a
shorter period at a time, a mechanism is needed to account for the long-term effects of
current decisions. This thesis uses the SDDP algorithm to generate expected-cost-to-go
functions that capture and communicate the long-term consequences of operation.

5.2.1 Generating the Cuts Using SDDP

The main function of the SDDP method in this optimization model is to generate
expected-cost-to-go functions that capture the long-term consequences of the three state
variables’ values. The first state variable in the optimization formulation is the monthly
peak grid import, which determines the demand charge for the EC. The second and
third are the SoC of the shared BESS and flexible EV charging, respectively. To fully
utilize the battery, it is permitted to discharge additional energy or store extra charge
into the following day. Optimizing across three state variables over a month can quickly
become computationally intensive if not considered properly when selecting the method.
Therefore, the SDDP methods are selected in this thesis to avoid the “curse of
dimensionality” as a consequence of having three state variables. A major advantage of
SDDP is its use of dual values to focus on the most relevant regions of the state space,
thereby reducing the overall computational load. A drawback is finding the optimal
operation strategy for the EC, as it does not account for the activation of an
Incentive-Based DR signal.

                                                       43
Applying the SDDP algorithm to the optimization model of the EC means that stages
represent days. Each day of the month is optimized with forwards and backwards
passes to generate the Benders cuts, and thereby a specific expected-cost-to-go function
for each day of the month. The activation of a signal may cause the peak import to
increase beyond the optimal value, or alter the BESS’ and EV’ charging from their
original plan. This reduces the accuracy of the expected-cost-to-go function, which
must be considered when interpreting the results of the model. In this optimization
model, stochasticity is not included. This can easily be included in future versions. The
modeling of the uncertain values is outside the scope of this thesis, as this focus is on
operational feasibility.

5.2.2 Rolling Receding Horizon

A combined method of the Rolling and Receding horizon methods is utilized in this
thesis. This will be referred to as a RRH framework, and it is visualized in Fig. 5.2.1.
The RRH framework provides a more realistic representation of how information is
revealed to the EC. This is combined with the expected-cost-to-go functions created by
the SDDP algorithm to reflect the long-term consequences of operation

Figure 5.2.1: The combined Rolling and Receding Horizon approach with expected-
cost-to-go functions provided by the SDDP algorithm.

                                                       44
In this thesis, the simulation runs over a month, as this is the typical time frame of the
energy bill. Consequently, this will be the length of the scheduling horizon. Since spot
prices are provided at an hourly resolution, this interval is used as the length of the
control horizon. The prediction horizon varies dynamically to capture the gradual
revelation of spot price information. Initially, input data for the first 24 hours is
available, and the initial planning horizon is thereby set to 24 hours. As the model only
has information about the first day, the expected-cost-to-go function for the remaining
days captures the long-term consequences, such as how the peak grid import influences
the demand charge. This is indicated by the blue graph in Fig. 5.2.1. This function is
obtained from running the SDDP algorithm in advance.

The model then runs iteratively, optimizing one hour at a time while the planning
horizon recedes. However, at 13:00, the next day’s spot prices are published. This
reveals new information that expands the planning horizon until the end of the next
day, and so on. This process mimics how real-time pricing information becomes
available during operation. When the planning horizon expands to include the following
day, the expected-cost-to-go curve must be updated accordingly. The incorporation of
newly available information as input data allows for necessary adjustments to be made
[80], [81]. This process is repeated until the end of the scheduling horizon is reached and
all hours have been optimized. Note that when the RRH framework has reached the
final day of the month, the expected-cost-to-go function is replaced by the actual cost of
the demand charge.

5.2.3 Flow Chart

A flow chart of the algorithm is presented in Fig. 5.2.2. First, the SDDP algorithm is
run to obtain the Benders cuts needed to create the expected-cost-to-go functions
providing the long-term consequences of operation for each day in the model, except for
the last. When the Benders cuts are acquired, the first planning horizon of the RRH
framework is initialized and the iterations over the hours of the month begin. First,
there is a check for an Incentive-Based DR signal. If a signal is received, marginal cost
curves are created before a decision is made whether to activate the signal or not. If the
signal is activated, the input data is updated with the necessary information. This
includes the grid import reduction in the restricted hour, and also the desired temporal
redistribution strategy selected by the DSO. Regardless of whether an Incentive-Based
DR signal is received or activated, the current planning horizon is solved to obtain the
solution to the control horizon. The next step is to check if the end of the scheduling
horizon is met. If not, there is an additional check if the hour is 13:00. If this is true,
the spot prices for the following day are revealed, and the planning horizon is extended,
as shown in Fig. 5.2.1. Then, regardless of whether the planning horizon has been
extended or not, the prediction horizon is reduced by one step, the iteration moves one

                                                       45
hour forward, and there is a new control horizon to be optimized. This loop continues
until the final control horizon has been analyzed.

The overall goal is to study how an EC with flexible resources can participate in an
Incentive-Based DR program and avoid potential rebound effects, while reducing
operational costs. The fictional EC studied is located in Trondheim, Norway. This

6.1.6 Cost of Energy Not Supplied

The optimization model includes an unrealistic penalty cost of ENS of 1000
NOK/kWh/h, a symbolically high value to strongly penalize the solution of not
supplying enough power to the EC. A lower cost of ENS may risk the optimization
model opting for cost–effective solutions that tolerate ENS, which should be avoided to
ensure the comfort of the residents of the EC. When ENS occurs, it is a sign that the
flexible resources are unable to deliver the flexibility that is demanded of them or that
the system is under too strict limitations.

6.1.7 The Restricted Hour

There is one hour when the grid import to the EC may be restricted. This simulates the
participation in an Incentive-Based DR program. The hour in which grid import to the
EC is restricted is henceforth referred to as the “restricted hour”. The restricted hour is
set at 09:00, as this has historically been an hour of high grid loads [100], and therefore
considered a possible hour that requires aid to alleviate grid strain.

12 hours before the restricted hour, a notice signal is sent from the DSO to the EC,
alerting to restrict the grid import for the specified hour. The hour when the notice
signal is received is henceforth referred to as the “signaled hour”. The notice time of 12
hours is considered by the DSOs Glitre Nett [66] and Lede Nett [67] in their flexibility
schemes. This notice time is also advised in a study done in [77].

                      57
During the restricted hour, the grid import to the metering point of the EC is limited to
the minimum load before the occurrence of ENS to the EC. This occurs when the BESS
discharges 40 kWh/h and the EVs postpone charging. To increase flexibility during the
restriction hour, the BESS’s SoC limits are relaxed. Under normal conditions, it
operates within a 20–80% SoC range to mitigate battery degradation. Upon receiving a
signal, however, the operational range may be extended to 0–100%.

6.2 The Modeled Cases

This thesis considers four different cases. First, there is a preliminary case without
Incentive-Based DR-signal activation. The subsequent three cases include activation,
however, each case shows a different implementation of temporal redistribution,
becoming increasingly restrictive for each case.

In the preliminary case, Case 0, the operation of the EC with a combined SDDP and
RRH approach is presented. This shows the operation of the EC without the occurrence
of the restricted hour, thus only reacting to the prices and not signals from the DSO.
This operation is purely driven by Price-Based DR strategies.

The three further cases are different setups of the same model receiving the same
Incentive-Based DR signal with equal notice time of 12 hours. The common goal is to
examine how the various implementations of temporal redistribution affect the rebound
effects, operational costs, and general operation of the EC. To study this, the allowed
grid import to the EC is restricted for one hour at 09:00 on 6th of January.

In Case 1, the redistribution of the energy intended to be utilized during the hour of
restriction is unrestricted. This is similar to existing Incentive-Based DR programs.
Case 2 studies the EC when forced to either act proactive or reactive by setting the
temporal redistribution to either before or after the hour of restriction, respectively.
Lastly, Case 3 analyzes the temporal redistribution of the load to predefined blocks,
each consisting of three-hour periods. For all three operational cases, the impact of
considering the demand charge on the operation is also examined. This is achieved by
comparing scenarios where it is taken into account in the objective of the optimization
problem, Eq. 5.1, and not accounted for.

On January 7th (Fig. 7.1.1b, orange line), an increase of the peak power import from 60
kWh/h to 69 kWh/h has a marginal cost of zero. This indicates that grid import below
69 kWh/h does not affect the demand charge’s cost as the model expects the peak grid

kWh/h, the marginal cost rises rapidly, reflecting the increased cost of having a peak
import higher than 69 kWh/h. The actual cost of increasing the peak power import by
1 kWh/h is 36.48 NOK as this is the variable cost of the demand charge, as seen in
Eq. 6.1. A marginal cost of less than 36.48 NOK is achieved by utilizing the increased
peak to take advantage of spot price variations. Raising the peak earlier in the month
allows for more possibilities of profiting from lower spot prices, and thereby a lower
marginal cost. As the month progresses, the chances to take advantage of these price
differences decrease. Consequently, the marginal cost of increasing the peak import
typically rises from one day to the next. The marginal cost for January 31st (pink)
stabilizes at 36.00 NOK/kWh/h, indicating low economic gain from increasing the peak
power import on the last day of the month.

By January 12th (green), the marginal cost is no longer zero for imports between 60 and
69 kWh/h. This indicates that the peak is most likely set between the 7th and 12th of
January. In reality, the marginal cost should remain zero in this range, as the peak
import already is set, and it would not cost the EC anything extra to have a grid
import below this value. This deviance from the actual value is a consequence of using
the SDDP algorithm to create the expected-cost-to-go function. The SDDP algorithm
uses an iterative structure, where each day’s decision is based on the historical peak set
earlier in the month. Only the interesting values of ypeak are explored in detail, while
values outside of this range are represented by constant marginal costs. Consequently,
both higher and lower values are not tested, which results in the constant marginal cost
profile observed. This limitation in investigated peak values may be a drawback if the
true peak is shifted above or below this interval, as consequences will be captured with
less accuracy.

The desired choice of temporal redistribution depends not only on factors such as
whether or not the demand charge is considered and change in costs, but also on the
DSO’s perspective. When excluding the demand charge, shifting the load to After the
restricted hour is economically desirable. However, grid conditions and the possibility of
mitigating rebounds may be such that it is desirable from the DSO’s perspective to
shift the load to Before the restricted hour. This is suboptimal, as the feasible grid

the feasibility of participating in Incentive-Based DR programs and the choice of

Though the cost is higher compared to ignoring the demand charge, the possibility of
greater freedom of choice of temporal redistribution for the DSO may outweigh the
downside of an increase in cost.

The plot shows that reducing the grid import, and thereby increasing the peak import,
is more expensive towards the end of the month. This is because when a peak increase
occurs early in the month, the operation schedule is adjusted for the rest of the month
to take advantage of this new peak. If a peak increase occurs towards the end of the
month, there are fewer hours to take advantage of the possible lower volumetric

                                                       87
electricity cost. The large steps in the marginal cost occur at different grid import
reductions because the possible grid import reduction before the peak grid import is
increased varies from day to day. For instance, day 31 increases the peak grid import
much earlier than day 2. This is solely due to the variations in daily operation in
response to the price signals and consumption demand of that day. Note that the cost
of increasing the peak power is constant, as it is simply a function of the demand charge
in Eq. 6.1. It is the potential savings through the volumetric reductions that influence
the overall marginal cost curves. These results indicate that not only does the time of
day influence the cost of providing flexibility, but also the time of month.

The marginal cost curves all approach 14 NOK/kWh/h because it is the cost of
increasing the peak by 1 kWh/h, as seen in Eq. 6.1. Given that a block is three hours
long, this results in a cost increase of 12.16 NOK/kWh/h as the peak import is spread
out. Utilizing the extended SoC range of the battery comes at a penalty cost of 1
NOK/kWh, in addition to the spot price variations which will also affect the marginal
cost. This is a value that may only be expected for the final days of the month. The
reason for finding this value in the earlier days of the month may be a result of a
weakness due to using the SDDP algorithm to make the future predictions. The
algorithm of SDDP only looks at the interesting values of the state variables, as
visualized in Fig. 7.1.1b. As the grid import reduction increases, making the peak
import increasingly greater than the optimal value, the predictions of future
consequences become more and more inaccurate. The reason day 6 does not reach the
14 NOK/kWh/h plateau is that, on this day, grid import can be reduced significantly
before triggering an increase in peak grid import. Consequently, the peak is not raised
as early as on other days, where the system is forced to increase the peak sooner. Thus,
the predictions for day 6 are not pushed into the linear region of the graph (illustrated
in Fig. 7.1.1a), and the expected-cost-to-go function remains accurate in this case.

8.1 Case Discussion

This section looks at the broader picture across the cases, discussing how the demand
charge, rebound effects and total cost are affected by the choice of temporal
redistribution strategy.

                                                       92
8.1.1 Optimizing with Respect to the Demand Charge

Optimizing for the demand charge, rather than not, is the superior approach across all
cases for several reasons. First, it leads to a lower total cost in every case. When
studying the marginal cost plots, excluding the demand charge may be perceived as
cheaper in all cases, as the demand charge cost is not reflected in the curves. Including
the demand charge shows increased marginal costs across the cases, however, this also
reflects the total costs more accurately. As the EC is naturally exposed to the demand
charge, it gives a more comprehensive view of all elements affecting the total cost. For
example, in Case 0, the demand charge is reduced by more than half, from 5212.39
NOK to 2594.45 NOK, when including the demand charge in the optimization problem
formulation. Although there are economic losses in being unable to freely utilize the
spot price variations, the cost savings made by reducing the demand charge are greater,
leading to an overall decrease in total cost by 6.25%.

Second, when the demand charge is not considered, the flexible resources become too
bound to the optimal operational schedule, and they are less able to provide the full
range of flexibility needed to manage the restricted hour. This is evident in some of the
strategies in Case 2 and Case 3. For both cases, the degradation penalty of utilizing the
BESS outside of its optimal range can be seen as a consequence of an already tight
BESS schedule. In Tab. 7.6.2, the EC can offer more flexibility with its available
resources when including the demand charge in the optimization problem formulation.
The charging and discharging of the BESS is not as aggressive, and thus, by not fully
discharging every time, there is capacity left if additional flexibility is required. The use
of the extended BESS range is also not necessary. This battery-friendly operation
contributes to prolonging its lifetime.

Third, the rebound effects seen across all cases are reduced to a great extent when
considering the demand charge. This is especially evident from studying Case 1 in
Fig. 7.2.1 and Case 2 in Fig. 7.3.1. The demand charge encourages an evening out of
the additional grid import, thus a less volatile grid import is seen. When the demand
charge is not considered, the optimization model prefers to perform much of the
additional grid import during low-priced hours. Case 3 is an exception as the peak is
increased between 5.37% and 16.52 %, despite considering the demand charge. This is,
however, a consequence of the time limitations of the temporal redistribution strategy,
which will be discussed further in the following sections.

Optimizing for the demand charge introduces some modeling challenges. It requires the
model to have accurate predictions for the future spot price and consumption data to
avoid setting a too low, or too high, peak early in the month. It also makes the
optimization problem more complex, as the expected-cost-to-go function is essential to
provide the model information about the future outside of its prediction horizon scope.

                                                       93
8.1.2 Cost of Temporal Redistribution of the Load

Case 1 is the least restrictive temporal redistribution strategy, having the longest time
available for alterations in the operation schedule. It can load shift both before and
after the restricted hour. This simulates how Incentive-Based DR participation is today,
where there are normally no restrictions for the temporal redistribution. This case offers
no control over when the potential rebounds occur. As a result of having many hours to
shift the load to, there is a relatively low increase in the total cost. It is the only case to
avoid the BESS’ degradation penalties when excluding the demand charge. The long
period makes it easier to deliver the requested flexibility without having to use the
extended SoC range. Trends from the heat maps in Fig. 7.2.4 show that many days can
deliver over 40 kWh/h grid import reduction at a low cost (0.35 NOK/kWh/h at most,
and many below 0.15 NOK/kWh/h). This is due, again, to the long period during
which modifications can be made. As the costs are low for including the demand charge,
it implies that the model never has to increase the peak grid import with this strategy.

Case 2 still retains much of the freedom seen in Case 1, however, now the load shift can
be decided to occur either Before or After the restricted hour. This comes at a higher
cost but offers increased control of the occurrence of rebounds. The duration of the
period After varies with the number of hours after the restricted hours until the end of
the day. If the restricted hour is in the evening, the number of hours to make
adjustments is far more limited than Before. Thus, the timing of the restricted hour is
an important factor in evaluating the cases. This discrepancy in the duration makes
comparison between the strategies in Case 2 more difficult. The cost increase in Case 2
compared to Case 1 is due to limited time and availability of the flexible resources and
the need to extend the BESS’s SoC. Before is limited to 12 hours, and when excluding
the demand charge, the BESS is on a tight schedule. The extended BESS’ SoC range
must be used to deliver some flexibility, resulting in degradation costs. After can deliver
the maximum possible grid import reduction, and avoid the BESS penalty. This
temporal redistribution strategy can offer a larger grid import reduction than Before, as
the remainder of the day may be utilized. Optimizing for the demand charge in Case 2
sees a stable grid import and the flexible resources are more available. This leads to
both Before and After being capable of delivering the maximum possible grid import
reduction, while not having to use the extended BESS SoC range. The difference in
costs is significantly higher when excluding the demand charge. Trends from the heat
maps in Fig. 7.3.4 show Before having to use the BESS extension and not deliver the
full range of grid import reduction, while After offers the full capacity at a much lower
price. Including the demand charge in Fig. 7.3.5 makes the two alternatives more like.
Here, an increase in the demand charge is avoided.

Lastly, Case 3 represents the most restricted case and provides the most control over
the rebound effects. Including the demand charge increases the amount of flexibility,
and a large grid import reduction may be provided more frequently across the blocks.

                                                       94
This is evident when studying the heat maps in Fig. 7.4.4 and Fig. 7.4.5. The heat
maps of excluding the demand charge are unable to capture the cost change caused by
an increased peak grid import, as this is not captured in the model. Therefore, the heat
maps must be interpreted with this in mind. This case generally results in the highest
costs, which are highly affected by the demand charge. This can, for instance, be seen
when comparing block-by-block in tables Tab. 7.6.1 and Tab. 7.6.2. Each block has a
higher cost increase when including the demand charge, and there are also considerable
cost variations between the blocks. For example, the cost increase for a single activation
of Block A is over four times the cost increase of Block C in Tab. 7.6.2.

8.1.3 The DSO’s Perspective

Optimizing the EC’s operation for the demand charge, and implementing temporal
redistribution in response to Incentive-Based DR signals, simplifies the grid
management for the DSO. The DSO benefits from stable grid operation. This enhances
the grid efficiency and enables the integration of more users without compromising
system stability or exceeding the power capacity.

Another valuable aspect for the DSO is having insight into the potential timing of
rebound effects to support their operational planning. Throughout the cases, as the
duration of temporal redistribution is shortened, the timing of the rebound effects
becomes increasingly predictable. Case 1 and Case 2 exhibit similar rebound patterns.
However, Case 2 provides additional insight by indicating whether the rebound occurs
before or after the restricted hour. This information may be valuable for the DSO in
anticipating the consequences of sending an Incentive-Based DR signal. Although Case
3 results in higher rebounds compared to Case 1 and Case 2, the duration of these
effects is shorter, but predictable. The general trend across the cases is that a shorter
time window to make adjustments is more expensive, and the impact of the rebounds is
greater. Longer time windows can help minimize the increase in peak import and lower
the rebound effects, though the exact timing is uncertain. From the DSO’s perspective,
this introduces a trade-off between rebound predictability and grid impact. The greater
certainty in the timing of rebound effects is associated with increased intensity, while
lower predictability tends to produce milder impacts.

While this thesis focuses on a single EC, an extended thought can consider multiple
participants of the same Incentive-Based DR program. The DSO may wish to activate
different blocks for different participants. Thus, with a portfolio of ECs, they can
simultaneously activate the desired blocks. Thereby, the DSO has more control over the
grid load as a whole and may use this portfolio of activations to mitigate rebound
effects. Various DSM measures may be conducted by the DSO depending on their
specific need. The only information the DSO needs to know is the amount of offered
grid import reduction, the cost increase and the highest peaks for the period. This

                                                       95
reduces the necessary data collected and other information, which may make
calculations demanding.

8.2 General Discussion

Separate from the cases, a general discussion considers how some of the assumptions
affect the results, and other topics that are fit for discussion.

8.2.1 The Use of SDDP to Capture the Long-Term
          Consequences

One of the main advantages of using the SDDP algorithm to create the expected
cost-to-go function is its efficiency. This is a consequence of it only testing interesting
values of the state variables, thereby avoiding the “curse of dimensionality.” The
expected cost-to-go function obtained is highly accurate around the optimal solution.
However, this comes at the expense of reduced accuracy for values that fall outside this
range. This can be seen in Fig. 7.1.1a, where ranges outside the interesting values are
represented only by a straight line. If the state variables are pushed outside the optimal
range, for instance by the activation of an Incentive-Based DR signal, the consequences
would not be depicted as accurately as if the values stayed within the explored range.

In addition, the use of SDDP comes at the cost of not being able to include non-linear
equations and binary variables in the optimization model. This is because the SDDP
algorithm requires the optimization model to be linear. It may be noted that more
advanced modifications of the SDDP algorithms exist and that they allow for the
inclusion of integer variables [101]. However, they are not applied due to limitations in
the scope and time frame of the thesis. The inclusion of non-linear equations allows for
a piecewise linear model of battery degradation cost and stepwise demand charge,
resulting in a more realistic model.

Another method used to convey the long-term consequences of multi-stage dynamic
optimization problems is the Stochastic Dynamic Programming (SDP) algorithm. This
method resembles SDDP in that it splits the optimization problem into individual
stages and creates an expected-cost-to-go function. However, one of the main differences
is that it does not iterate through forward and backward passes. Instead, it starts at
the end and uses a discretization of the state space to test different values of the state
variables. The information obtained is then used to convey the consequences in the
previous stage, and so on. Consequently, the expected cost-to-go function is created for
the entire range of tested state variables. This approach to solving the multi-stage
optimization model allows for the use of non-linear equations. However, for this

                                                       96
optimization problem, SDP would have quickly become very large, as there are three
state variables and individual stages for each hour in the month. Additionally, the
number of discretized values must also be determined. This involves a trade-off between
accuracy and computational time. Furthermore, SDP is not as accurate near the
optimal solution as SDDP.

In this thesis, computational feasibility has been prioritized over modeling accuracy.
The linearized demand charge and the simplified battery degradation model have been
deemed sufficient for the analysis.

8.2.2 The Modeling of the Demand Charge

As a consequence of using the SDDP algorithm to model the operation of the EC, the
demand charge is linearized. However, if a more realistic model of the demand charge
should be included in the optimization problem, it should have been represented as a
stepwise function based on the price brackets introduced in Tab. 6.1.2. When the EC’s
monthly peak power import is 70.69 kWh/h, the linearized cost is 2594.45 NOK. Under
the stepwise model, this peak would fall within the 50–75 kWh/h bracket, resulting in a
cost of 2720 NOK, which is an error of 4.61%.

When linearized, it is no longer possible to take advantage of the upper bounds of each
price bracket. For instance, in Tab. 7.6.2, Case 1 and Case 2 show maximum peak
imports of 70.69 kWh/h and 71.64 kWh/h, respectively. If the stepwise model were
used, increasing the peak import to just below 75 kWh/h would not increase the
demand charge. This would allow more energy to be imported during hours with lower
volumetric prices, without raising the demand charge.

In Case 3, each block would fall into different price brackets depending on its peak
import. Block A, B, and D all exceed 75 kWh/h and fall within the 75–100 kWh/h
bracket, while Block C remains in the lower bracket. The cost difference between these
brackets is 988 NOK. Therefore, the stepwise function would significantly influence the
block selection if a more accurate cost of peak import was reflected in the marginal cost
curve, something the linearized model fails to capture.

Another model limitation is that only the single highest monthly peak is used to
determine the demand charge. However, as of May 2023, the actual demand charge is
based on the average of the three highest monthly peaks [97]. This change does not
affect the optimized cases, since when the model optimizes for the demand charge, it
tends to produce multiple hours with imports equal to the monthly peak. The only
results that may be affected are those obtained without optimizing for the demand
charge. In such cases, the peak is more likely to be a single event, and averaging the top
three values would reduce the demand charge. For example, in Case 0, the demand

                                                       97
charge reported in Tab. 7.6.1 is 2.38% higher than it would be if averaged over the three
highest peaks. Nevertheless, the symbolic interpretation of having such a high demand
charge remains valid despite this minor error.

Lastly, applying a common demand charge to all households in the EC is unrealistic, as
such costs are billed individually. Using an aggregated model already introduces a
deviation from reality. This is particularly evident when applying the stepwise demand
charge, which is intended for individual customers. In the aggregated model, the peak
power import tends to fall within the higher brackets of the demand charge, where step
sizes increase significantly. In contrast, individual households typically operate within
the lower brackets, where smaller step increments exist. This structure provides a
stronger incentive to reduce peak demand, as achieving a 5 kWh/h reduction, rather
than a 25 kWh/h reduction, is more achievable. This mismatch between the intended
use of the demand charge and its application in an aggregated model is another reason
why a linearized representation of the demand charge is considered a reasonable
assumption for this thesis.

8.2.3 Battery Degradation

When the demand charge is not factored into the optimization, the BESS and EVs may
charge rapidly within a single hour, even when subsequent hours have the same
volumetric cost. This behavior needlessly increases the demand charge and accelerates
the degradation of both the BESS and the EV batteries. Charging at lower power over
multiple hours is preferable, as it represents a healthier way of operating for flexible
resources [3]. This issue is mitigated when the optimization accounts for the demand
charge as the EC is incentivized to avoid large deviations in grid import across the
hours. In addition, the applied penalty of 1 NOK/kWh for utilizing the full capacity of
the BESS serves as a repercussion for increased battery degradation. However, this
penalty does not capture the effects of rapid charging within the 20–80% SoC range.
Including a comprehensive battery degradation model in the optimization could
incentivize more deliberate and conscious use of flexibility resources, thereby improving
their availability for participation in Incentive-Based DR programs. However, as
discussed previously, this comes at the cost of using SDDP to convey the long-term
consequences.

8.2.4 Household Consumption Data

The optimization model’s behavior would be the same regardless of the composition of
the household consumption data. The flexible resources primarily aim at limiting peak
import and utilizing spot-price variations. It is the behavior of the flexible resources in

                                                       98
response to different DR signals that is investigated in this thesis. This makes the
choice of exact sampled household consumption data less important.

When modeling an energy-aware EC like this, the consumption data can be
supplemented with smart controlled space heating and water heaters. In such cases,
enabling additional flexibility in an EC must be done with caution if the demand charge
is ignored. This is because the flexible resources react solely to spot price variations,
and the peak power import increases drastically. This further demonstrates the
importance of including the demand charge in the optimization model.

8.2.5 Feasibility of Smart EV Charging

In cases when it is optimal to shift EV charging from the daytime to the nighttime, the
results assume that the EVs are physically connected to a charger during those hours.
In reality, this is not always guaranteed, as the availability of plugged-in vehicles
depends on user behavior. Without a sufficient number of EVs plugged into the charger,
the planned load shift is impossible. In such cases, the charging must be carried out in
higher-cost hours, or consequently, increase peak power import. Also, the charging
periods are assumed to be full hours, while in practice, the times at which customers
connect their vehicles are a fraction of an hour. By neglecting these real-world barriers,
the resulting schedule may overestimate the achievable performance of the system and
underestimate the operational costs, leading to a sub-optimal solution. An upside of
fewer connected EVs is that the rebounds are smaller, as the total impact of the
reactivation of charging is reduced. If the smart EV charging is extended to include
vehicle-to-grid, then the presence of EVs is vital to provide the grid service. Because of
these factors, these EC charging should be considered more conservative than what is
found in the results.

8.2.6 The Ramification of Price-Based DR on Incentive-Based
          DR

The problem of optimizing the EC to price signals alone is that it weakens the
performance in Incentive-Based DR programs. Similar results are also observed in [4].
This is seen in Tab. 7.6.1 in the column “Reduction” as there is less reduction in grid
import possible before the occurrence of ENS. Case 0 schedules solely for price signals.
In the subsequent cases, the additional flexibility requested by the Incentive-Based DR
signal may not be met to the full extent as the flexible resources are too bounded to the
economical optimal schedule. Alterations lead to a suboptimal solution, introducing
BESS degradation penalties. Optimizing for the demand charge loosens this tight

                                                       99
schedule, and there is more flexibility in reserve that may provide the flexibility
requested by the Incentive-Based DR signal.

From a total cost perspective, participating in Incentive-Based DR programs, as
opposed to optimizing solely for price signals, increases operational costs. This is
because it forces the EC to deviate from its economically optimal schedule. Without
proper remuneration, the motivation for additional participation in Incentive-Based DR
programs is low for the EC. The value for the DSO may be higher than the cost
increase in the EC. With appropriate compensation structures in place, Incentive-Based
DR program participation may economically benefit both parties.

8.2.7 The Value of Flexibility

As flexibility is a service, the question arises of how much remuneration should be given
for providing this flexibility. In Case 2 Before, when including the demand charge, the
40 kWh shifted during the restricted hour increases the total cost by 11.69 NOK, from
Tab. 7.6.2.

Valuing flexibility can be done similarly to how power producers make bids into the
day-ahead market, based on quantities and marginal production costs. Case 2 can be
further investigated when considering the demand charge and selecting Before as the
temporal redistribution strategy, and solving it as a market-clearing problem. If the
marginal cost curve is the only bid in the market, and the entire bid of 40 kWh is
accepted, the market-clearing price is 0.88 NOK/kWh. This cost reflects the highest
marginal cost, and this price can be seen when observing the marginal cost curve in
Fig. 7.3.3b. The profit is the total income minus the costs, and can be found to be 23.51
NOK, as shown in Eq. 8.1.

Profit = Revenue − Expenses                    (8.1)
        = (0.88 NOK/kWh · 40 kWh) − 11.69 NOK
        = 35.20 NOK − 11.69 NOK
        = 23.51 NOK

If the 23.51 NOK profit is split among the 25 households, it results in a seemingly low
remuneration of 0.94 NOK per household. The revenue, however, is over twice the cost
of providing the flexibility. Participation in Incentive-Based DR programs in this case
resulted in only a 0.03% total cost increase for the entire EC. Despite this, the potential
remuneration of two times the added cost still makes this a profitable arrangement. At
a minimum, the customer is compensated for the additional costs incurred, as suggested

                                                      100
in [3]. Despite this, it could be argued that the 0.94 NOK remuneration is too modest
to motivate end users to adjust their power consumption.
In this example, the EC is considered the only provider of flexibility. However, it is
likely that the EC is a part of an aggregated portfolio to provide a substantial grid
import reduction for the local DSO. If there are several bids, the accepted bid is
guaranteed to be at least the highest marginal cost of the EC or higher, making the
single bid situation a low estimation. From the DSO’s perspective, the remuneration to
the EC is cost-effective, as their savings in operation and maintenance are likely greater.
The Euroflex program, highlighted in Sec. 3.4, reports an average remuneration of 8.9
NOK/kWh [78], indicating that DSOs value end-user flexibility.
Analyzing the EC’s marginal cost increase for a period provides the DSO with insight
into its operational flexibility. A consistently low marginal cost suggests a high degree
of flexibility, indicating that rescheduling does not significantly impact costs. On the
other hand, a high cost increase is a result of inflexible operation. To optimize
cooperation with the EC, the DSO must consider three key sensitivities: the timing of
temporal redistribution, the amount of load shifted and the costs of this. By
understanding these factors, the DSO can make informed decisions to enhance
cooperation with the community, ensuring mutual benefits.

9 Chapter

Conclusion

This thesis investigates how flexible resources in an Energy Community (EC), with a
Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) and smart Electric Vehicle (EV) charging, can
participate in an Incentive-Based Demand Response (DR) program, while minimizing
rebound effects and operational costs. A linear optimization model of the EC is
developed using the Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming (SDDP) methodology
combined with a Rolling-Receding Horizon (RRH) approach. The RRH framework
simulates the daily revelation of spot prices, while the long-term consequences of
operation is captured by the SDDP algorithm. A temporal redistribution strategy,
providing controlled load shifting, aims to predict and mitigate the severity of rebound
effects when reactivating curtailed loads. The loads are curtailed upon request by the
Distribution System Operator (DSO), simulating an Incentive-Based DR event. Case 0
optimally schedules the EC’s flexible resources for price signals, thus, the possible
additional deliverable flexibility requested by the DSO is challenged. Three cases
impose increasingly stricter limitations on temporal redistribution.

Case 1 has unrestricted daily load shifting, enabling selection of the most cost-effective
hours. The case consistently delivers 40 kWh/h of flexibility with the lowest cost
increase of all the cases, with a marginal cost increase of 0.12 NOK per additional kWh
shifted. Case 2 restricts the temporal redistribution to either proactive or reactive
periods. This increases control of when the rebounds occur. As a consequence of having
fewer hours available for load shift, the marginal cost increases to just below 1
NOK/kWh/h before ENS as a consequence of BESS degradation penalties, and a total
cost increase additionally with 11.69 NOK is found. Case 3 has four three-hour blocks,
providing high rebound predictability but large and quick steps in the marginal costs up
to 8 NOK/kWh/h. The compressed load shift results in the highest rebound effects and
total costs, with a cost increase ranging from 62.53 NOK - 279.24 NOK. Overall,
increased rebound predictability comes at the expense of higher costs, though all remain
below a 0.79% increase when compared to Case 0.

                                                      102
Excluding the demand charge and optimizing solely for spot prices overly constrains the
flexible resources when an Incentive-Based DR signal requests additional flexibility.
Consequently, possible deliverable flexibility drops from 40 kWh/h in Case 1, to 25.9 -
27.6 kWh/h in Case 3 when shifting the load to before the restricted hour. This occurs
because the flexible resources are already optimized for the low-price hours before the
restriction, and additional flexibility is limited. In contrast, when including the demand
charge in the optimization formulation, all cases provide 40 kWh/h of load reduction at
a lower total cost. The BESS and EVs charge more slowly, reducing the rebound effects
and benefiting battery longevity, and always have some charge to provide additional
flexibility. This emphasizes that including the demand charge in the optimization
formulation is essential to mitigate rebound effects, provide more flexibility and reduce
operational costs. Altering the schedule is more expensive towards the end of the month
as there are fewer low price hour to take advantage of to mitigate the potential increase
in demand charge from a increase in peak grid import.

Temporal redistribution is flexible as it allows for the interplay between intensity,
timing and cost of rebound effects. These are valuable parameters for the DSO to
control when selecting the temporal redistribution strategy. Further grid-side analysis is
required to judge each option’s suitability. The low cost increase when participating in
Incentive-Based DR suggests that its true value lies in grid benefits. To motivate
participation, the EC’s remuneration must reflect the value it provides the DSO, in
addition to covering any additional costs.

9.1 Further Work

Throughout the project, several possibilities for future work are identified. The
assumptions described in Sec. 5.4 provide a basis for improving the optimization model.
Considering these, together with insights developed during this thesis, future
researchers may explore the following:

Modeling uncertainty into the SDDP algorithm is possible with a simple initialization in
the input data. Uncertainty provides a more realistic model of the forthcoming demand
and spot prices. In addition, the current model uses aggregated data for households and
EVs. A more detailed modeling could be achieved by using sampled data from
individual units.

It may be of value for the DSO to understand the long-term effect of multiple
Incentive-Based DR activations, and at various activation times. This way, it is possible
to understand the implications of recurring grid service, how frequently the service is
needed and further set a value for the flexibility service provided by Incentive-Based DR.

                                                      103
As the sampled data from [96] contains data for longer periods. Expanding by studying
a summer month or an entire year provides a more holistic view of the effect of
temporal redistribution. The load curves in the summer months are different from those
in January. As PV production is the highest, a DR event of consuming additional power
to avoid over-voltages may be explored.
When modeling the period length of temporal redistribution, the time before and after
the restricted hour should have equal length to improve the comparison. Experimenting
with block lengths other than three hours or incorporating more blocks for increased
control and analysis is a possibility for expansion. As temporal redistribution is a new
concept, the duration and time of occurrence are suboptimal at this stage and are
subject to improvement.
A more accurate representation of the BESS and EVs would involve modeling
degradation effects. For reference, the degradation model in [3] may be adapted.
Including investment costs in this model would also allow a more complete analysis of
the economic trade-offs associated with long-term use of flexible resources.
Lastly, how the DSO values the ability to choose the temporal redistribution strategy
and its contribution to local grid stability is an important direction of study. Matching
the flexibility cost of the EC with the value for the DSO is fundamental for establishing
a fair remuneration scheme. Additionally, this opens up bidding flexibility in existing
energy markets.
