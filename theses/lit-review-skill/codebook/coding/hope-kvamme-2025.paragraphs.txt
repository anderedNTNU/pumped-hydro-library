### 3.1 EV Charging  (1 paragraphs)

[P04] section 3.1, pos 1/1
In recent years, the number of EVs in Norway has grown significantly [68], putting additional strain on the grid. At the same time, smart EV charging can support the grid and act as a flexible resource in DR programs. These perspectives provide a basis for studying the operational and economic impacts of controlled EV charging.

### 3.1.1 Impact of EV Charging on the Grid  (3 paragraphs)

[P05] section 3.1.1, pos 1/3
A study examines the power grid and the negative impacts of EV charging [2]. It also discusses measures to support EV integration. Solutions are proposed to reduce grid strain and losses, stating that "... EV deployment can be profitable for the operation of the power grid and EV owners" [2]. EV charging is proposed to contribute to load shifting in the grid, resulting in reduced power losses and operational costs. However, battery degradation, complex hardware requirements, and social barriers challenge their realization.

[P06] section 3.1.1, pos 2/3
The impact of EV charging on a Taiwanese distribution grid seen from the grid perspective is examined [69]. The study considers effects such as average losses and voltage drops. By using real-world measurement and survey data, low voltage is found when the EV penetration becomes high. However, the number of buses and EV chargers in the grid affects under-voltage and over-current events. Smart EV charging is suggested as a measure to mitigate the security problems.

[P07] section 3.1.1, pos 3/3
Geographically concentrated loads in the Great Britain power system are considered in [9]. Distribution networks with a high number of EV charging points may experience voltage and frequency issues. The article indicates that smart EV charging is vital to protect both the grid stability on transmission and distribution levels. However, optimizing EV charging for the distribution grid may have a destructive effect on the transmission grid and vice versa.

### 3.1.2 EVs and Flexibility  (3 paragraphs)

[P08] section 3.1.2, pos 1/3
A case study from Trondheim examines the FP of EV charging in apartment buildings [70]. The aim is to describe charging habits, analyze load profiles and quantify the FP. The study finds EV chargers, when connected to private charging points, are plugged in for twice as long as for shared charging points, leading to a larger FP. The study states that EV charging is the main source of flexible electricity use in apartment buildings.

[P09] section 3.1.2, pos 2/3
How EVs may be a source of flexibility for the DSOs is investigated in [71]. The aim is to describe the flexibility services that EVs may provide at the distribution level. The study states that it is possible for flexible loads to control the voltage through modulation of active and reactive power. It further suggests that flexible loads "... could be modulated as an effective way to mitigate congestion and overloading". Drawbacks include the need for algorithm implementation, coordination, new markets, and it also requires the DSO to take on a new role as flexibility enablers. Future work highlights the importance of calculating the value of flexibility.

[P10] section 3.1.2, pos 3/3
An empirical Norwegian study evaluates how end users can reduce charging costs through a digital smart EV charging platform [72]. When aiming to shift the charging time from peak to off-peak hours, users significantly reduce costs compared to conventional charging. From the grid's perspective, this load shifting contributes to peak clipping, reducing both power losses and congestion. The study highlights the benefits of coordinated users' charging behavior for lowering costs.

### 3.2 Battery Energy Storage Systems  (1 paragraphs)

[P11] section 3.2, pos 1/1
A BESS offers valuable flexibility to the grid through fast response times and the ability to store and discharge energy as needed. When properly operated, it can enhance grid stability and reduce electricity costs for the end users. However, the economic effectiveness depends on different market and tariff structures.

### 3.2.1 BESS and Flexibility  (3 paragraphs)

[P12] section 3.2.1, pos 1/3
The operation of a hypothetical local Finnish EC contributing capacity to the m-FRR market is investigated in [73]. The community's flexible resources, including a BESS and multiple EVs, are coordinated. The study aims to optimize the scheduling and operation of these resources through a two-stage model seeking to maximize community profit. The results show that providing m-FRR can be profitable. Future work suggests expanding the range of services offered and incorporating other flexibility resources.

[P13] section 3.2.1, pos 2/3
A study on the Norwegian FFR-market participation of a campus with a BESS and EVs capable of vehicle-to-grid functionality is investigated in [74]. The study aims to evaluate the economic and operational impact of different contracts, while also considering the suitability of the resources due to their rapid activation times. Results show a reduction in average total costs by 5.1-6.8% under the different contracts. However, the absence of an investment cost analysis makes the economic savings uncertain. Additionally, participation in the FFR-market has negative impacts on limiting peak loads.

[P14] section 3.2.1, pos 3/3
A study examines how a Tesla Powerwall BESS can be scheduled to mitigate rebound effects caused by electric water heaters in a 500 house residential neighborhood [56]. The study is unclear about whether the method used is deterministic or not. Additionally, as the analysis is for only one summer day, the credibility of the results is questioned. Nevertheless, the results show great potential that a BESS can mitigate rebound effects when the discharging occurs after a DR event.

### 3.2.2 BESS Operation and Sizing  (2 paragraphs)

[P15] section 3.2.2, pos 1/2
A Norwegian study examines the cost-effectiveness of an EC, and how a BESS may provide voltage support from the EC to the distribution grid [3]. The primary aim of the study is to coordinate the BESS to improve voltage at the distribution level. The study finds an optimal BESS modeling that results in fewer voltage spikes and no cases of overvoltage. Operating in a battery-degradation-friendly way increases the lifetime of the BESS, postponing battery replacements and the costs that follow because of this. Emphasis is put on optimizing for the demand charge, as this could lower peak consumption further and result in fewer grid issues.

[P16] section 3.2.2, pos 2/2
A Finnish study aims to profitably optimize the sizing of the BESS and PV system for an apartment building [48]. The study highlights that the correct sizing of the battery is heavily dependent on the type of DR, the design of the electricity tariff, and investment costs. The study emphasizes that sizing of the BESS must be tailored to each specific DSM measure.

### 3.3 Demand Response  (1 paragraphs)

[P17] section 3.3, pos 1/1
DR are signals that encourage end users to alter their consumption, either through price signals, Price-Based DR, or a grid operator or another enablers, request signal, Incentive-Based DR. This makes the end user an active part in grid support and allows them to reduce their electricity bill. In DR programs, the focus is on the end user perspective.

### 3.3.1 Price-Based Demand Response  (4 paragraphs)

[P18] section 3.3.1, pos 1/4
Price-Based DR encourages consumers to shift usage based on varying electricity prices and grid tariffs, enabling cost savings for end users and reducing grid stress for the DSO. This section focuses on time-of-use pricing and demand charges, examining how these signals influence operation and flexibility for end users.

[P19] section 3.3.1, pos 2/4
A paper investigates cost-minimization for a single South African household by the use of Price-Based DR measures [75]. The electricity price contains fixed costs and varying costs depending on time-of-use, similar to the current end user pricing in Norway. By implementing a home battery, monitoring the electricity price, and altering the usage with respect to it, the household can shift its consumption. This resulted in cost savings of 23-29%, along with a peak reduction.

[P20] section 3.3.1, pos 3/4
BESS, EV charging, and space heating in a Norwegian residential building are investigated for their influence on long-term operational costs in [76]. The flexible resources, electricity pricing, and future cost curves created are comparable to this thesis' model and that of an EC. When exposing Price-Based DR for both the volumetric energy costs as well as the demand charge, the total electricity cost decreases. Flexible EV charging is most influential on total costs, reducing the total electricity cost by 14.6% compared to passive charging.

[P21] section 3.3.1, pos 4/4
An EV parking lot with vehicle-to-grid capabilities is considered in [4], aiming to determine the optimal level of participation in the energy and reserve markets in addition to performing Price-Based DR. The latter is achieved by considering aspects such as the time-of-use, critical peak power periods, and real-time pricing. The specific time-of-use tariff presented is deemed the most profitable option, though it significantly limits its ability to participate in Incentive-Based DR programs. A combination of the DR methods could yield higher profits.

### 3.3.2 Incentive-Based Demand Response  (5 paragraphs)

[P22] section 3.3.2, pos 1/5
Incentive-Based DR enables grid operators, or other enablers, to request load reductions from end users. Sufficient notice time and remuneration are important factors for end users to deliver the requested flexibility and motivate participation. These implications are of interest in this thesis.

[P23] section 3.3.2, pos 2/5
The aforementioned study by [4] also investigated participating in an Incentive-Based DR program. Based on a price signal, the EV parking lot partly or fully curtailed their import for one hour. The study achieves its goal of profitable operation and cost reduction for its customers, but it does not mention how long in advance the signal arrives, which could significantly affect total costs.

[P24] section 3.3.2, pos 3/5
The optimization of an EV parking lot connected to an office building while also providing flexibility services through an Incentive-Based DR program is studied in [77]. Grid import is restricted for one hour with varying notice times, each linked to different levels of demand charge reduction. The study finds that only the 12-hour notice time is an economically viable outcome while still meeting charging demands. Sufficient notice time is crucial to shift the necessary load before activation, as flexibility is limited afterwards.

[P25] section 3.3.2, pos 4/5
The same study in Sec. 3.2.2 aims to provide voltage control for the DSO [3]. However, this voltage control comes with an increased cost for the consumers, the participants should at least be remunerated with e15 a year to compensate for the 0.12% increase in yearly electricity costs and battery degradation. This arrangement, where consumers offer voltage control services in return for remuneration for their expenses, is an example of a potential Incentive-Based DR program.

[P26] section 3.3.2, pos 5/5
Belgian DSOs purchasing flexibility from residential consumers with Photovoltaics (PV) installations is modeled in [23]. The aim is to postpone or limit the need for grid expansion investments. The model provides an indicative price per unit of flexibility and quantifies the benefits for the DSO. The flexibility is modeled as a percentage of the total consumption. Results show that the savings in expansion cost exceed the remuneration to the end users, resulting in an overall cost reduction. The price benefit for end users is approximately e10 per kWh.

### 3.4 Flexibility Programs and Markets  (4 paragraphs)

[P27] section 3.4, pos 1/4
Sec. 2.6 introduced emerging flexibility products and markets aimed at increasing energy efficiency and grid support. This section presents a selection of existing programs and market implementations, providing context for evaluating how different DR signals impact flexibility and costs.

[P28] section 3.4, pos 2/4
Grid Rewards is a program launched by Tibber (a Norwegian power retailer) that targets private end users' EV charging habits [5]. By activating smart EV charging and participating in the program, participants may reduce their power bill. A limitation is that it solely targets private end users with EVs and not larger entities nor other home appliances. Additionally, whether the demand charge is considered or not depends on the user's local DSO.

[P29] section 3.4, pos 3/4
Euroflex is the next generation after the 4-year pilot project called Norflex [78]. Euroflex is a large-scale Norwegian demo project utilizing the Nodes-platform, working with end users, energy retailers, and DSOs to provide flexibility and finance the program. Participation volume is 1 kWh - 5 MWh, thus inviting numerous candidates. By targeting customers with smart houses, electric water heaters, or EV chargers, the participants are paid directly or receive a discount on their power bill.

[P30] section 3.4, pos 4/4
Glitre Nett [66] and Lede [67] are two Norwegian DSOs that offer participation in Incentive-Based DR programs. Each motivates participants to reduce peak power consumption using different incentives. For Glitre Nett, participants with yearly demands exceeding 100 MWh may receive a signal between 12 hours and 0 minutes before complete grid disconnection. Depending on the notice time, the demand charge reduces by 40% to 80% [66]. Lede offers a 30% discount on the demand charge for simply participating in the program. If the flexibility is activated and the customer is disconnected from the grid, the demand charge is reduced by 95% for the following 12 months [67]. Currently, there is no standardized approach to Incentive-Based DR among DSOs, as neither of them specifies the disconnection duration. Glitre Nett does not specify the flexibility service the end users provide, and Lede does not inform on the notice time.

Limitations include controlled load shifting to predefined time slots as a way to mitigate the rebound effect in an Incentive-Based DR program, and the operational and economic effects of this.

### 4.4 Optimization Literature Review  (1 paragraphs)

[P38] section 4.4, pos 1/1
This section reviews relevant studies applying Rolling Horizon, Receding Horizon, and the SDDP method. The aim is to understand how these methods are implemented under various conditions and how they perform in practice. Their strengths and limitations are analyzed to identify best practices that can inspire the selection of methodology for this thesis. Particular attention is given to how these approaches adapt to updated information and how they interact when combined.

### 4.4.1 Rollingand Receding Horizon  (5 paragraphs)

[P39] section 4.4.1, pos 1/5
Rolling Horizon The work in [84] investigates how to optimally schedule energy and heat supply and demand in a microgrid, aiming to minimize operational costs. Weather forecasts are updated every 30 minutes, which is the length of the CH. A Rolling Horizon approach is applied for different PH lengths, ranging from 2 to 24 hours. The SH is 24 hours. Using longer PHs leads to more accurate solutions, as more information is taken into account and updated regularly. However, this also increases computational demands. Only a 24-hour SH is considered, reducing the effect of analyzing the long-term implications of the operation.

[P40] section 4.4.1, pos 2/5
The management of a micro-grid on a university campus in Malta, aiming to minimize the operational cost, is explored in [85]. A Rolling Horizon approach is implemented, with the CH of one hour and the SH of one day. By testing different lengths of the PH, 11 hours is found to be the minimum length that obtains the same results as in a deterministic case. The results show high optimality for the stochastic case, achieving net cost savings for the micro-grid.

[P41] section 4.4.1, pos 3/5
In [86], an investigation of how a household with a PV, BESS, flexible domestic hot-water tank, and EV with vehicle-to-home functionality can be scheduled to minimize operational costs, also including the grid tariff. An optimal operation schedule is found by using a Rolling Horizon framework, with the SH equal to one year, the PH one day and the CH one hour. When all flexible assets are active, a yearly cost reduction of 7.93% is achieved. A different grid tariff model with capacity subscriptions is used instead of the demand charge based upon the monthly peak, as the Rolling Horizon method alone cannot account for end-of-month peaks and requires additional methods to capture long-term operational consequences.

[P42] section 4.4.1, pos 4/5
Receding Horizon The study in [81] investigates how a BESS can reduce system costs in a commercial building in Trondheim, using DSM strategies like peak clipping and load shifting. A Receding Horizon approach is used, where the optimization is run over a month, with an hourly resolution. The PH has 8 hours of certain data, and a scenario tree of three scenarios representing the remainder of the month. Both peak clipping and load shifting prove effective, lowering system costs by up to 17.8%. Incorporating a BESS on the system level is more efficient than on the individual level. The continuous revealed information of Receding Horizon allowed for better adaptability over time. Only three scenarios are investigated due to limited computational capacity. Also, the Receding Horizon does not model the revealing of spot price information accurately.

[P43] section 4.4.1, pos 5/5
Knudsen et al. [87] present a report from an experiment on a radiator in a highly insulated residential building, controlled by a Receding Horizon framework. The aim is to use space heating for short-term energy storage as a measure. The optimization model has access to a heating meter, two temperature sensors and the weather forecast. The CH is half an hour long, updating the temperatures and forecasts. The experiment runs over 14 days. The results indicate that substantial DR potential can be achieved by utilizing a Receding Horizon framework to control the radiator and exploit the thermal mass in a residential building.

### 4.4.2 Stochastic Dual Dynamic Programming  (3 paragraphs)

[P44] section 4.4.2, pos 1/3
Hafiz et al. [88] investigate using SDDP to minimize electricity costs for a community of five households by optimally scheduling and sizing a battery and PV system. The uncertainties lie in solar generation and electricity demand. The nine state variables indicate that SDDP is a viable method to avoid the curse of dimensionality seen in other optimization methods [83]. The results indicate that the SDDP method coordinates storage and generation under uncertainty, with peak energy savings of 17%. The method is applied without the update of new information, which is not favorable as the weather forecast changes over time. Implementing this could make the solution more accurate.

[P45] section 4.4.2, pos 2/3
In [89], SDDP is applied to medium-term hydropower modeling for an investment analysis for a Norwegian power producer. The SDDP algorithm creates weekly expected-cost-to-go functions over a horizon of 104-208 weeks, considering inflow and power price as uncertain parameters. This medium-term model provides cuts to a deterministic short-term model, thereby enhancing its detail and accuracy. The results indicate that the simplifications made in the medium-term modeling lead to a higher net income compared to the short-term model. The authors emphasize the trade-off between improved accuracy due to finer time resolution and the associated increase in computational demand.

[P46] section 4.4.2, pos 3/3
Helseth et al. [90] study how physical and legislative environmental constraints can be respected while maximizing profits for a hydro power producer. SDDP is used to avoid the "curse of dimensionality", as the runtime is three years with a time step of one week. Using the approach, the constraints introduced non-convexity in the formulation, and challenge accurate modeling of extremely high and low inflow values. This is a known problem of SDDP. Relaxation of constraints showed feasible results without losing the performance of the SDDP algorithm.

### 4.4.3 Combined Rolling- Receding Horizon with SDDP  (2 paragraphs)

[P47] section 4.4.3, pos 1/2
A study addresses the optimal scheduling of a hydro-thermal power system, by combining various combinations of SDDP and the Rolling Horizon approach [91]. The uncertainties lie in variable power demand and river flows. Using SDDP alone is computationally intensive and only captures the most likely optimal path, missing broader possibilities. Combining SDDP with a Rolling Horizon reduced computation time and improved adaptability, resulting in more stable and realistic planning outcomes. The study warns that overly strict constraints in the Rolling Horizon can lead to infeasible solutions.

[P48] section 4.4.3, pos 2/2
A study examines the cost and benefits of an EV parking lot participating in an Incentive-Based DR program [77]. The method implemented a SDDP method to provide future-cost curves to a combined Rolling and Receding Horizon framework. The cost curves aim at reducing the demand charge of an EV parking lot, and the RRH framework manages the short-term operation. The results show that SDDP performs well in reducing the monthly peak and scheduling flexibility regarding the spot price variations. Also, the combined RRH framework handles the notice time for the Incentive-Based DR signal well, as long as the notice time is sufficient. Given that the system only contained one state variable, the authors indicate that other methods can be better suited.

### 4.4.4 Summary  (2 paragraphs)

[P49] section 4.4.4, pos 1/2
Updating input data through Rolling and Receding Horizon frameworks adds adaptability to short-term scheduling, allowing the system to respond dynamically to new information when it becomes available. However, long planning horizons are computationally demanding and depend on possessing all the future information needed, limiting their practicality. For this thesis, a shorter, dynamically updated planning horizon is more realistic and effective. While Rolling and Receding methods performs well in short-term adaptability, they do not account for long-term uncertainty. SDDP offers a valuable complement, especially in systems involving multiple state variables. Combining SDDP with Rolling and Receding Horizon approaches enhances the overall performance by integrating the ability to convey long-term operational consequences with the agile responsiveness in short-term operation.
